# A Child's Ordinary Day — Low Tide, Lift Broken

Pip wakes before the others because the gulls do.

The building she lives in was grown, not stacked — mycelium block and old beams somebody left honest, so the north wall wears its moss the way a rock does, unashamed. Downstairs her mother is up, and her mother's wife is up, and there is porridge and there is the argument they have every morning about salt, which is not really an argument, which is a thing they do because they like the sound of each other. Pip eats standing at the window. The tide is going out. She can tell by the smell that comes up off the flats, green and a little rotten, the good rot, the kind that means today the mud will be open and the little crabs will be there.

School is not a building you sit inside. It is a morning that moves. First the boards along the seawall, where Pip and a boy named Teo have the salmon count this week — their real job, not a pretend one. You lie on your stomach on the warm wood and look down through the glass blocks set in the sidewalk, down into the lit corridor under your own body where the young Chinook run silver, and you count what you can, and you carry the number to the clinic and chalk it on the slate. The number goes to the river's guardians, the two who speak for the Duwamish, one seated by the nation and one by the commons. Pip does not fully understand this. She understands that the fish are somebody, the way she is somebody, and that the count is a way of asking after them. Some mornings the number is low and nobody pretends it isn't.

Then Della takes them to the flats. Della is Duwamish and she is old and she teaches them the words — the word for the little crabs, the word for the eelgrass, the word you say to the water that means something close to *thank you* but heavier, with the whole of your people inside it. Pip loves the words. She wants all of them. And there are words Della will not give her, words that stay with Della's people, and when Pip asked why, Della said, *Because they are ours, sweetheart, and some things you love you don't get to keep,* and did not soften it into a lesson. Pip has decided this is the truest and saddest thing an adult has ever told her. She holds the words she is given carefully, the way you carry water in your hands.

Today, though, Wren is not at the rail.

Wren uses a chair, and to get down to the flats you take the little lift that runs on rails down the bank, and the lift has been dead four days. A part is coming — by tram to the ferry to a shop across the water, *Thursday, weather willing* — and it is not Thursday. So Wren is up top on the boards, and the crabs are down below, and between them is a bank Wren cannot cross.

Della stops the whole class on the ramp. She says, *We can go down and Wren can watch, or we can have the lesson up here where we can all be. It costs either way. You choose, and you choose out loud.*

It is not clean. Pip wants the mud. She has wanted the mud since the smell came up at breakfast, and she hears herself say so, and she sees Wren's face when she says it, and the wanting and the shame arrive in the same second and she will remember that second for a long time. In the end most of them come up top. Not all. A lesson happens on the boards with two buckets of flat-mud Teo hauls up the ramp, and it is not the flats, and everyone knows it is not the flats, and Wren is quiet in a way that is not forgiveness, only patience, which is a different and harder thing.

Pip sits by Wren's chair. She does not say sorry, because Della has taught her that sorry is sometimes a thing you say to make *yourself* feel better, and she is trying not to do that. She says, *The lift's still broke tomorrow, too.* Wren says, *Yeah.* They watch a crab work its claw at the edge of the bucket. *They built the whole city so nobody'd get stuck,* Wren says, not crying, just saying it, *and I get stuck all the time.* And Pip, who has been told her whole short life that this world was made so no one is stranded, feels the sentence land somewhere under her ribs and stay there, because it is true, and because the grown-ups who tell her the other thing are not lying either, and both of those are real at once and she is eight and nobody has shown her how to hold two true things yet.

She holds them anyway. She is learning.

In the afternoon there is play, real and loud, a game with no score because scores are the sort of thing this world starves on purpose. Pip's knee gets opened on a barnacle and the tender comes — not summoned, just near, the way the tenders are always near — and cleans it and presses cedar salve in and knows Pip's name and her mothers' names and asks after the one with the bad winter cough. Pip watches Wren roll home along the boards where the wood is smooth, the long way, the only way, and thinks: *tomorrow I will ask why the lift takes four days.* It is a child's question. It is also the only question that ever fixed anything.

## PROPOSED CANON

- **The land-and-language school.** Children learn in a moving, mixed-age, place-based day rather than a sealed classroom — the seawall, the flats, the clinic, the float-gardens are the rooms. **Coast Salish nations lead the language and land teaching** (here, Duwamish knowledge-keepers teaching Lushootseed), and settler children learn as guests: they are given words and are plainly, unapologetically *not* given others, which stay with the nation. This is the ethics-of-borrowing knot rendered at a child's scale — you can love a knowledge and still not be entitled to keep it. Grounded in the real Indigenous-language-nest lineage (Māori Kōhanga Reo, Hawaiian Pūnana Leo) and in living Lushootseed revitalization descended from **Vi Hilbert (taqʷšəblu, Upper Skagit)**, plus the Reggio Emilia and Nordic outdoor-school traditions.
- **Children do real, load-bearing, right-sized work as part of learning** — e.g. the weekly salmon count chalked to the clinic slate and passed to the river's guardians. It is genuine (the number matters, and is sometimes bad) and never extractive; the point is belonging and responsibility, not output. The honest tension: the line between meaningful child work and putting weight on children is one the world has to keep re-watching, not a solved thing.
- **Reachable-by-design is a promise mostly kept and sometimes in arrears.** The same slow supply chains that leave an eldercare lift dead six days (see `threads/one-ordinary-day.md`) leave a beach-access lift dead four, and a disabled child stranded above a lesson. Collective-access choices (change the plan so all can be present) are made *out loud* and *cost something*, and are not always chosen unanimously or cleanly. The world does not pretend access is finished; it keeps the grief of the days it fails.
