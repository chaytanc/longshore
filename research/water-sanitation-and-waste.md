# Water, Sanitation, and Waste — a research brief

*Domain: the unglamorous infrastructure a utopia hides. Sewage, greywater, composting toilets, urine, stormwater, sludge, discard. Who does this labor, whether it is honored, and the genuinely disgusting parts — the maintenance debt, the confined-space deaths, the failure that kills people in the gap. Anchored in Max Liboiron's discard studies (*Pollution Is Colonialism*), real ecological sanitation, and the Salish Sea's actual, still-overflowing sewers.*

The through-line, stated up front so the rest can complicate it: **a society that refuses extraction cannot have an "away."** The flush toilet is the perfect emblem of the extractive settlement — you press a lever and the problem becomes someone else's, somewhere else, usually downhill, usually poorer, usually on land that was taken. A post-extraction world has to close that loop: your shit becomes soil, your rainwater stays where it falls, your discard has an address and a person tending it. But closing the loop does not abolish the disgusting labor — it *relocates* it, and the deepest question in this brief is whether relocating it onto neighbors instead of onto a distant underclass is liberation or just a nicer-smelling version of the same trick. This brief tries not to flinch, because sanitation is exactly where a utopia's hidden costs go to hide.

This picks up an open thread that runs through `food-and-marine.md` (the sea is *nursed, not healed*), `death-grief-decay.md` (the body becomes soil; the whale fall as anti-liquidation), and `more-than-human.md` — and it sits directly on the Canon's line about the "lethal interregnum" when "frightened neighborhoods rationed by panic and people died in the gap." Sanitation failure is one of the most concrete ways people die in that gap.

## 1. The premise: flush-and-forget is a colonial technology, and there is no "away"

Start with the frame that governs the whole brief. **Max Liboiron** (Red River Métis / Michif; geographer, Memorial University of Newfoundland; founder of the **CLEAR** lab — Civic Laboratory for Environmental Action Research) argues in *Pollution Is Colonialism* (Duke University Press, 2021) that pollution is **not primarily a byproduct of capitalism or a failure of good management — it is an enactment of colonial land relations.** Every "sink," every "assimilative capacity," every place we send waste to be diluted and forgotten, presumes **access to land that can be used as a receptacle** — and that presumption of access *is* colonialism, whether the actor is an oil company or a well-meaning environmental scientist. Her sharper move: even benevolent solutions — recycling, "the commons," consumer responsibility, hydro dams sold as green — are often built on the same colonial assumption that Land is available to absorb the costs of somebody's good life. ([Duke UP](https://www.dukeupress.edu/pollution-is-colonialism); [CLEAR / maxliboiron.com](https://www.maxliboiron.com/); [Next Nature summary](https://nextnature.org/en/magazine/story/2021/max-liboiron))

CLEAR's own method models the alternative and is worth naming because it is *reachable* — it is a working lab, not a manifesto: **anticolonial science** means being *invited* by Indigenous communities rather than assuming access to their land or water for sampling; **community peer review**; and **judgmental (not random) sampling** — because "random" sampling itself assumes a right of access to everywhere. ([CLEAR lab summary](https://nextnature.org/en/magazine/story/2021/max-liboiron))

Apply this to the toilet. The modern sanitary flush was one of the great life-savers in history (see the steelman in §8 — do not skip it), *and* it is the machine that most perfectly hides its own "away." You defecate into drinking water, mix it with everyone else's and with the rain and with industrial effluent, and send it downhill to a place — historically a poor place, a Black place, an Indigenous place, a downstream place — to be someone else's problem. **The Salish Sea is downhill.** So the first design fact of this world is not a gadget; it is a refusal: *the loop closes here, on this block, and someone you can name tends it.*

## 2. Ecological sanitation: closing the loop, and the buildable tech that already exists

"Ecological sanitation" (**ecosan**) is the umbrella for treating human excreta as a **resource to be recovered**, not a waste to be dumped — closed-loop flows of nutrients from the body back to the soil. It is not speculative; it is a live field with commercial hardware, WHO-referenced treatment standards, and running municipal pilots. ([Ecological Sanitation: A Concept, CTC-N/GTZ PDF](https://www.ctc-n.org/sites/default/files/resources/4f535120-5c4c-4844-8f20-76020ae4f5bb.pdf); review: [Ecological Sanitation and nutrient recovery from human urine, *J. Cleaner Production*](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S246820391630173X))

The core insight is **source separation** — don't mix the streams, because once mixed they are far harder to recover:

- **Urine diversion.** Urine is ~1% of wastewater volume but carries the majority of the nutrients — **nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium make up as much as ~95% of the plant-available nutrients in human excreta**, and urine is nearly sterile leaving a healthy body. A **urine-diverting dry toilet (UDDT)** or a diverting flush fixture separates it at the source. Isolated urine can be processed into **struvite** (magnesium ammonium phosphate) — a slow-release crystalline fertilizer — or dehydrated/alkalized into a dry fertilizer. This directly attacks the **phosphorus problem**: mined phosphate rock is a finite, geopolitically concentrated resource, and we currently flush it into the sea where it *causes* the eutrophication that kills the water. Recovering it from urine turns a pollutant back into the exact fertilizer the food brief (`food-and-marine.md`) needs. ([review, ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S246820391630173X))
- **Composting / dehydrating toilets** handle the feces stream: microbial reduction with a carbon bulking agent (sawdust, wood chips, coco coir), time, and controlled aeration render it to a stable, pathogen-reduced humus. This rhymes exactly with the Canon's already-established human-composting practice (Recompose; see `death-grief-decay.md`): *the same city that composts its dead composts its shit, and knows the rhyme is not an accident.*
- **Greywater** (from sinks, showers, laundry) is **~70% of domestic wastewater volume and far less contaminated** than blackwater. **Constructed wetlands** — engineered reed beds — treat it biologically with near-zero energy: documented removal efficiencies **>84% COD/TSS and >95% turbidity/BOD**, producing water clean enough for non-potable reuse (irrigation, toilet flushing) while doubling as habitat and landscape. ([Constructed wetlands for greywater, review, ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2468584426000334); [natural greywater/blackwater on-site systems](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0925857412001176))
- **Blackwater** (the feces stream, if flushed) goes to compact evapotranspiration/biodigester systems that can also yield **biogas** for cooking or heat.

**Where the evidence ends and the imagination begins (flagged per Canon):** the individual components above are all real and deployed *today*. The leap the fiction takes is treating a whole car-free bioregional city as a **source-separated nutrient watershed** — urine banked block by block, struvite reactors at neighborhood scale, greywater wetlands threaded through the street grid where car lanes were. The seedling is real (UDDTs, struvite recovery at plants like Hamburg and in Swedish/Swiss pilots; constructed wetlands worldwide). The gap that remains is honest and large: **behavior, maintenance, and disgust do not scale as cleanly as chemistry** (see §8, §9). Naming that gap is the point.

## 3. Stormwater: the car-free city's secret advantage, and the sewer that still overflows

Sanitation's forgotten twin is **rain** — and this is where the Salish Sea's real infrastructure is most damning and most hopeful at once.

Much of Seattle runs on a **combined sewer**: one pipe carries both sewage and stormwater. In dry weather it all goes to treatment. In heavy rain — which is to say, routinely, this being Seattle — the pipes exceed capacity and the surplus discharges *untreated* into the nearest water as a **combined sewer overflow (CSO)**. The numbers are not historical; they are current. **King County discharges roughly 900 million gallons of untreated sewage a year via CSOs; the City of Seattle roughly 200 million gallons** more, into Puget Sound, Lake Washington, and the Duwamish. Both governments are under a **federal consent decree** to control ~95–99% of it — and in 2024–25 the County *renegotiated the deadline from 2030 out to 2037*, i.e., the fix keeps slipping. ([EPA settlement](https://www.epa.gov/enforcement/seattle-washington-and-king-county-washington-settlement); [King County CSO](https://kingcounty.gov/en/dept/dnrp/waste-services/wastewater-treatment/facilities/cso-treatment-facilities); [WA Ecology, 2025 penalties](https://ecology.wa.gov/about-us/who-we-are/news/2025/oct-1-seattle-and-king-county-penalized-for-sewer-overflow-violations)) The **West Point Treatment Plant** (opened 1966, beside Discovery Park, ~90 MGD dry-weather) is the region's big centralized lung — and it has *catastrophically flooded and failed* in storms, dumping raw sewage into the Sound. Centralization concentrates the failure.

Here the car-free city has a genuine, buildable advantage: **the impervious surface causing the overflow is mostly roads and parking.** Take the cars away and you take away the asphalt that sheds the rain that overwhelms the pipe. What replaces it is **green stormwater infrastructure (GSI)** — Seattle already builds it: **rain gardens, bioswales/bioretention, permeable paving, cisterns, urban forest**, capturing and infiltrating runoff *where it falls*. The **RainWise** rebate program (Seattle Public Utilities + King County) pays property owners in overflow basins to install rain gardens and cisterns; the **Natural Drainage System** program builds bioretention swales in Thornton, Longfellow, and Piper's Creek watersheds; the regional goal is branded "**700 Million Gallons**" kept out of the Sound. ([Seattle GSI](https://seattle.gov/utilities/your-services/sewer-and-drainage/for-our-waters/green-stormwater-infrastructure); [RainWise](https://seattlerainwise.org/solutions/); [700 Million Gallons](https://700milliongallons.org/)) This is the literal, real mechanism behind the Canon's "shoreline came up to meet the city — marsh, mussel-rope, floating garden where the parking was": a de-paved city is a city that *drinks its own rain* instead of flushing it, poisoned, into the salmon's water.

**The knot even here:** GSI is slow, expensive, and inequitably distributed — rebate programs favor homeowners who can front costs; renters and the poorest basins get retrofitted last. And it doesn't eliminate the sewage stream, only the stormwater that overwhelms it. The overflow is a *maintenance and capital* problem dressed as a technical one.

## 4. The discard that will not disappear: sludge, biosolids, and PFAS

Here is where "close the loop" collides with a hard truth Liboiron would insist on: **you cannot fully close a loop that industry keeps poisoning upstream.**

King County runs the state's **longest-running biosolids program (~50 years)**, refining treated sewage sludge into **"Loop,"** a Class-A fertilizer applied to Eastern Washington wheat farms and forests; farmers credit it for yields and soil retention, and a new Loop compost is being offered to community gardens. ([King County biosolids](https://kingcounty.gov/en/dept/dnrp/waste-services/wastewater-treatment/resource-recovery/biosolids/leading-with-science); [Seattle Times, Loop](https://www.seattletimes.com/seattle-news/climate-lab/king-county-refines-wastewater-into-fertilizer-for-wa-crops-forests/)) This *is* the closed loop working at municipal scale — the real proof the world's central conceit is buildable.

**And it carries the poison of the world it came from.** Biosolids concentrate whatever the city excretes, including **PFAS ("forever chemicals")**. King County's own year-long study (published 2025) found PFAS in its wastewater and biosolids at levels "low" and comparable to other non-industrial systems — *reassuring only relative to Maine*, where PFAS-laden sludge spread on farmland **contaminated soil, water, milk, and livestock so badly the state banned biosolids land-application outright.** Washington now requires PFAS testing of biosolids. ([King County PFAS](https://kingcounty.gov/en/legacy/services/environment/forever-chemicals); [Ballard Spahr, PFAS in biosolids / state actions](https://www.ballardspahr.com/insights/alerts-and-articles/2025/05/pfas-in-biosolids-states-keep-focus-as-epa-acts-on-other-fronts)) This is Liboiron's point made concrete and unavoidable: **there is no clean "away" for a body that has been eating a contaminated world.** A post-extraction city that stops *making* PFAS still inherits decades of it in its soil, its people, and therefore its shit. The loop you close is not clean; it is as clean as your supply chain was, and ours was filthy. Any honest fiction keeps a monitoring station and a person whose job is to catch the batch of compost that can't be trusted — and sometimes to catch it too late.

## 5. Who does this labor — and the trap inside "honoring" it

The assignment's hardest demand: *who does this labor and how it is (or isn't) honored.* The honest answer starts with the fact that **sanitation labor is where societies hide their cruelty**, and dressing it in dignity is not the same as ending the cruelty.

**Caste and the caste of it (non-Western, and non-negotiable to include).** In India, **manual scavenging** — cleaning dry latrines, open drains, and sewers by hand — is a caste-bound occupation forced overwhelmingly onto **Dalits** (one report: **77% of manual scavengers are Dalit**), the communities placed at the bottom of the caste hierarchy. It is lethal: workers descend into sewers and septic tanks with no protection and die of **hydrogen-sulfide asphyxiation**. **Bezwada Wilson**, born into a manual-scavenging family in Karnataka's Kolar Gold Fields, founded the **Safai Karmachari Andolan (SKA)** in 1993 and won the Ramon Magsaysay Award for three decades of fighting to abolish it; SKA documented **593 sewer/septic-tank deaths (2021–2026) against the government's admitted 332**, accusing the state of hiding 261 corpses. Wilson calls the practice "worse than slavery." ([Bezwada Wilson, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bezwada_Wilson); [IDR interview](https://idronline.org/idr-interviews-bezwada-wilson/); [The News Minute, 77% Dalit](https://www.thenewsminute.com/news/77-of-manual-scavengers-are-dalit-says-report-despite-union-govts-denial); [UCA News, court on deaths](https://www.ucanews.com/news/indian-states-face-courts-wrath-over-manual-scavenger-deaths/114525))

**It is not only an Indian story.** In the United States, **hydrogen sulfide is a leading cause of workplace gas-inhalation death** — heavier than air, it pools in manholes and paralyzes the sense of smell before it kills. In **Trinity County, Texas, August 2025**, three sewer workers died in a single manhole — one entered, two died trying to save him. The would-be rescuers dying too is a *signature* pattern of confined-space sanitation death. ([OSHA hydrogen sulfide](https://www.osha.gov/hydrogen-sulfide); [Confined Space blog, Aug 2025](https://jordanbarab.com/confinedspace/2025/08/29/three-more-killed-in-confined-space/)) The people who keep our "away" working die in it, quietly, and the news moves on.

**The trap in "honoring."** The comfortable move — the one this world is *tempted* by — is to take the humblest, foulest labor and drape it in reverence: the sacred maintainer, the honored composter, the dignity of tending shit. But the sharpest Dalit-rights position (the **Ambedkarite** critique, and Wilson's own) is that **"honoring" the labor is how you keep people trapped in it.** Gandhi romanticized the scavenger's work as spiritually ennobling; Ambedkar answered that the only justice is **abolition and mechanization — get human beings out of the shit entirely**, do not consecrate their presence in it. A utopia that says "we honor those who tend the sewers" may be building a prettier caste. This is the knot the assignment demands and the brief refuses to untie: **is a beloved, well-fed, socially-central sanitation worker liberation — or is it the same shit-work with better PR, still landing on whoever the world has decided is available to do it?**

## 6. Wildcard lens (rolled): disability justice & crip theory

*Entropy source, disclosed: the last hex digit of my session scratchpad UUID (`…3204` → session id `a872f444-…`, first-segment terminal digit **4**) → lens **4**, disability justice & crip theory. I'd have reached first for soil science (6) — the obvious lens for a sewage brief — which is exactly why the Protocol makes me take the one I wouldn't.*

Crip theory turns out to be the sharpest possible lens on sanitation, because **sanitation is fundamentally about the body that leaks, and every body eventually leaks.** Three mappings:

- **The myth of the independent body is the same myth as the independent house.** Disability justice (Mia Mingus's "access intimacy," the interdependence framing) insists that autonomy is a fiction — everyone is cared for by hidden others. The flush toilet sells the same fiction at civic scale: *my* waste is *my* private problem, dispatched invisibly, no one implicated. Ecological sanitation breaks that fiction on purpose — it makes the interdependence **visible and local**: your shit is now legibly connected to your neighbor's soil and your neighbor's labor. Crip theory says: good, that's not a bug, that's the truth the flush was built to hide. **Interdependence is not the failure state; it is the real state.**
- **Continence is a privilege the world is built around, and it excludes people.** The **Changing Places** movement documents that standard accessible toilets fail hundreds of thousands of people — those with profound disabilities, spinal injury, stroke, ostomies, who need a **height-adjustable adult changing bench and a ceiling hoist** — forcing carers to change adults on public bathroom *floors*. "No dignity on the floor." ([Changing Places](https://www.changing-places.org/); ["No Dignity on the Floor," PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10309150/)) Any utopian sanitation system built only around the continent, ambulatory, unassisted body **re-inscribes the exclusion in fresh eco-friendly concrete.** A composting toilet up three steps in a shared yard is a wall around disabled people. The design test: does the closed loop include the body that needs a hoist, a bench, a caregiver, a catheter, a stoma bag — and where does *that* stream go?
- **Maintenance is care work, and both are feminized, racialized, and invisibilized.** The Maintainers (Andrew Russell, Lee Vinsel, Jessica Meyerson; *The Innovation Delusion*, 2020) argue our whole culture worships **innovation** and despises **maintenance** — the unglamorous upkeep that actually keeps the world alive — and that this contempt is gendered and classed. ([The Innovation Delusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Innovation_Delusion); [IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/decoding-the-innovation-delusion-nurturing-the-maintenance-mindset)) Crip theory adds the punchline: the disability-justice tradition has *always* known that survival is maintenance — the daily, repetitive, unheroic labor of keeping a fragile body going — and has built an ethics around valuing exactly that. A post-extraction sanitation world is a **maintenance civilization**, and the question of whether it honors maintenance is the same question as whether it honors disabled life and the (mostly women's, mostly migrants') care labor that already runs the world.

## 7. Precedents: London's pipe (historical, Western) and Edo's night soil (non-Western) — and Haiti's bucket (Global South, now)

**Historical / Western — London's Great Stink (1858) and the case *for* the pipe.** In the hot summer of 1858 the Thames, by then an open sewer for two million people, stank so badly it drove Parliament from its chamber; cholera had already killed **6,000+ in 1832 and worse in 1849 and 1854**. Under miasma theory (wrong) and John Snow's water-borne theory (right, and unvindicated at his death), Parliament panicked and passed funding **in 18 days**, and **Sir Joseph Bazalgette** built the intercepting sewers, pumping stations, and embankments that serve London still and **collapsed the cholera death rate.** ([Great Stink, London Museum](https://www.londonmuseum.org.uk/collections/london-stories/great-stink-of-1858/); [Rapid Transition](https://rapidtransition.org/stories/londons-great-stink-of-1858-how-a-rapid-policy-response-stopped-the-stench/)) The lesson this world must *keep*, not discard: **centralized sanitary engineering is one of humanity's greatest life-saving achievements.** Any romance of the composting bucket that forgets Bazalgette is a romance that gets children killed. The Salish world does not un-invent the sewer; it de-centralizes *around* it, keeping the pipe as backbone and failsafe while pushing recovery to the source.

**Non-Western — Edo Japan's night-soil economy (a working closed loop, at a million people).** Pre-industrial **Edo (Tokyo), ~1 million people, had cleaner streets than contemporary London and essentially no sewage crisis — because human waste was a *bought commodity*.** Farmers, lacking livestock manure (Buddhism kept meat and thus herds marginal), *paid* for the city's excreta: an estimated **500,000 tons/year of fertilizer**, collected under formal contracts (**tsuke-tsubo**) paying households in rice, vegetables, or silver for the year's rights to their waste. Merchants held collection routes; **richer households' waste sold for more** (better diet, more nutrients). No one designed it as recycling — "it was the result of everyone pursuing profit," and it worked for centuries. ([Compost Magazine, Edo night soil](https://www.compostmagazine.com/night-soil-collecting-edo/); [Between Nature and Human, Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/handbook-of-environmental-history-in-japan/between-nature-and-human-history-of-the-use-of-night-soil-in-japan/CF189B539B14B880030FD8C29A7DC111)) The lesson: **a closed sanitation loop can be ordinary, unromantic, even market-mediated — and can outperform the flush.** The shadow, kept on the page: it still ran on a hierarchy of who collected and who profited, and it spread parasites (helminth/"nightsoil" infection) precisely because the loop was closed without pathogen treatment — the gap that ecosan's composting/dehydration steps exist to fill.

**Global South / now — SOIL's EkoLakay in Haiti (container-based sanitation that pays its workers).** In Cap-Haïtien, **SOIL** runs **EkoLakay**: households pay a small monthly fee for an in-home **container-based toilet**, weekly collection by SOIL's workers, and treatment into **WHO-standard agricultural compost.** From a 2013 pilot it now serves **~9,000+ residents and produces 80+ tons of compost a year (500+ tons cumulatively)**, rebuilding Haiti's eroded soils. ([SOIL / oursoil.org](https://www.oursoil.org/); [Urban Agenda Platform, EkoLakay](https://www.urbanagendaplatform.org/best-practice/soil-ekolakay-circular-economy-solution-urban-sanitation)) It proves the loop closes in a dense, low-resource, no-sewer city **without descending anyone into a manhole** — the collection is scheduled, above-ground, gloved, and waged. This is the operational template closest to what a Salish neighborhood system could look like — and its unglamorous truth is that *someone still carries the bucket every week.*

## 8. Steelmanned opponents (who disagree with each other)

Per the Protocol, at least two, at strength, ideally in conflict with *each other*.

**Opponent A — the sanitary-engineering universalist: "decentralized ecosan is a romantic step backward that will get poor people killed."** The mainstream public-health / WASH position holds that the centralized, piped, treated, chlorinated sewer is **among the greatest life-saving technologies ever built** (Bazalgette, §7; the collapse of cholera and typhoid) and that **ecological sanitation has a long record of pilot-scale success and at-scale failure** — dry and composting toilets that get abused, under-maintained, or culturally rejected become *disease vectors*, and urine-diversion programs founder on user behavior. On this view, the Global South's need is **more centralized treatment, not artisanal buckets**, and rich-world composting toilets are a boutique indulgence for people whose safety is already guaranteed by the very pipe they're pretending to transcend. The steelman: *your utopia is safe enough to play with shit because someone already built you the sewer; the poor cannot afford your experiment, and the graveyards of failed ecosan pilots are real.* (This is the Bazalgette case, weaponized against §2.)

**Opponent B — Liboiron and the abolitionist labor critic, who reject A *and* reject our comfort.** Liboiron would answer A that "just build more centralized treatment" is the colonial reflex itself — it still requires an "away," still presumes access to someone's downstream Land, still concentrates the harm on the communities beside the outfall and the plant. **But she and the Ambedkarite abolitionists also reject *our* feel-good version** — the honored neighborhood composter, the sacred maintainer (§5). Their sharper cut: **every sanitation system so far, centralized or ecological, has solved the disgust problem by finding a class of people it is acceptable to expose to shit** — Dalits in the sewer, migrants at the plant, women in the household, the downstream poor at the outfall. Bezwada Wilson's demand is not "honor the scavenger" but "**abolish and mechanize the scavenging**." So B attacks A (pipes are colonial and still need an underclass) *and* attacks us (your loving local loop still has to find someone to carry the bucket, and history says who that will be).

**Where they collide, and what survives.** A says: *centralize, professionalize, chlorinate — sentiment kills.* B says: *centralization is the colonial machine and professionalization just relocates the caste; the real fix is to remove the human from contact with waste altogether, by machine.* These are genuinely incompatible — one wants the big pipe, the other wants no one downstream of anyone. What survives the collision, and what the fiction should hold: **the buildable middle is a source-separated, largely decentralized loop with the centralized pipe kept as failsafe (answering A's safety point), running on maximally mechanized, above-ground, well-waged, rotated, and honestly un-romanticized labor (answering B's caste point) — and it still, on its worst days, fails and exposes someone, and the world must not pretend otherwise.**

## 9. Disconfirming evidence & failure modes

The case *against* everything above, at strength.

1. **Maintenance debt is where utopias actually die — and it's invisible until it kills.** The **Jackson, Mississippi** water/sewage collapse (2022) left **150,000+ people without safe water**; the treatment plant was over a century old, the system "just falling apart" after **decades of disinvestment and an eroded (white-flight-hollowed) tax base**, repair estimated at **$2 billion.** ([Yale E360](https://environment.yale.edu/news/article/jacksons-water-crisis-offers-lessons-cities-aging-infrastructure); [*Environmental Justice*, Jackson & environmental racism](https://liebertpub.com/doi/full/10.1089/env.2022.0117)) The lesson is brutal for a gift/commons economy with *thin, local, boring money* (Canon): **decentralized, un-glamorous infrastructure is exactly the kind of thing a community defers maintaining until it fails catastrophically.** A thousand neighborhood composting-and-wetland systems are a thousand things that can be quietly neglected until an outbreak. The Canon's "lethal interregnum" is a sanitation story.
2. **Ecosan fails at scale, repeatedly.** Urine diversion, composting toilets, and dry sanitation have a documented history of pilot success and full-scale abandonment — behavioral rejection, maintenance lapse, mismanaged compost becoming a pathogen source. Enthusiasm is not epidemiology.
3. **Closing the loop can concentrate poison, not nutrients (PFAS).** §4 — Maine had to **ban biosolids land-application** after forever-chemicals in sludge contaminated farms, water, and milk. A closed loop faithfully returns whatever the world put into the body, including what you can't yet test for. The loop is only as clean as the supply chain, and every real supply chain is dirty.
4. **"Honor the labor" is a documented trap.** Gandhi's ennobling of scavenging vs. Ambedkar's demand to abolish it (§5); the persistence of caste-bound sewer death (593 vs. 332) shows reverence and law can coexist with mass death. Making sanitation labor *beloved* is not the same as making it *safe* or *optional*, and can entrench it.
5. **Disgust doesn't yield to design.** People do not want their neighbor's shit near their food, their kids, or their nose, however sound the chemistry. Source separation, home collection, and composting all depend on a population's continuous consent to intimacy with waste — and disgust is ancient, protective, and not obviously wrong (it evolved to keep us from pathogens). A system that requires everyone to overcome it forever is fragile.
6. **The confined space still kills the rescuer.** Even mechanized, above-ground, well-run systems have manholes, digesters, wet wells — and hydrogen sulfide still pools there and still kills the person who climbs down, and the person who climbs down after them (§5). You can reduce the exposure; you cannot design a sewer with no low place for the gas to gather.
7. **Green stormwater is inequitably distributed and slow.** RainWise-style rebates favor property owners; the poorest basins are retrofitted last; the CSO deadline already slipped from 2030 to 2037 (§3). Good intentions plus capital scarcity plus deferral is the Jackson recipe in a nicer climate.

## Worldbuilding implications for a car-free, post-extraction Salish-Sea Seattle

Concrete, scene-ready, not sanded smooth.

1. **The block is the sewage-shed, and it has a name and a keeper.** Most neighborhoods run their own **source-separated loop**: urine banked and cropped to struvite in a small neighborhood reactor, feces composting in a shared facility on the old parking footprint, greywater running through **reed-bed wetlands** where the car lane was — a strip of rushes and red-winged blackbirds down the middle of the street, cleaning the shower water. The old **West Point** pipe still exists as the backbone and the failsafe, kept honest (Second Refusal: old infrastructure kept, not demolished-and-resold). The person who tends your block's loop is someone you can name, and they are not poor and not hidden.

2. **The rain is a resident, not a nuisance.** With the asphalt gone, the city drinks its own rain — rain gardens, bioswales, cisterns, de-paved creeks daylighted (Thornton, Piper's, Longfellow, real watersheds). "Where does the rain go?" has a walkable answer, the way "where did she go?" does for the composted dead. The **combined-sewer overflow** — 900 million real gallons a year into the Sound — is the *before-times* wound everyone's grandparents remember: the winters the Duwamish ran with sewage after every storm.

3. **The toilet includes the body that leaks.** Every shared sanitation facility is built to **Changing Places** standard — adult bench, hoist, room for a caregiver — because a loop that excludes the incontinent, the ostomate, the hoist-dependent, the catheterized is just fresh eco-concrete around the old exclusion. The disabled body is designed *in* from the start, not retrofitted after a complaint. (Ties to `health-and-care.md`.)

4. **Someone still carries the bucket, and the world watches how it treats them.** In the dense core, container-based collection on the **EkoLakay** model runs weekly — gloved, scheduled, above-ground, mechanized where possible, **waged well and rotated** so no caste forms. This is the Canon's bounded-inequality problem made visceral: the ratio cap (3:1–9:1) is *tested* precisely here, at the foulest job. If the sanitation worker is at the bottom of the 9:1, the world has failed its own test. The honest fiction shows a season where the rota breaks and the same three families keep ending up on collection, and someone has to name it.

5. **The manhole still kills, and the world knows the name of everyone it took.** A confined-space death — hydrogen sulfide in a wet well, and the second worker dead trying to pull out the first — is a live danger, not an abolished one. The names go on the wall (Second Refusal; the "wall of names" from `death-grief-decay.md`). The city argues, every accounting season, about how much mechanization it can afford to keep bodies out of the low places — and sometimes chooses wrong, and buries someone for it.

6. **The compost is monitored for the sins of the old world.** A neighborhood soil-lab tests each batch of humanure compost and biosolids for **PFAS and heavy metals** before it touches food ground — because the bodies of this generation still carry the forever-chemicals of the last. Sometimes a batch fails and has to be sequestered, an unglamorous heartbreak: the loop you built to be clean handing you back the poison you inherited. There is a person whose whole vocation is catching the bad batch, and a story in the one they caught too late.

7. **Urine is phosphorus is the harvest, and people feel the loop close.** The struvite from the neighborhood reactor goes to the P-Patch and the ocean farms (`food-and-marine.md`); someone in the world has noticed that the winter greens grow on last spring's piss, and finds it either holy or unbearable — the exact register of the Dark Garden and the whale fall (`death-grief-decay.md`). The city eats by the same loop it excretes; not everyone can stomach the rhyme.

8. **Sovereignty leads the water, not the pipe.** The **Duwamish** is a recognized legal person (Canon), and it was a **Superfund site** — PCBs, sewage, a century of industry. The nations of this water — the **Duwamish**, and the **Muckleshoot** and **Suquamish** whose treaty fishing rights are paramount here — lead the river's cleanup and set the terms under which any sanitation outfall may exist upstream of a fishery. The commons routes *around* that authority, not through it (per `indigenous-coast-salish.md` and the Canon's sovereignty clause). Sacred specifics are not on this page and belong in no scene; what's named is the standing, the treaty right, and the leadership.

9. **Maintenance is the honored civic religion, and it's still hard to staff.** This is a **maintenance civilization** (The Maintainers): the people who keep the wetlands from clogging and the reactors from fouling are as central as any artist, and the culture works at honoring them. *And* the Ambedkarite warning is a live thread — a character argues that "honoring" the shit-work is how you keep people in it, that the only justice is to mechanize the human out of it entirely, and the world has not resolved the argument. Reverence and a rota that quietly always lands on the same people can coexist. Keep the knot visible.

10. **Failure is a genuine, recurring event — not a solved problem.** A hard winter, a deferred repair, a neighborhood that let its wetland silt up: an outbreak, a beach closed to shellfish (ties to `food-and-marine.md`'s PSP/red-tide closures), a block's loop broken with sewage in the greywater. The **Jackson** lesson governs: decentralized, boring, un-sexy infrastructure is exactly what a thin-money commons defers until it fails. The world's answer is not that it never fails; it's that when it does, the failure is *legible and local and owned* — you know which block, which rota, which deferred repair — instead of a 900-million-gallon overflow no one person is accountable for.

## PROPOSED CANON

*World-level facts I'm proposing; a human merges these into `CANON.md`. Additions that complicate, never contradict.*

1. **There is no "away."** The Salish world's governing sanitation principle, drawn from Max Liboiron's *Pollution Is Colonialism*: waste has no elsewhere to go, so every stream — sewage, greywater, stormwater, discard — is closed into a loop that is **legible, local, and tended by a nameable person.** The colonial flush-and-forget is understood as a technology for hiding harm downhill onto poorer and Indigenous land, and is refused.

2. **Source-separated, decentralized sanitation with a centralized failsafe.** Most neighborhoods run their own loop — **urine diverted to struvite fertilizer, feces composted, greywater cleaned in constructed reed-bed wetlands** in the de-paved streets — while the old centralized pipe and treatment plant (West Point) are kept as backbone and emergency failsafe (Second Refusal: infrastructure kept honest, not demolished). Grounded in real ecosan, urine-diversion, struvite recovery, and constructed-wetland practice. The car-free city's freedom from road/parking asphalt is what makes green stormwater infrastructure sufficient to end the combined-sewer overflow.

3. **Sanitation labor is the acid test of the bounded-inequality cap.** The foulest, most dangerous work (collection, sewer/wet-well maintenance, compost handling) is **above-ground, mechanized where possible, well-waged, and rotated so no caste forms** — the EkoLakay/SOIL model, not the manual-scavenging model. Whether this labor sits near the *top* rather than the bottom of the Canon's 3:1–9:1 ratio is a live, re-argued civic question, and the world holds unresolved the split between *honoring* the labor (Gandhi) and *abolishing* it by machine (Ambedkar/Bezwada Wilson).

4. **The closed loop inherits the old world's poison.** Composted humanure and biosolids are **monitored for PFAS and heavy metals** before touching food ground, because this generation's bodies still carry the forever-chemicals of the extraction era (the Maine biosolids ban is the real cautionary case). Sometimes a batch fails; sanitation is *nursed, not perfected*, exactly as the sea is (`food-and-marine.md`). Sanitation failure — a broken loop, an overflow, a confined-space death — is a genuine, recurring event, legible and locally owned, and is one concrete form of the Canon's "lethal interregnum."
