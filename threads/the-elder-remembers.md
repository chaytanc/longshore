# The Elder Remembers

*A fiction thread for The Reality Next Door. Slug: `the-elder-remembers`. Read `CANON.md` and `AGENTS.md` first.*

---

You want to know if it was worth it. Everyone your age wants that, and you want the old woman to say yes so you can stop asking. Sit down. Drink the tea. I'll tell you what I remember, and you can decide worth for yourself, which is the only honest way anybody ever decides it.

I grew up on the Duwamish when the Duwamish was a poison. You've walked it clean — the marsh, the salmon they coaxed back, the glass in the sidewalk. When I was a girl it was a straightened ditch the color of an old bruise, and my father worked the container terminal at the mouth of it, and the diesel got into everything. Into the laundry. Into the tap water some years. Into him. He loved it. That's the first thing nobody tells you: the men who worked the extraction, most of them loved the work. It fed us. A crane operator was a king. My father could set a forty-ton box on a truck bed soft as you'd set down a sleeping child, and the whole yard knew it, and he was somebody. Then the turning came and made his hands worthless, and then it made them criminal, and he never once forgave it, or me.

Because I was on the crews. When they starved the cars out — closed the shortcuts, pulled the parking, let the roads rot until driving got slow and stupid — somebody had to do the tearing, and I was young and strong and I believed. I liked it, God help me. The smell of broken asphalt is like nothing else, tar and cold dirt underneath, and you'd get down through a hundred years of road in a weekend with crowbars and get to soil that had been holding its breath the whole time. We planted it the same day. I have never felt more useful.

It was not peaceful. Learn that from me if you learn nothing. There were men who fought us for those streets — men like my father, whose whole lives were the engine and the road — and some of them fought with more than words. A crew I wasn't on got beaten with their own bars in Georgetown. A woman I knew lost an eye. And we gave it back. We were not gentle either. We told ourselves we were saving the world and maybe we were and we still put a man in the hospital because he wouldn't let us close his alley, and I remember being glad. That gladness is the thing I could not compost when they finally taught us to compost everything else. It's still in me. It didn't cure.

Here is the ugliest one, so you have it and you don't get to pretend I was clean. In the lean years the medicine stopped coming. You're used to the committee now — the one whose names are on the wall, the one you can shout at. Before that there was just us, a neighborhood board of frightened people, and no rule, and too little of everything. We decided who got the last of the insulin off the last diesel runs while we were tearing up the roads those same runs came in on. We decided it the way frightened people always decide: we helped the ones who were like us. A boy died three streets over that a helicopter and an overnight package would have saved in the world I was busy killing. His mother knew I'd voted. She never spoke to me again, and she was right not to, and I would vote the same way tonight, and both of those are true and I have made my peace with neither.

So. What do I miss.

I miss my car. There. The one thing I'm not allowed to miss, and I miss it most. Not the speed — the *alone*. You have never once in your life been nobody to no one, and I had it, every night, a dark road and the radio and not a soul who knew my name or my business or whether I'd eaten. The care web is warm. It is also total. Everyone knows when I'm low now. Someone always comes. It is a beautiful thing and some nights it sits on my chest like a hand, and I would give a year of this soft kept life to drive one more hour through a city that did not love me.

I did not go to my father when he was dying. He was out past the walkshed by then, in Kent with the others we priced out, and the fast boat was right there, and I would not take it — I'd built my whole self on the slow crossing being the point, on speed being the old sin. So I waited on the Weave like a righteous fool, and his thread reached me heavy and late, and by the time I crossed he was three days into the composting-house and I never got the word he might have had for me. That was pride wearing the costume of principle. Don't do what I did. Take the fast boat. The one who is late for a real reason takes it and nobody's eyebrow goes up — that's the rule, and I wrote half of it, and I couldn't obey it for the one man it was for.

Was it worth it. The river runs clean. Children put their feet in it. No child breathes what I breathed. Yes. It was worth it.

And it cost exactly what I've told you, and it cost more that I've forgotten, and there is a mother three streets over who would spit on the word *worth*, and she gets a vote too, and hers weighs the same as mine.

Now go. You're keeping the tram, and the tram waits for no woman's grief.

## PROPOSED CANON

- **The turning was violent, and the violence ran both ways.** The car-starving and depaving (Pontevedra/depave model, per `research/car-free-urbanism.md`) were physically contested — beatings, injuries, retaliation — not a peaceful civic consensus. The working people whose livelihoods *were* the extraction economy (port labor, driving, engine trades) were not villains; many loved their work and were made first redundant, then criminal, by the transition. Their grief and grievance are legitimate and largely unaddressed.
- **There was a lethal interregnum before the accountable God Committee.** In the lean transition years, before rationing bodies had public/rotating/revocable membership (per `research/death-grief-decay.md` and `scarcity-and-hard-tradeoffs.md`), scarce medicine was rationed by ad-hoc frightened neighborhood boards that fell into exactly the 1962 God Committee's failure — helping those most like themselves. People died in the gap who the extractive-but-abundant before-times could have saved. This is a specific historical wound the world carries, not an abstraction.
- **Not everyone reconciled to the loss, and the world does not require them to.** Some elders honestly miss things they "should not" — the car above all, understood as *anonymity and unsurveilled solitude*, not speed. The total warmth of the care web has a shadow: it is also total legibility, and some people experience being always-known as a weight. This complicates, does not contradict, the Weave's "no one is stranded" — being un-strandable is also being un-hidden.
