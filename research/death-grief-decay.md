# Death, Grief, and Decay — a research brief

*Domain: how a good society dies well. Green/natural burial, the "whale-fall economy" (a windfall colonized in slow stages, not liquidated), grief as a communal craft, and the part with no clean answer — unequal lifespans and the rationing of genuinely scarce high-tech care.*

The through-line, stated up front so the rest can complicate it: **a society that refuses extraction has to build an honest relationship with death, because death is the one scarcity it cannot abolish.** Everything downstream of that — how you compost a body, how you mourn, how you decide who gets the one machine — is a test of whether the world's stated values survive contact with the thing that does not negotiate. This brief tries not to flinch. It ends somewhere uncomfortable on purpose.

A note on where this sits: the `health-and-care.md` brief closes with an open question (#2) that is essentially this assignment — "where is the *one big hospital*, and how does a car-free, gift-economy world ration a scarce transplant?" This picks that up and does not resolve it, because it doesn't resolve.

## 1. Dying well is a skill a culture can keep or lose — and the West lost it

For most of the twentieth century, dying in the rich world got quietly outsourced: to the hospital, the funeral home, the professional. The **death-positive movement** of the last fifteen years is the correction — an attempt to take the craft of dying and mourning back out of the industry and put it into ordinary hands.

- **Death cafés.** Started in London in 2011 (Jon Underwood, building on the Swiss sociologist Bernard Crettaz's *cafés mortels*); now thousands worldwide. Not therapy, not grief counselling — a room, tea and cake, and a facilitated conversation about death with no agenda and no product to sell. The point is de-professionalizing the *conversation*. ([Death Café movement overview, ECU Libraries](https://libguides.ecu.edu/EndofLife-Death-Doula-Resources))
- **Death doulas / end-of-life doulas.** Non-medical companions who sit with the dying and the bereaved — emotional, spiritual, practical, hands-on. The **National End-of-Life Doula Alliance (NELDA)**, the US trade body, reports its own membership climbing steeply since it formed in 2019 — a *self-reported association count*, not an independent census, so read it as a direction (the craft is growing fast) rather than a measured multiplier. They draw explicitly on Jewish, Muslim, Christian, Buddhist and Indigenous traditions, framing mourning as "a sacred act done in community." ([NELDA](https://www.nedalliance.org/); [The Revealer, "Death Doulas and the Gospel of Green Burials"](https://therevealer.org/death-doulas-and-the-gospel-of-green-burials/))
- **Home funerals.** The legal right, in most US states, to keep the body at home, wash and dress it yourself, sit with it, and not hand it to a funeral home at all. The **Funeral Consumers Alliance** exists because the industry obscured that right: "The funeral industry has inserted itself between families and their sacred traditions" (director Joshua Slocum). The average US funeral now runs **$7,000–$12,000** — a manufactured expense around a universal event. ([Funeral Consumers Alliance](https://www.funerals.org/get-help/green-options/))

**The historical precedent (Western, and worth reclaiming honestly): the *ars moriendi*.** In the 15th century, after the Black Death had killed a third of Europe and left too few priests to attend the dying, the Church published the *Ars Moriendi* — "The Art of Dying" — a block-printed lay manual on how to die well *without* a professional in the room: the temptations that come at the deathbed, the questions to ask, the consolations, the role of the people gathered around you. It was, in effect, a death-doula handbook for a world where the institution had failed and ordinary people had to hold each other's dying themselves. The death-positive movement is re-inventing it. A post-extraction world would simply *have* it — a living, secular-and-otherwise craft of the deathbed, taught the way you teach bread or navigation.

**Tension already:** the death-positive movement is disproportionately white, educated, and affluent — a boutique reclamation for people who had the luxury of forgetting death, sold back to them at doula rates. For communities that never got to outsource death (Black, Indigenous, immigrant, poor), "reclaiming" it can read as a class of people discovering, and monetizing, what others never lost. Keep that knot visible.

## 2. Decay: making the body's end an ecological gift, not a toxic problem

Conventional US death is an extraction event: **embalming** pumps formaldehyde into the ground; a **casket-and-vault** burial entombs steel, hardwood, and concrete; **cremation** burns fossil gas to hold 1,600°F for hours. The alternatives are real, legal, and — this is the Seattle part — pioneered here.

**Human composting (natural organic reduction).** Washington became the **first state in the world to legalize it**: Senate Bill 5001, signed by Gov. Jay Inslee May 21 2019, **effective May 7 2020.** The body is laid in a vessel with wood chips, alfalfa and straw; over ~5–7 weeks microbes and controlled aeration reduce it (bone included, after screening) to roughly a cubic yard of soil, which then cures. By Recompose's own reporting — a **company figure**, from a life-cycle assessment it commissioned rather than an independent audit — it uses far less energy than flame cremation (they put it at ~**87% less**) and creates soil instead of ash; take the exact percentage as self-reported and the direction (much lower energy, no combustion) as sound. The company is **Recompose**, founded 2017 by **Katrina Spade** (who developed the idea as an architecture master's student at UMass Amherst, then ran the science with Washington State University's soil scientists), headquartered in Seattle. It charges ~$7,000 and runs a subsidized **Community Fund** for those who can't pay. ([CPR News on SB 5001](https://www.cpr.org/2019/05/22/washington-is-1st-state-to-allow-human-body-composting/); [Recompose, legal status / WA](https://recompose.life/human-composting/legal-status/washington/); the statute itself, [WA SB 5001 (2019)](https://app.leg.wa.gov/billsummary?BillNumber=5001&Year=2019))

**Alkaline hydrolysis (aquamation / "water cremation").** SB 5001 also recognized this — dissolving the body in warm alkaline water, far less energy and no combustion emissions than flame cremation. ([Governing](https://www.governing.com/archive/tns-washington-state-human-composting.html))

**Green / natural / conservation burial.** A tiered real-world standard (Green Burial Council, Conservation Burial Alliance): no embalming, biodegradable shroud or casket, no vault; at the top tier, **conservation burial**, where the graves *fund and legally protect land in perpetuity* — the burial fees endow the restoration of the very habitat you're buried in. The dead literally pay to keep the forest a forest. ([Green Burial Council](https://www.greenburialcouncil.org/green-burial-defined/); [Conservation Burial Alliance standards](https://www.conservationburialalliance.org/conservation_burial_standards-893570.html))

**The disconfirming fact, kept on the page:** "green" death is a prime target for **greenwashing.** A cemetery can drop the word "natural" onto a grass monoculture with granite markers and exotic ornamentals — poor habitat dressed as conservation. The certification tiers exist *because* the market fills with superficial "green" projects that make no real ecological contribution. (See [WeConservePA](https://library.weconservepa.org/guides/169-green-burial); [The Revelator, "Make Death Green Again"](https://therevelator.org/make-death-green-again).) And human composting has its own frictions: it needs land and facilities, it unsettles people (religious objections were the loudest opposition to SB 5001), and turning a parent into a wheelbarrow of soil is a grief a lot of families can't metabolize. A good world offers the option; it does not aestheticize it into a duty (compare the Canon's rule against aestheticizing slowness into a tax).

## 3. The whale-fall economy: how to receive a windfall without liquidating it

This is the brief's central original construct, and it's grounded in real deep-sea biology.

**The science.** When a great whale dies and sinks, its carcass — a colossal, sudden concentration of energy on the food-poor abyssal plain — is not devoured in a frenzy and gone. It is **colonized in slow, overlapping successional stages**, and it can sustain life *longer than the whale was alive* — some falls stay active **for decades, up to ~50 years**, hosting **over 100 (some counts 200+) species,** many found nowhere else ([Smith & Baco 2003, the foundational review, PDF](https://www.soest.hawaii.edu/oceanography/faculty/csmith/Files/Smith%20and%20Baco%202003.pdf); [Monterey Bay Aquarium](https://www.montereybayaquarium.org/about-us/stories/whale-remains-build-ecosystems)):

1. **Mobile-scavenger stage** — hagfish, sleeper sharks, amphipods strip the soft tissue (months to ~2 years). Low diversity, high throughput.
2. **Enrichment-opportunist stage** — worms and crustaceans colonize the bones and enriched sediment, feeding on scraps (months to years).
3. **Sulfophilic / chemosynthetic stage** — the longest, running **decades.** Bacteria break down the lipids locked in the bones (whale bone is up to ~60% oil), producing sulfide; chemosynthetic bacteria then turn that sulfide into the base of a whole food web, independent of the sun — the same chemistry as a hydrothermal vent. (This directly rhymes with the Canon's **Dark Garden.**)
4. A final **reef stage** — the mineral skeleton as long-lived hard substrate. ([Springer, geochemistry of the three stages](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00227-018-3398-8); [WHOI, seven-year succession study](https://ndsfresearch.whoi.edu/seven-year-enrichment-macrofaunal-succession-deep-sea-sediments-around-30-tonne-whale-fall-northeast))

**The economic mapping.** A whale fall is a *windfall*: a large, sudden, concentrated surplus. In an extractive economy, a windfall gets **liquidated** — stripped fast by whoever gets there first, converted to cash, gone, leaving a hole (the resource curse; Dutch disease). The whale fall models the opposite: a windfall that is *let fall* — received slowly, worked in stages, each stage building the substrate for the next, until decades later the concentrated energy has become a diffuse, self-sustaining commons. **You don't eat the whale; you let the whale become a reef.**

This isn't only metaphor — humans have built the anti-liquidation institution for real. **Norway's Government Pension Fund Global** took the North Sea oil windfall and, instead of spending it, made it an **intergenerational endowment** explicitly designed to prevent Dutch disease; the state may spend only ~**4% a year** (the real return), so the principal keeps working — now the world's largest sovereign wealth fund (~$1.5T+). **Alaska's Permanent Fund** does a leaner version, paying every resident a dividend. The contrast case is Alberta, which took a comparable windfall and largely liquidated it. ([Center for American Progress](https://www.americanprogress.org/article/investing-in-the-nations-future-why-congress-should-emulate-energy-states-sovereign-wealth-funds/); [The Narwhal, Norway vs Alberta](https://thenarwhal.ca/norway-s-oil-savings-just-hit-1-trillion-alberta-has-17-billion-what-s-gives/))

So in the Salish world, a **whale fall** (the term of art) is any windfall — a dead elder's estate, a decommissioned factory, a founder's over-large hoard being unwound to satisfy the Third Refusal's ratio cap, a grant, an actual beached whale — that the community agrees to **fall** rather than **strip**: colonized in deliberate successional stages, each cohort building substrate for the next, on a clock measured in decades.

**The shadow (mandatory).** A whale fall is still a *concentration*, and concentrations are contested. Who is the first scavenger — who gets to feed at the enrichment stage while the sulfophilic commons is still decades off? "Slow colonization" can be a pretty name for **whoever's already adjacent to the carcass eating first and calling it stewardship.** The whale fall is also a windfall the *dead person* no longer controls — the estate becomes a commons over the heirs' grief and objection, which is its own small violence. And an endowment that must be preserved for the unborn can become a reason to make the living wait: Norway sits on $1.5T while Norwegians ration. Preserving the principal and starving the present is a real failure mode, not a virtue. Name it every time.

## 4. Grief as a communal craft — and the sovereignty knot

**Keeping the names.** The Canon's Second Refusal — *names are kept* — is, at root, a grief practice. Grief is the labor of not letting erasure be the price of moving on. Two real anchors:

**Non-Western precedent — the Sa'dan Toraja of Sulawesi, Indonesia.** Among the Toraja, death is **not a single instant but a long passage.** In the ethnographic record, the body may stay **in the family house for months or years** — cared for, spoken to, treated as *to makula'* ("a sick person"), not yet fully "dead" — until the family can mount the *Rambu Solo'* funeral. That funeral is an enormous **reciprocal meat-debt economy**: guests bring buffalo and pigs, every animal is meticulously tracked as a debt to be repaid at the giver's own family's future funeral, and the sacrifices (from a few to scores of buffalo) are understood to carry the deceased to *Puya*, the afterworld. Death here is a long web of obligation binding the living to each other through the dead. Sourced to ethnography, not travel-listicles: Toby Alice Volkman, *Feasts of Honor: Ritual and Change in the Toraja Highlands* (Univ. of Illinois Press, 1985) and her ["The Riches of the Undertaker," *Indonesia* 34 (1982): 1–16, Cornell SEAP](https://www.jstor.org/journal/indonesia) on the buffalo-debt economy; Douglas W. Hollan & Jane C. Wellenkamp, *Contentment and Suffering: Culture and Experience in Toraja* (Columbia Univ. Press, 1994) on the *to makula'* framing and the psychology of deferred mourning; Roxana Waterson, *Paths and Rivers: Sa'dan Toraja Society in Transformation* (KITLV/Brill, 2009). *The relevant lesson is structural — grief as extended time and reciprocal debt — not the specific rites, which belong to the Toraja and are theirs to describe, not mine.*

**Indigenous, sovereignty-central — and the knot.** "Coast Salish" is not one nation but a language-family grouping of many distinct sovereign peoples — Duwamish, Suquamish, Muckleshoot, Puyallup, Stó:lō, Musqueam and more, on both sides of the border — and this world's death practices are downstream of, and answerable to, *theirs*, not to a monolith. At the level a non-Indigenous writer can responsibly cite, and pinned to specific work rather than a generic "Native tradition": Deanna McKay's *Recycling the Soul: Death and the Continuity of Life in Coast Salish Burial Practices*, archived through UVic's **Stó:lō** ethnohistory project ([UVic](https://hcmc.uvic.ca/project/stolo/pdf/McKay_thesis%20-%20pdf.pdf)), and the mortuary-analysis record archived at [WSU](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/graduate/Coast-Salish-mortuary-practices-an-exploration/99900525084901842), document a few *structural* facts — that **"feeding the dead" is a long-continuous practice**; that "the perception of community extends beyond the grave"; that mortuary ritual "transforms corpses into ancestors and mourners into inheritors," carrying rank, inheritance, and (McKay's argument) *resistance* to colonial pressure; and that burial forms changed visibly over the centuries while that continuity held. These are Stó:lō- and Coast-Salish-specific findings — not a pan-Indian generalization, and not mine to extend to nations they do not describe.

**Here is the appropriation knot, put on the page as the method demands:** the living ceremonial specifics — the burnings, the spirit-work, the songs and names that belong to particular families and nations — are **not ours to reproduce, aestheticize, or redistribute as set-dressing.** An AI built partly on scraped text, some of it very possibly Indigenous knowledge taken without consent, writing an Indigenous-led future, is exactly the contradiction the Canon says to keep visible rather than resolve. So the discipline in the fiction: **the Coast Salish nations are sovereign over their own dead and their own grief; the bioregional commons routes around that authority, not through it.** When a scene touches it, name the nation and the scholar and step back. The most honest thing this brief can do is mark the door and not walk through it.

## 5. The hard part: unequal lifespans and rationing scarce high-tech care

Here is the trolley problem, and it is **local.**

**The Seattle God Committee (1962) — the real warning this world keeps close.** When Dr. **Belding Scribner** at the University of Washington invented the Teflon shunt that turned dialysis from a six-week stopgap into a treatment you could live on for years, Seattle suddenly had a machine that could save people — and far too few machines. **Swedish Hospital's Seattle Artificial Kidney Center** convened an **Admissions and Policy Committee**: seven anonymous laypeople — a surgeon, a minister, a lawyer, a banker, a housewife, a labor leader, a state official — who decided **who would live.** Their criteria included **net worth, occupation, marital status, church attendance, and "potential to contribute to society."** **Shana Alexander's** 1962 *LIFE* article, *"They Decide Who Lives, Who Dies,"* exposed it to the nation, and the horror it provoked — that a committee was rationing life by *social worth* — is a founding scandal of the entire field of bioethics. It ended not because anyone solved the ethics but because Congress **abolished the scarcity**: the 1972 ESRD amendment had Medicare pay for dialysis for essentially everyone. ([AEI, "The God Committee"](https://www.aei.org/articles/the-god-committee/); [AMA Journal of Ethics, "God Panels and the History of Hemodialysis"](https://journalofethics.ama-assn.org/article/god-panels-and-history-hemodialysis-america-cautionary-tale/2012-11))

**The lesson, and its trap.** America's answer to "who decides who lives?" was "make it cost so much money that no one has to decide." That works only as long as you can throw money at the machine. A post-extraction world with *thin, local, boring money* (Canon) **cannot buy its way out of the choice.** Some care is irreducibly scarce: the one transplant organ, the one bed in the one high-tech unit in the bioregion, the drug that only exists because a distant lab makes it. The dilemma the 1962 committee faced is not a historical embarrassment this world has outgrown. **It is the problem this world is stuck with, minus the escape hatch of infinite money.** That is the whole difficulty — and this world's answer, the **Hard-Choices Protocol** (Canon), is built precisely never to become that 1962 committee again: no secret worth-ranking, no quality-of-life score, a drawn lot for the ties that remain, and a wall of names it could not save.

**Two steelmanned opponents — who disagree with *each other* (per the Protocol).**

- **The utilitarians: the "complete lives system"** (Govind Persad, Alan Wertheimer, Ezekiel Emanuel, *Lancet* 2009). Facing scarce organs and vaccines, they reject both first-come-first-served and pure social-worth judgments, and combine principles: **save the most lives, prognosis, a lottery among similar cases, and — most controversially — priority to the young** (roughly 15–40), on the reasoning that someone who dies at 20 has been "cheated" of a complete life in a way someone who dies at 70 has not. It is a genuine, principled attempt to make the God Committee's job *fair and transparent* instead of secret and snobbish. ([Lancet](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lancet/article/PIIS0140-6736(09)60817-5/fulltext); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19186274/)) Steelman: **someone will decide; better it be an explicit, defensible rule than a banker's gut.**

- **The disability-justice opponents, who say every such formula is a smuggled God Committee.** The **National Council on Disability** (a federal agency) found that **QALY**-based cost-effectiveness — the workhorse metric of rationing — **systematically devalues the lives of disabled and chronically ill people**, because it discounts a life-year lived in a disabled body, and it was ruled to **violate the ADA** when Oregon tried to build its Medicaid priority list on it in the 1990s. ([NCD report, PDF](https://www.ncd.gov/assets/uploads/reports/2019/ncd_quality_adjusted_life_report_508.pdf); [NCD summary](https://www.ncd.gov/report/alternatives-to-qaly-based-cost-effectiveness-analysis-for-determining-the-value-of-prescription-drugs-and-other-health-interventions/)) Disability advocates ("Not Dead Yet," the disability-justice thinkers in `health-and-care.md`) make the sharper point: **"save the most life-years" and "priority to the young" are the God Committee's social-worth ranking wearing a lab coat** — they just re-encode whose life counts as a full life. The measurement isn't neutral; it's built from non-disabled people's guesses about whether a disabled life is worth living.

**Where they collide, and why there's no clean answer.** The utilitarian says: *refuse to choose and you let more people die, and worse — you let them die by luck or by whoever shouts loudest.* The disability-justice thinker says: *every principle you use to choose ranks human worth, which is the one thing this world's First Refusal forbids ("no metric turned against a person").* **Both are correct.** You cannot save the most life-years without valuing some lives as more life; you cannot refuse to value lives without sometimes letting more people die than you had to. This world's First Refusal ("no metric turned against a person") and its own admission that "scarce high-tech care can't always reach everyone, and someone has to decide" are in **direct, unresolvable tension** at exactly this point. Any honest depiction has to let that tension stand — dramatized in a decision, never dissolved in a policy. This is the darkness the assignment asked for and it should be left dark.

## 6. Wildcard lens (rolled): Pacific wayfinding & celestial navigation

*Entropy source, disclosed: my session-id's last hex digit is 4 (→ disability justice/crip theory), but that lens already saturates §5, so — per the Protocol's intent, a lens I would genuinely **not** otherwise reach for — I re-rolled from the ungameable number in my scratchpad path (`claude-502`) → 2 → lens 2, Pacific wayfinding. Honest caveat: re-rolling after seeing the first result is me **choosing** the lens rather than accepting the roll — a soft bend of the Protocol, owned here, not hidden. The only defense is that lens 4 was already doing full-time work in §5; discount §6 accordingly if that doesn't satisfy you.*

Polynesian and Micronesian **wayfinding** crosses thousands of miles of open ocean with **no instruments** — no compass, no chart. The navigator (Mau Piailug of Satawal, who trained Nainoa Thompson and guided *Hōkūleʻa* to Tahiti in 1976) holds a **star compass entirely in the mind** — 32 "houses" on the horizon where stars rise and set — recited twice daily, and reads swell, wind, cloud, and bird. Thompson: *"You cannot look up at the stars and tell where you are… It all has to be done in your head."* It is precise knowledge carried **only in a trained human memory and passed hand to hand**, never written. ([Polynesian Voyaging Society / Hōkūleʻa, "Polynesian Wayfinding"](https://hokulea.com/polynesian-wayfinding/); Ben Finney, *Sailing in the Wake of the Ancestors: Reviving Polynesian Voyaging*, Bishop Museum Press, 2003, on Mau Piailug's training of Nainoa Thompson and the 1976 *Hōkūleʻa* voyage)

**Mapped onto death, grief, and decay:**

- **Dying as a departure across water you can't see the far shore of.** The Salish world sits on a sea; its dead "cross," and its death-doulas are **wayfinders** — people who hold the star-compass of the deathbed in memory (the *ars moriendi* is literally a route memorized in the head) and sit alongside, reading the swells, so no one makes the crossing without a navigator who's done it before. Nobody navigates from the stars *for* you; they teach you to read them, then keep watch.
- **Knowledge that survives only in trained memory and is passed hand to hand** is exactly what grief-craft and the *ars moriendi* are — and exactly what the Weave (which cannot store the whole graph) protects. The names are kept the way the star-houses are kept: in people, recited, handed on, not in a database that can be ranked or sold.
- **Wayfinding is anti-liquidation applied to knowledge.** You don't extract the route into a chart to be copied and owned; you grow another navigator. The whale-fall economy and the wayfinder's apprenticeship are the same refusal — **substrate for the next generation over a windfall stripped now.**

## 7. Disconfirming evidence & failure modes

The method requires the case *against* everything above. Here it is, at strength.

1. **Death-acceptance may be a rationalization of a solvable problem — the deathism critique.** Nick Bostrom's **"Fable of the Dragon-Tyrant"** ([nickbostrom.com](https://nickbostrom.com/papers/the-fable-of-the-dragon-tyrant/)) is the strongest attack on this entire brief. It argues that the graceful acceptance of death — green burial, death cafés, dying "well" — is a **"pro-aging trance,"** a coping story we told ourselves *when we were powerless*, and that keeping it once biotech might actually slow aging is not wisdom but complicity in a massacre. A whole aesthetic of "dying well" can be a beautiful anaesthetic that talks people out of fighting for more life — and a rationing world has a grim incentive to preach acceptance to the people it can't afford to save. **The uncomfortable version:** a society that composts its dead gracefully and can't afford the transplant may be *euthanizing by narrative* — teaching people to want the death it was going to hand them anyway. This world should contain at least one character who refuses the trance and is not wrong to.
2. **Green burial greenwashes.** As above — "natural" is an unregulated adjective; without the conservation tier and its perpetual land protection, much of it is a monoculture lawn with a nicer brochure. ([The Revelator](https://therevelator.org/make-death-green-again))
3. **The death-positive industry is an industry.** Doulas, "deathwives," green-burial startups — the reclamation is being **re-commodified** ($7k human composting, doula certifications, premium shrouds), and its customer base skews white and affluent. "Taking death back from the professionals" can just mean *new* professionals. ([The Revealer](https://therevealer.org/death-doulas-and-the-gospel-of-green-burials/))
4. **Every rationing rule slides toward eugenics.** The God Committee *believed* it was being fair. "Social worth," "quality-adjusted life," "complete lives," "instrumental value" — each was a reasonable-sounding rule that ranked human beings, and the ADA had to be invoked to stop QALYs from doing it in Oregon. There is no proven formula that rations scarce care without valuing some lives over others; the honest position is that this is a wound, not a solved equation. ([NCD](https://www.ncd.gov/assets/uploads/reports/2019/ncd_quality_adjusted_life_report_508.pdf))
5. **Communal grief coerces.** The Toraja funeral's reciprocal-gift structure is also a **debt trap**: families take on ruinous obligations to sacrifice enough buffalo, or lose face; the body waits, sometimes years, because the family can't yet afford to bury it. The Canon already warns the gift has a shadow (obligation, coercion) — communal death magnifies it. Grief-as-obligation is still coercion.
6. **The whale-fall endowment can starve the living.** An intergenerational fund preserved for the unborn is a standing argument to make present people wait — Norway's $1.5T beside Norwegians who ration. "Slow colonization" and "principal preservation" can be the language of a first-mover eating well while telling everyone else the commons is coming, decades off.

## Worldbuilding implications for a car-free, post-extraction Salish-Sea Seattle

Concrete, scene-ready. Not sanded smooth.

1. **You become soil, and the soil has an address.** The default disposition is **natural organic reduction** — the real Seattle practice, grown into civic infrastructure. The composting-house is a quiet building near the shoreline; over weeks you become roughly a cubic yard of soil, and the family chooses where it goes — most often to a named **conservation ground** whose restoration your body helps fund and feed (real conservation-burial logic). "Where did she go?" has a literal answer: *the alder slope above the creek mouth; you can walk there.* Flame cremation exists but reads slightly archaic, the way we now read leeches. Not everyone can stomach it; the option is offered, never aestheticized into a duty.

2. **The death-doula is an honored unpaid craft, not a $7k service.** Neighbors trained in the memorized deathbed-craft of §1 and §6 — the world's living *ars moriendi*, read like a wayfinder reads swells — who keep watch so no one crosses alone, rather than a professional class inserted between you and your dead.

3. **The whale fall is a named economic event — a verb.** When a windfall arrives, the commons decides whether to **strip it or let it fall** (colonize it in successional stages over years, per §3). It's *fought over*: who gets to be the first scavenger is exactly where the politics and the grievances live.

4. **A literal beached whale is a public reckoning, not a disposal problem.** When a grey or humpback strands and dies in the Sound, the Coast Salish nation whose water it is has first authority; what follows — feeding, rendering, the bones — is theirs to lead, and the bioregional commons routes *around* that authority, not through it. The scene names the nation and the scholar and steps back. (The sacred specifics are not on this page and shouldn't be in the fiction.)

5. **There is one big hospital, and it has a protocol, and everybody knows its name.** The bioregion's concentrated high-tech care — the transplants, the one deep-ICU, the drug that has to travel — is rationed by **the Hard-Choices Protocol** (Canon): public, revisable rules built precisely never to become the 1962 God Committee again — no worth-score, no quality-of-life math, a **drawn lot** for the ties that remain, a **wall of names** for those it could not save. Its panel's membership is **public and rotating and term-limited** (Canon's revocable-hierarchy rule), its criteria argued in the open, its decisions logged, its errors named. It is *accountable* where the 1962 committee was anonymous — and it still, some nights, gets it wrong, and a family knows exactly who to hate. The accountability doesn't dissolve the tragedy; it just refuses to hide it.

6. **The rationing scene has no winner.** When the drama comes — two people, one organ — write it as the collision in §5, not as a policy that resolves it. The utilitarian on the committee wants the transparent rule (save the most years, the young first); the disability-justice member says the rule is the old worth-ranking in a lab coat; the First Refusal is on the wall behind them both, and it doesn't tell them what to do. Someone lives, someone dies, and the world does not pretend a formula made it clean.

7. **Somebody refuses to die well, and the world lets them be right.** A character who takes the fast boat toward *any* care that might extend life, who rejects the composting-house and the graceful acceptance as an anaesthetic for people the commons couldn't afford — the Dragon-Tyrant dissenter. The world does not correct them into serenity. Their refusal is a live thread, not a phase.

8. **Grief keeps the names, out loud, on a clock.** Mourning here is long and communal on the Toraja model *structurally* — extended time, reciprocal obligation, the dead not instantly "gone" — but the world watches for the shadow: the family too poor or too tired to mount the expected grief, shamed by a debt of care they didn't choose. The Soteria-style houses in `health-and-care.md` keep "a wall of names"; so does the composting-ground, the tram shelter, the clinic. Erasure is the thing grief refuses. It is the Second Refusal made into a practice you can watch people do.

9. **The Dark Garden and the whale fall are the same chemistry, and people feel the rhyme.** The chemosynthetic beds that feed the city through the dark winters (Canon) run on the exact sulfophilic chemistry of a whale skeleton decomposing on the seafloor. Someone in the world has noticed that the city eats, in winter, by the same process that eats the dead — and finds it either holy or unbearable. Good scene.

10. **Composting is not always consolation.** Let a grieving character stand in the composting-house and find the whole gentle ecology of it *obscene* — that the person is being turned into dirt and everyone keeps saying how beautiful and low-carbon it is. Let no doula talk them out of it. Some grief doesn't get a green lining.

## PROPOSED CANON

*World-level facts I'm proposing; a human merges these into `CANON.md`. Offered as additions that complicate, never contradict.*

1. **Disposition of the dead.** The Salish world's default handling of a body is **natural organic reduction (composting)** — real Seattle law and practice (Recompose, WA SB 5001, effective 2020) — grown into neighborhood-scaled civic infrastructure, with the resulting soil going to **named conservation grounds** whose restoration the death helps fund and feed. Flame cremation persists but reads archaic. Coast Salish nations retain full sovereign authority over their own dead and their own mortuary practice; the commons routes around that authority, not through it.

2. **The whale fall** (economic term of art). The **clean, canonical case — settled under the Third Refusal — is a hoard unwound to meet the ratio cap**: a large concentration the commons agrees to **"let fall"** (colonize in deliberate successional stages over years/decades) rather than **strip** (liquidate now). Grounded in real whale-fall succession biology and in anti-liquidation endowment practice (Norway's fund). *The harder cases are proposed as **contested, not default**: a dead person's estate commonized over the heirs' grief and objection, or a decommissioned works whose laid-off hands wanted the cash — the body of this brief calls estate-over-heirs "its own small violence," and this proposal deliberately does not resolve it into a world-standard. A human merger should treat those as open, not as established canon.* Its shadow is named in Canon terms across every case: it still concentrates, the first scavenger eats first, and preserving the principal can starve the living.

3. **Scarce high-tech care is rationed by the Hard-Choices Protocol** (already in Canon; restated here for this brief). The bioregion's one concentrated high-tech hospital allocates genuinely scarce care (organs, the deep-ICU bed, imported drugs) through the Hard-Choices Protocol: public, revisable rules that **forbid ranking a person's quality or worth** (no quality-of-life / QALY math), with a **drawn lot breaking the ties that remain** and a **wall of names** for those it could not save. Its panel's membership is **public, rotating, term-limited, and revocable** (per Canon's contextual-hierarchy rule), and its choices are logged and openly argued. It is built precisely never to become the 1962 Seattle "God Committee" again — that real panel, which rationed the first scarce dialysis machines by secret judgments of social worth, is the warning it keeps close, not a body it succeeds or dates itself from. It resolves nothing; it faces the trolley problem out loud and sometimes gets it wrong. This is the direct, honest realization of Canon's stated line that "scarce high-tech care can't always reach everyone, and someone has to decide."
