# Car-Free Urbanism & Slow Transit — a research brief

*Domain: how a Seattle on the Salish Sea moves without private cars, and what happens to the ground the cars used to own.*

This brief is homework, not manifesto. Everything below happened, is happening, or is under construction somewhere on the real Earth. Numbers are rough and cited so the next contributor can chase them down. The point is to keep the fiction *credible*: a car-free Salish-Sea Seattle should feel less like a poster and more like a place with drainage problems, delivery logistics, and a donkey-equivalent.

---

## 1. Car-free is old, not new — it just got paved over

The largest continuously inhabited car-free places on Earth are medieval, and they work.

- **Fes el-Bali (Fez, Morocco)** — the medina is generally called the largest contiguous car-free urban area *by population* on the planet: roughly **156,000 people**, streets down to about two feet wide. Logistics run on **~5,000–7,000 working donkeys and mules**, wooden handcarts, and human porters moving on the order of **1,000+ tons of goods a day**. Nothing motorized; it is not a museum, it's a working city with tanneries and markets. ([carfree.com/fes](https://www.carfree.com/fes/), [islands.com](https://www.islands.com/1947476/))
- **Venice** — larger in land area, car-free by water; freight moves by boat and then by hand-cart over the bridges.

The lesson for worldbuilding is not "medieval = quaint." It's that **a car-free city needs a last-hundred-meters freight answer** — a pack animal, a porter, a cargo bike, a canal barge. That answer shapes street width, doorways, and the daily soundscape more than any transit map does.

## 2. The retrofit precedents — how a *car* city un-cars itself

These matter more than the medinas, because Salish-Sea Seattle would be a *conversion*, not a fresh build.

**Groningen, Netherlands (Traffic Circulation Plan, 1977).** Max van den Berg's plan cut the medieval core into **four sectors**; a car cannot drive from one to another, it must go out to the ring road and back in, while a bicycle passes straight through. Overnight it made driving downtown slower than cycling. Private-car traffic in the center fell ~**47%**; today **~60% of all trips in Groningen are by bike**. Notably, **2.7 of the 6.4 million euros** spent went specifically to bike facilities. The plan was politically brutal at first and is now beloved — a useful reminder that the "long, uneven turning" in CANON had angry city-council meetings. ([bicycledutch](https://bicycledutch.wordpress.com/2016/03/08/), [Fietsberaad PDF](https://www.cycling-embassy.org.uk/sites/cycling-embassy.org.uk/files/documents/060608_Fietsberaad.pdf))

**Houten, Netherlands (built from the 1970s).** A new town *designed* around the bike from zero. A **ring road** carries all car traffic on the outside; the inside is a fine mesh of **~130 km of cycle paths** radiating from the train station on green "spines." **Bollards and gates** split the town into segments passable by bike/foot but not by car — so a bike goes A→B directly while a car has to exit to the ring and loop around. Has a famous two-level bike roundabout. Bike mode share rivals Groningen's. ([bicycledutch](https://bicycledutch.wordpress.com/2018/01/16/), [PeopleForBikes](https://www.peopleforbikes.org/news/suburbia-reimagined-afternoon-houten-netherlands))

**Pontevedra, Spain (from 1999).** The clearest "we just did it" case. Mayor Lores pedestrianized ~**1.3 million m²** of the center and inverted the mobility hierarchy: pedestrians and wheelchair users first, then bikes, then transit, then private cars last. Results: downtown traffic fell from **~80,000 vehicles/day (1999) to ~7,000**; center traffic down **69% since 2013 (90% in the very core)**; **CO₂ down ~70%**; **no traffic deaths since 2011**; in 2016 the police wrote **zero speeding tickets**. Key mechanic: they didn't ban cars so much as **kill the through-shortcuts and the on-street parking**, so driving through simply stopped being useful. ([citychangers.org](https://citychangers.org/pontevedra-the-little-utopian-city-that-drove-out-cars/), [fastcompany](https://www.fastcompany.com/90952175/))

**Vauban, Freiburg, Germany (1990s, on a former French barracks).** Not a total ban — a **"car-reduced" (stellplatzfrei)** neighborhood. You may own a car, but you cannot park it at your home; parking lives in **garages at the edge**, and a space costs on the order of **€17,500–23,000**. Result: **~70% of families own no car**; **~57% sold a car to move in**; car ownership ~**150–180 per 1,000 residents** vs. ~430 for Freiburg overall and ~800 in the US. Carpoolers get free tram passes. The tram runs down the spine; kids play in the street because the street was designed to expect them. ([csmonitor](https://www.csmonitor.com/2006/1220/p01s03-woeu.html), [fastcompany](https://www.fastcompany.com/90327301/))

**Barcelona Superblocks (*superilles*, from 2016).** Take a **3×3 grid of blocks**, push through-traffic to the perimeter, and hand the interior back to people. The first Poblenou superblock created **four ~2,000 m² plazas**; the Eixample plan promises **21 green streets + 21 squares** and **~33 hectares** reclaimed for pedestrians. Inside zones, key pollutants dropped ~**40%**; a full rollout was modeled to prevent **~667 premature deaths/year**. Worth keeping honest: it was **contentious** — businesses complained, some residents said perimeter traffic worsened. Utopia had public comment. ([C40](https://www.c40.org/case-studies/barcelona-superblocks/), [Urban Land](https://urbanland.uli.org/planning-design/barcelonas-experiment-superblocks))

## 3. The design vocabulary — woonerf, shared space, the 15-minute city

- **Woonerf** ("living yard"), pioneered by engineer **Joost Vahl in Delft in the late 1960s**, sanctioned by the Dutch government in **1976**. The street has **no curbs and no lane markings**; cars are legally guests moving at **walking pace (<~20 km/h)**; planters, trees, and playing children are placed *in the roadway* on purpose so a driver must weave and slow. The UK copy is the **"home zone."** This is the design DNA for a street that belongs "to feet, bikes, and small quiet trams." ([Wikipedia: Woonerf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woonerf))
- **The 15-minute city** — coined by **Carlos Moreno (Paris, 2016)**, championed by Mayor Anne Hidalgo: everything a person routinely needs (work, food, school, care, culture, green space) within a **15-minute walk or bike ride**. Went global during COVID. This is the spatial logic behind "bioregional, not national, is the unit of belonging" — belonging shrinks to the walkshed. ([RIBAJ](https://www.ribaj.com/culture/profile-carlos-moreno-15-minute-city-obel-award-planning/))
- **Tactical urbanism** — coined ~**2010 by Mike Lydon** (with Anthony Garcia): "short-term, low-cost, scalable interventions" — paint a plaza, drop planters, steal a parking lane for a week. Lineage runs through **Ciclovía (Bogotá)**, **Paris-Plages**, and **Janette Sadik-Khan's pedestrianization of Times Square** with lawn chairs and paint before any concrete was poured. This is *how* the "long, uneven turning" would actually look on the ground: not a decree, but a thousand illegal-ish planter boxes that stayed. ([gbdmagazine](https://gbdmagazine.com/tactical-urbanism/))

## 4. Slow transit that actually moves people — trams & light rail

CANON's "small quiet trams you flag like a taxi" is plausible with real numbers:

- **Street-running trams** average **~15–25 km/h** in mixed traffic; with a dedicated lane, fewer stops, and **signal priority** they hit 40+ (and up to ~88 km/h on reserved track). ([Railway News](https://railwaynews.net/), [dimensions.com](https://www.dimensions.com/collection/trams-light-rail))
- **Capacity**: a bus line moves roughly **3,800–7,200 passengers/hour/direction**; light rail **~12,000–20,000+**. On fixed track it isn't stuck behind congestion, so it stays *frequent and predictable* — which is what makes "flag it like a taxi" work: if it comes every few minutes you don't need a schedule. ([liveabout](https://www.liveabout.com/passenger-capacity-of-transit-2798765))
- Real-world texture for the setting: Seattle already runs the **Link light rail** and two modern **streetcar** lines, so trams-in-the-street are not exotic here; they're an existing organ the world would simply have grown.

Design note: to keep trams "quiet," the world would favor **grass/sedum track beds** (common in French and German systems — green rail rights-of-way that muffle noise and absorb rain) rather than steel-on-concrete canyons.

## 5. Cycling infrastructure — what makes a bike city vs. a bike gesture

- **Copenhagen**: ~**400 km of protected cycle tracks** (curb-separated, not paint), 25+ bike/pedestrian bridges. **~28% of *all* trips** in the city and **~49% of commutes to work/school** are by bike. **E-bikes and cargo bikes doubled 2020→~2024** — the city is now widening tracks specifically to fit **cargo bikes**, which do the work minivans used to. ([C40](https://www.c40.org/case-studies/c40-good-practice-guides-copenhagen-city-of-cyclists/), [Bicycle Account PDF](https://kk.sites.itera.dk/apps/kk_pub2/pdf/2420_d4db2492337f.pdf))
- The consistent finding across all Dutch/Danish cases: **protection and directness beat exhortation.** Bikes win when the bike route is *shorter and safer* than the car route by design (Groningen, Houten), not when people are asked to be virtuous. The **cargo bike / bakfiets** is the quiet workhorse: groceries, two kids, a cello, a keg for band practice.

## 6. Wind and water — long-distance travel that doesn't wreck the earth

CANON: "you cross the Sound in an afternoon under a self-trimming sail... Fast options exist (foiling boats) and are considered slightly rude." Both ends of that are real, current technology.

**Sail-cargo revival (the slow, honorable end):**
- **Grain de Sail (France)** — first modern purpose-built cargo *sailing* ship operator (2020, ~50 t). **Grain de Sail II** entered service **Jan 2024**: **52 m, ~350 t**, wind-powered except port maneuvers, running France↔New York↔Caribbean (wine out, organic cocoa/coffee back). **Grain de Sail III** planned for 2027: ~**200 TEU / 3,000 t**, ~**4,000 m² of sail** on three masts, Atlantic in ~**13 days**, claimed **>90% decarbonization**. ([Maritime Executive](https://maritime-executive.com/article/french-sail-cargo-company-unveils-plans-for-200-teu-sail-cargo-ship), [BusinessWire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20241023179973/en/))
- **TOWT (TransOceanic Wind Transport)** launched its largest sail-cargo vessel in Aug 2024. A real fleet is forming, not one stunt.

**Wind-*assist* on big conventional ships (the transitional, pragmatic end):**
- **Pyxis Ocean** (Cargill, 2023–24), fitted with two **BAR Technologies WindWings** (rigid wing sails ~**37.5 m / 123 ft tall**, foldable in port), reported **~15%** average fuel savings, and on favorable routes ran **~40% less fuel** than a sister bulker, saving ~**11 t fuel/day** in good wind. Other systems: **Flettner rotor sails** (spinning cylinders), **suction sails**, **kites**. Whole-fleet **WAPS** savings run **~5–30%** depending on route and wind. ([Cargill](https://www.cargill.com/2024/first-wind-powered-ocean-vessel-maiden-voyage), [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2023/10/05/1200788439/))

**The "slightly rude" fast option, made concrete:**
- **Candela P-12** (Sweden) — an **electric hydrofoil ferry** that *flies* on computer-controlled submerged wings above ~20 knots, cutting energy use **~80%**. Service speed **25 kn** (30+ in trials), **30 seats**, **252 kWh** battery, **~40 nm** range. Its Stockholm boat *Nova* is in service with **~98% less CO₂** than the diesel ferry it replaced. This is *exactly* CANON's "foiling boat you take one floor" — real, electric, silent, and a bit gauche because it skips the afternoon. ([Candela](https://candela.com/newsroom/candela-p-12-completes-worlds-longest-electric-sea-journey/), [CompositesWorld](https://www.compositesworld.com/news/))

So the setting's mobility ladder is fully grounded: **feet → cargo bike → tram → self-trimming sailboat / sail-ferry (the normal, dignified way to cross the Sound) → foiling electric hydrofoil (fast, silent, faintly embarrassing).**

## 7. What happens to the ground the cars used to own

This is the richest visual seam for the fiction, and it's all real practice.

**Depaving.** Started in **Portland, OR in 2008** ("Depave"); volunteers pull up asphalt by hand. Portland alone has removed **200,000+ sq ft** of pavement. Rule of thumb: **removing 1,000 sq ft of pavement kills ~22,000 gallons of annual runoff**, lets soil recharge groundwater, and cools the heat island. The movement spread to Chicago, Cincinnati, Canada. ([depave.org](https://www.depave.org/whydepave), [Streetsblog](https://usa.streetsblog.org/2019/06/12/))

**Daylighting buried creeks.** Cities are digging up creeks that were piped underground a century ago and letting them run in open air again.
- **Cheonggyecheon, Seoul (2002–2005)** — tore out an **elevated freeway**, daylighted **~6 km** of stream, cost **~$380M**, done in under 2.5 years; now a downtown green corridor.
- **Thornton Creek, Seattle** — a piped creek under a parking lot restored to an open, multi-chamber channel that floods and filters sediment on purpose. ([land8](https://land8.com/10-reasons-why-cities-should-daylight-rivers/), [ArchDaily](https://www.archdaily.com/1020945/))
- Seattle has *dozens* of buried creeks (Ravenna, Licton Springs, the ghost of the Duwamish's meanders) — a ready-made map of where the water wants to come back.

**The shoreline coming up to meet the city** — already underway here. Seattle's **Elliott Bay seawall rebuild** designed the wall itself as **salmon habitat**: a textured face with shelves for algae and invertebrates, offshore rock beds for juvenile salmon to hide and forage, and — the vivid detail — **glass blocks set into the sidewalk overhead that let daylight down to the water** so young salmon will use the migration corridor instead of avoiding the dark. The old seawall had cut salmon by ~**90%**; a 2018 count logged **10,000 juvenile salmon in a single May day** along the new one. ([UW News](https://www.washington.edu/news/2017/05/18/), [Encyclopedia of Puget Sound](https://www.eopugetsound.org/magazine/IS/seawall))

**Grown, not just built (the material palette).**
- **Cross-laminated timber (CLT) / glulam** is the structural backbone: kiln-dried boards glued cross-grain into panels with concrete-comparable strength and good fire resistance (it chars predictably instead of buckling). Proof it scales: **Mjøstårnet, Norway (2019)**, **85.4 m** tall, mostly CLT/glulam/LVL, holding apartments, a hotel, and a pool. ([Dezeen](https://www.dezeen.com/2019/03/19/mjostarne-worlds-tallest-timber-tower-voll-arkitekter-norway/))
- **Mycelium** — fungal-network composites grown into molds, then dried; excellent **insulation**, light, fire-retardant, compostable, but **weak in structure** (good for panels, acoustic tiles, insulation, non-load-bearing infill — *not* beams). This maps precisely to CANON's "soft-cornered, grown as much as built": a CLT-and-salvaged-steel skeleton, mycelium-and-timber skin, moss on everything.

## Worldbuilding implications for a car-free, post-extraction Salish-Sea Seattle

Concrete, usable details — grounded in the precedents above:

1. **The turning was tactical before it was legal.** The city didn't ban cars; it *starved* them, Pontevedra/Groningen-style. First came a decade of guerrilla planter boxes and painted plazas (Lydon-style tactical urbanism) that nobody quite authorized and nobody removed. Then the through-shortcuts were cut and the parking pulled, and driving simply stopped being worth it. Old people still argue about the year it "really" happened; there is no year. (Matches CANON's "long, uneven turning.")

2. **The Sector Gate.** Adopt Groningen/Houten's trick literally: the city is quartered by **bollards and low stone gates** a bike or tram or handcart slides through but a vehicle can't. Locals navigate by which gate you take. "Meet me at the Fremont gate" is an address. The gates are old, mossy, and half-decorative now — nobody remembers them being controversial, though they were.

3. **The cargo-bike is the pickup truck.** The workhorse of daily life is the **bakfiets / cargo trike** — hauling kids, kegs, a double bass, mussels from the rope-farms. Copenhagen's real cargo-bike boom says this is the mundane texture, not a novelty. The band girl is perpetually late because she's pedaling a borrowed cargo bike with the amp strapped in, uphill, in the rain.

4. **A donkey-equivalent for the last hundred meters.** Fez runs on 5,000 mules; a car-free Seattle needs its own answer for the alley that's too narrow and too steep for a tram. Invent it: hand-porters, a pulley-and-dumbwaiter culture on the steep streets, dock-carts, maybe actual pack animals on the greenbelt hills. Whatever it is, it has a *sound* (Fez's donkey bells) — this is the city's ambient noise where engines used to be.

5. **The trams are quiet because they run on grass.** Sedum-and-grass track beds (real, all over French/German systems) mean the rails are green ribbons, not steel canyons; they hush the wheels and drink the rain. You flag one like a taxi because it comes every few minutes — frequency, not schedule, is what "flag it" requires. They average a walking-ish 15–20 km/h and nobody minds.

6. **Crossing the Sound is a self-trimming sail, and the afternoon is the point.** Grain de Sail / TOWT prove wind-cargo is a working revival, not nostalgia; the **Candela hydrofoil** proves the "slightly rude" fast boat is real and electric. Put both in the harbor: dignified sail-ferries for people who have an afternoon, and a silent foiling hydrofoil for the person in a hurry who gets a *look* for taking it.

7. **The creeks came back and brought the salmon.** Where a road was pulled, a buried creek was daylighted (Thornton Creek is the real local precedent). Kids know the creeks by the culverts they used to hide in. The **Elliott Bay seawall with glass sidewalk-blocks lighting the salmon's path** is almost too on-the-nose to be invented — use it: you can stand on the waterfront and see, through the pavement under your feet, young salmon running the lit corridor below.

8. **The material honesty is structural, not decorative.** Buildings are a CLT/glulam-and-salvaged-steel skeleton (Mjøstårnet proves 85 m is fine) wrapped in mycelium-and-timber skin. Mycelium is insulation and infill, never a beam — so a knowledgeable character would *scoff* at someone claiming a mushroom held up a roof. Moss on the north faces. The rust on the salvaged steel is left honest (Refusal #2).

9. **Reclaimed street space is uneven and imperfect.** Not every old road became a jewel-box plaza. Some are depaved to bare soil and weeds and community potatoes; some are half-plaza, half-swale; some superblock interiors still argue with their perimeters (Barcelona's honest mess). Restoration is "ongoing and imperfect," per CANON — show the seams.

10. **The city has a walkshed, and belonging shrinks to fit it.** The 15-minute city is the spatial reason "the Salish" replaced "the nation" as the unit of belonging: when everything you need is a 15-minute walk and the farthest you *routinely* go is a sail across the Sound, your loyalties re-scale to the bioregion. People know their walkshed the way we know our phone.

## Open questions for the next contributor

1. **The steep-hill problem.** Seattle is *hills* — Queen Anne, Capitol Hill, the counterbalance. Bikes and trams both struggle with real grade. What's the actual mechanism? Electric-assist cargo bikes? Funiculars and inclined elevators (real in Zagreb, Valparaíso, old Seattle's Counterbalance)? Public dumbwaiters? Someone should walk one steep block in full.

2. **Accessibility and the slow city.** "Slowness is the point" is lovely until you use a wheelchair, are in labor, or are having a stroke. Pontevedra explicitly put wheelchair users *first*. What does emergency response, disability mobility, and care-at-distance actually look like when there are no ambulances-as-we-know-them? (Pairs with whoever writes the care/medicine system.)

3. **What happened to the concrete?** A car city is millions of tons of asphalt, seawall, parking structure, and I-5 itself. Depaving is hand-scale; a whole freeway is not. Was I-5 daylighted like Seoul's Cheonggyecheon freeway? Left as a ruin-park? Ground up for aggregate? The bones of the old system are a place, and someone should walk it.

4. **Freight at bioregional scale.** Sail-cargo is proven for wine and cocoa across an ocean. But how do 700,000 people get their *bulk* — grain, timber, medicine, the things the walkshed can't grow? What's the port, the rail, the barge network on the Sound? Where's the warehouse district, and who works the docks?

5. **The un-quaint winter.** Cascadia is dark and wet Nov–March. Cycling mode share in Copenhagen holds through winter with infrastructure and habit — but what does the *slow* city feel like in a 4pm December dark with rain sheeting off the mycelium eaves? The romance is easy in June. Write the wet version.
