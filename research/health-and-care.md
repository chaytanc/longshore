# Health and Care — a research brief

*Domain: how healthcare works when it isn't broken, in a post-extraction bioregional Seattle.*

This brief gathers real programs, real outcome data, and real thinkers so the world stays credible. The through-line: **the most effective care systems humans have actually built are small, local, relational, and refuse to make care a commodity.** That is not utopian wishful thinking — it is the finding of the evidence. The hard part is never the vision. The hard part is staffing it, funding it, and living with the cases it can't fix.

---

## 1. The neighborhood is the unit of care

Two real national systems prove that primary care organized around *where people live* — not around hospitals or insurance — produces rich-country outcomes on poor-country budgets.

**Cuba's consultorio / polyclinic model.** The front line is the *consultorio*: a neighborhood office staffed by a family-doctor-and-nurse team who **live in the neighborhood they serve** and care for a defined panel of roughly 600–900 people they can describe from memory. Each consultorio is backed by a *polyclinic* — a community multispecialty center offering labs, dentistry, physiotherapy, psychiatry, ob-gyn, and traditional/natural medicine. One polyclinic supervises up to ~30 consultorios (20,000–60,000 people); there are ~451 of them. Care runs on low-tech population-health methods: paper family charts, home visits, risk-stratification registries, daily logs. Consultorios resolve ~80% of health problems at the neighborhood level and emphasize prevention. The system was built in 1984 and reaches near-universal outcomes despite a developing-country economy and a decades-long embargo. ([MEDICC Review](https://mediccreview.org/cubas-family-doctor-and-nurse-teams-a-day-in-the-life/); [RAND](https://www.rand.org/pubs/commentary/2017/10/doing-more-with-less-lessons-from-cubas-health-care.html); [JABFM](https://www.jabfm.org/content/18/4/297))

**Costa Rica's EBAIS.** Introduced in 1994, the *Equipo Básico de Atención Integral de Salud* is a basic team — a doctor, nurse, technical assistant (a community health worker who does home visits), medical clerk, and pharmacist — assigned to a geographic population of ~4,500. About ten EBAIS teams form a "health area." By 2019 there were 1,053 EBAIS teams plus 106 support teams for behavioral health and social services. Outcomes: over the reform's first nine years, areas that got EBAIS teams saw a **9% drop in infant mortality and 2% drop in adult mortality**, with the biggest gains among older adults and in cardiovascular deaths. Costa Rica spends a fraction of US per-capita health dollars and has comparable or better life expectancy. ([Commonwealth Fund](https://www.commonwealthfund.org/publications/case-study/2021/mar/community-oriented-primary-care-lessons-costa-rica); [PHCPI](https://improvingphc.org/costa-rica))

**The shared design principles** (usable directly for worldbuilding):
- Every person is *empaneled* — assigned to a specific team that is responsible for them whether or not they show up.
- The team **goes to the home.** Care is proactive and outreach-based, not appointment-and-wait.
- Prevention and social determinants are inside the clinic's job, not outside it.
- The data collected is population health, not billing.

**Tradeoff / honesty:** Both systems trade some individual choice and consumer-style convenience for universality. Cuba's system also runs chronically short of supplies and medicines, and its doctors are underpaid; the "efficiency" is partly extracted from provider sacrifice. A good world keeps the model and fixes *that*.

## 2. Free at the point of care — the principle, stated plainly

When Aneurin Bevan launched Britain's NHS on **5 July 1948**, he built it on three principles worth quoting because they are the cleanest statement of the ethic: care should be **comprehensive** (meet everyone's needs), **universal** (free at the point of delivery), and allocated **by clinical need, not ability to pay**. Bevan's phrase was "**universalise the best**" — not a safety net for the poor, but the good care extended to everyone.

The origin story matters for a mutual-aid world: Bevan drew the model from the **Tredegar Medical Aid Society**, a workers' mutual-aid scheme in his Welsh mining town where members paid small dues and everyone got care. "All I am doing," he said, "is extending to the entire population of Britain the benefits we had in Tredegar." The NHS is, in its DNA, a mutual-aid fund scaled to a nation. ([UK Gov History Blog](https://history.blog.gov.uk/2023/07/13/the-founding-of-the-nhs-75-years-on/); [Wellcome Collection](https://wellcomecollection.org/stories/the-birth-of-britain-s-national-health-service))

## 3. Community health workers: the evidence is now solid

The "technical assistant" in Costa Rica, the *promotora* in Latin America, the *barefoot doctor* — the **community health worker (CHW)** is a trusted member of a community, often lay-trained, who bridges people and the formal system. This was long dismissed as a poor-country improvisation; it isn't. Strong US evidence now shows CHWs improve outcomes for chronic disease (diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular disease), improve perinatal outcomes and cancer screening, improve access to primary care, and reduce costs. The strongest evidence is for CHWs *employed by* and *integrated into* care teams (not bolted on). The **IMPaCT** model (Penn) is a rigorously trialed, evidence-based CHW program that improved outcomes and returned a positive ROI to Medicaid. ([Penn LDI](https://ldi.upenn.edu/our-work/research-updates/community-health-workers-role-grows-with-evidence-that-they-improve-care-and-equity/); [Health Affairs, IMPaCT](https://www.healthaffairs.org/doi/10.1377/hlthaff.2019.00981); [CHW Central / Annual Review of Public Health](https://chwcentral.org/wp-content/uploads/Community-Health-Worker-Integration-with-and-Effectiveness-in-Health-Care-and-Public-Health-in-the-United-States.pdf))

**The local ancestor.** Seattle's own **Carolyn Downs Family Medical Center** (Central District, 2101 E Yesler Way) is the **last survivor of the 13 People's Free Medical Centers founded by the Black Panther Party** (1969–1975). The Panthers ran free first-aid, vaccination, and screening clinics (blood pressure, lead, TB, diabetes) staffed by volunteer physicians, nurses, and pharmacists who *trained community members to become lab techs, patient advocates, and CHWs*. Their sickle-cell screening campaign shamed Congress into the 1972 National Sickle Cell Anemia Control Act. Alondra Nelson's *Body and Soul* is the definitive history. When the other clinics closed under police harassment and funding strain, Seattle's chapter (Elmer Dixon) secured federal funding; it reopened in 1978 named for Carolyn Downs, a Panther organizer and mother, and **still operates today.** This is the real, local root of the community-clinic movement — it belongs in the world's history. ([BlackPast](https://blackpast.org/african-american-history/black-panther-partys-free-medical-clinics-1969-1975/); [Country Doctor CHC](https://countrydoctor.org/carolyn-downs-fmc/); [Think Global Health](https://www.thinkglobalhealth.org/article/the-black-panthers-community-health-legacy))

## 4. Disability justice: care as collective responsibility

The most important recent thinking about *how care should feel* comes from disability justice — a framework launched in **2005 by disabled activists of color Patty Berne, Mia Mingus, and Stacey Milbern**, incubated at the Bay Area performance project **Sins Invalid**. Its **ten principles**: intersectionality; leadership of the most impacted; anti-capitalist politics; cross-movement solidarity; recognizing wholeness; sustainability; cross-disability solidarity; interdependence; collective access; collective liberation. It centers "disabled people of color, immigrants, queer and trans and gender-non-conforming people, houseless people, incarcerated people, people whose ancestral lands were stolen." ([Sins Invalid, 10 Principles](https://sinsinvalid.org/10-principles-of-disability-justice/))

Key concepts a fiction writer can put in a character's mouth:

- **Access intimacy** (Mia Mingus): "that elusive, hard-to-describe feeling when someone else *gets* your access needs" — as small as a knowing look, without your having to explain or justify. Its opposite is **forced intimacy**: the exhausting labor of having to disclose and educate to get basic access. In this world, access intimacy is the *design goal* of a care encounter. ([Leaving Evidence / Mia Mingus](https://leavingevidence.wordpress.com/2017/04/12/access-intimacy-interdependence-and-disability-justice/))
- **Pods and care webs** (Mingus; and Leah Lakshmi Piepzna-Samarasinha's *Care Work: Dreaming Disability Justice*, 2018): a **care web** is a self-organized group — disabled and non-disabled — who provide each other care and access as a *collective responsibility and pleasure*, not a chore or charity. This is the grassroots substrate beneath any formal clinic. Piepzna-Samarasinha frames access "as radical love." ([Care Work — Arsenal Pulp](https://arsenalpulp.com/Books/C/Care-Work); [Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Care_Work))
- **Interdependence over independence.** Disability justice rejects the "independent living" fantasy of the self-sufficient individual in favor of openly *needing each other.* This dovetails exactly with a mutual-aid economy.

**Mutual-aid care in practice (real, recent):** During COVID-19, the **Disability Justice Culture Club** (five disabled/neurodivergent queer people of color in Oakland) ran a mutual-aid project pairing high-risk, disabled, and elderly people with allies for shelter-in-place support. Across queer/trans and disabled communities, survival ran on shared medication, pooled bail and rent funds, food distributions, and neighbor-built collectives — "the ways we kept each other alive are often completely incomprehensible to abled and neurotypical people." Trans Lifeline runs no-questions-asked microgrants. This is the texture of care in the world already; the difference in *our* world is that it is the load-bearing layer, not the emergency backstop. ([In These Times](https://inthesetimes.com/article/disabled-queer-activists-isolation-mutual-aid-bay-area-covid-19); [Disability Visibility Project](https://disabilityvisibilityproject.com/2021/10/03/how-disabled-mutual-aid-is-different-than-abled-mutual-aid/))

## 5. Mental health without the asylum

**The deinstitutionalization lesson.** Emptying the asylums (from the 1960s, driven by antipsychotics, exposés of abuse, civil-rights advocacy, and states wanting to cut costs) was *right in principle* and *catastrophic in execution*, because the community services meant to replace the institutions were never funded. The result: homelessness, the "revolving door," and **trans-institutionalization** — people moved from hospitals into jails and prisons. The clear finding: where comprehensive community services *do* exist, people with severe mental illness benefit greatly; where beds close without them, harm follows. The moral for the world: you cannot abolish an institution without *building the web that replaces it first.* ([Psychiatric Services](https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/appi.ps.52.8.1039); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deinstitutionalisation))

Two humane, evidence-backed alternatives to the locked ward:

**Open Dialogue (Western Lapland, Finland).** A whole regional mental-health system (pop. ~72,000) retrained *all* its psychiatric staff in family/network therapy. When someone hits a crisis, a team responds within 24 hours and holds **network meetings — usually in the person's home — with the person, their family, and their social network**, making decisions transparently, together, tolerating uncertainty rather than rushing to diagnose or medicate. Outcomes for first-episode psychosis over multiple cohorts: **~81% with no residual psychotic symptoms at two years, ~84% back in work or study, and only ~33% ever using antipsychotics** — with results holding stable a decade later and duration of untreated psychosis falling to ~3 weeks. ([Seikkula et al., long-term outcomes](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/17522439.2011.595819); [review, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26909395/))

**Soteria (Loren Mosher, 1971, Santa Clara).** A small home-like house where people in acute psychosis live with mostly **non-professional staff** in a calm relational community, with **minimal or no antipsychotic medication** (none in the first six weeks except emergencies). Trials found outcomes at least as good as standard hospital care — and *better* for those predicted to do worst; ~43% of Soteria residents never took neuroleptics and did better than those who had. Replicated at Soteria Berne. ([Mad in America](https://www.madinamerica.com/2019/09/soteria-house-heal/); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soteria_(psychiatric_treatment)))

**Honesty / limits:** Neither model claims to work for everyone. Both depend on early response, small caseloads, and staff who can sit with distress — expensive in the one resource that can't be mass-produced: skilled, patient human attention. And even at their best, some people remain in lasting suffering. The world should show that too.

## 6. Birth, harm reduction, and gender-affirming care — three concrete services

**Community birth / midwifery.** For **low-risk pregnancies** (term, single fetus, vertex, no prior cesarean), planned birth at home or in a midwife-led birth center — *inside an integrated, regulated system with fast transfer to hospital when needed* — shows outcomes comparable to hospital birth with far fewer interventions: dramatically lower cesarean rates (one birth-center study: −48% for first-time mothers, −82% for those who'd given birth before), lower NICU admission, higher breastfeeding. The load-bearing safety condition is the **integrated referral system**, not the building. ([AAFP, Out-of-Hospital Birth](https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2021/0601/p672.html); [Wallace et al., birth-center outcomes](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10771911/))

**Harm reduction.** The evidence is unambiguous and worth stating flatly because it's still politically contested in our world: **syringe service programs** cut HIV/HCV transmission (roughly 10–40% risk reduction) and needle-sharing (~50%); **supervised consumption sites** reduce overdose deaths and disease and increase entry into care, **with no increase in drug use or crime**; **naloxone** distribution saves lives. Harm reduction treats the person's survival and dignity as the goal, not their abstinence. ([AJPM systematic review](https://www.ajpmonline.org/article/S0749-3797(21)00275-0/abstract); [systematic review, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12765503/))

**Gender-affirming care — the informed-consent model.** Pioneered and formalized at **Callen-Lorde Community Health Center** in New York, the informed-consent model treats the adult patient as the authority on their own gender: hormone therapy starts once the person understands risks and benefits and consents — **no gatekeeping mental-health evaluation required** unless a specific concern arises. WPATH's Standards of Care v8 endorses the shift away from gatekeeping toward informed consent. Outcomes: **very high patient satisfaction and better mental-health outcomes**, largely *because* access improves and the humiliating gauntlet is removed. ([Callen-Lorde informed-consent study](https://academic.oup.com/jsm/article/18/1/201/6955923); [Journal of Ethics, AMA](https://journalofethics.ama-assn.org/article/informed-consent-medical-care-transgender-and-gender-nonconforming-patients/2016-11))

## 7. Land, plants, and whose knowledge this is

Care in the Salish Sea does not start in 1948 or 1969. The **Coast Salish peoples** have tended a sophisticated medicine tradition here since time immemorial. **Western redcedar** ("the Tree of Life") — needle teas for respiratory and urinary infection, pitch salves for wounds. **Devil's club** root and inner bark for inflamed joints and respiratory ailments. **Salmonberry** leaf teas for digestion; berries as medicine and convalescent tonic across colds, burns, and more. Much of this was **outlawed and nearly lost to colonization** and is now in active revival, taught to youth alongside language and food. Any credible Salish-Sea health system is *layered on top of and in dialogue with* Indigenous knowledge and sovereignty — not a wellness aesthetic borrowed from it. Handle with attribution and humility. ([Salish plant medicine — CWIS](https://cwis.org/salish-foods-medicines/); [UBC Learning Circle](https://learningcircle.ubc.ca/2018/03/29/ancestral-knowledge-and-northwest-native-plants/))

## Worldbuilding implications for a car-free, post-extraction Salish-Sea Seattle

Concrete, usable texture. Not sanded smooth — this world has grief and limits too.

1. **The clinic is the size of a corner house, and you walk to it.** Every neighborhood has its *consultorio* — soft-cornered, mycelium-and-salvaged-timber, moss on the north wall, a kettle always on, kids' drawings and a hand-painted panel of who works here. Your team of maybe four — a doctor or nurse-practitioner, a nurse, a *tender* (the community health worker), and someone who keeps the paper family-charts — is responsible for the ~600 people on your block-cluster whether you come in or not. They resolve most of what happens to a body right there. The polyclinic (labs, dentistry, midwifery, the crisis house, the traditional-medicine room) is a tram-flag away, shared by a dozen consultorios.

2. **When you're sick, the tender comes to you.** There is no waiting room as a place of dread. Someone knocks — someone who knows your stairs are hard, knows your cat's name, knows you skipped last winter's checkup because your sister was dying. This is *access intimacy* made into infrastructure: you don't have to explain yourself from scratch to be cared for. Care is proactive and it comes on foot.

3. **Getting gender-affirming care is a conversation, not a trial.** You go to the clinic (or the trans-run care-web that meets above the record store), you talk through what you want and what it does to a body, you consent, you start. No panel decides whether you're "really" trans. The old before-times gatekeeping is told as a horror story — the way we tell stories about bleeding people with leeches. The band girl who came in from the flat interior town found the *thread* first; the hormones and the haircut were waiting when she arrived.

4. **The crisis house is a house.** When someone comes apart, they are not locked in a ward. A team is at their door within a day, and the meeting happens in their own kitchen, with their people in the room and nothing decided behind their back (Open Dialogue). If they need somewhere to *be*, there's the Soteria-style house by the water — a real home, mostly non-professional companions who sit up with you through the nights, minimal medication, no rush to name what's happening. Most people come back. Not everyone. The house keeps a garden and a wall of names.

5. **Your care web is realer than the state.** The formal clinic is the thin, boring, reliable layer (like the money). The thick layer is the *web*: the people who bring soup, split your meds run, take your shift, sign to you, push your chair to the shoreline. Disabled and chronically ill people built these webs to survive and now everyone lives inside one. Interdependence isn't a hardship here; it's the water. "Independence" sounds, to these people, a little sad and a little cruel.

6. **Harm reduction is just... reduction of harm.** There is a clean, warm, unremarkable place near the docks where you can use safely, get supplies, get naloxone, get patched up, get met. Nobody is trying to catch you or fix you as the price of care. Someone hands you tea you didn't order.

7. **Birth happens where the person wants it, safely.** Most low-risk births are at home or the neighborhood birth-room with a midwife, and the whole thing is stitched into the polyclinic so a hard case is on the water to the hospital fast. Birth is a community event, not a medical emergency by default.

8. **The medicine is layered and old.** The traditional-medicine room in the polyclinic is not decoration — it's cedar-needle tea and devil's-club salve alongside the antibiotics, and the Coast Salish knowledge-keepers who steward it are respected professionals, not props. The pharmacy shelf and the drying-herbs shelf are in the same building and neither pretends the other isn't there.

9. **The grief is kept, like the names.** People still die. Chronic pain still grinds. The Soteria house loses people. Cuba-style shortages have their echo here — a rare drug that has to travel by rail and boat and *isn't there yet*; the slowness that is usually the point is, sometimes, the thing that kills you. The world does not pretend care is a solved problem. It pretends nothing.

10. **The clinic on E Yesler still has its old name.** Following the Second Refusal (names are kept), the neighborhood clinic in the Central District is still **Carolyn Downs** — named for a Panther organizer and mother, dead young, in the before-times. The plaque tells the truth: this was one of thirteen free clinics the state tried to shut down, and the last one standing. The whole system remembers it came from mutual aid that had to fight to exist.

## Open questions for the next contributor

1. **Who trains the tenders, and how is the work honored without a metric?** In a world with no attention-scores and thin money, what makes someone a respected community health worker? An apprenticeship? A naming? How do you value care labor without ranking or paying-per-patient — the very things that broke it before?

2. **What happens at the edge of the model?** Cuba/Costa Rica resolve ~80% locally — but the other 20% (complex surgery, cancer, ICU, rare disease) needs concentrated, expensive, high-tech care. Where is the *one big hospital* in a bioregion that distrusts centralization, and how does a car-free, gift-economy world ration a scarce transplant or a machine that only exists in one city? This is where the grief and the politics live.

3. **How does the Weave carry medicine?** A trans kid finds *you are not the only one* over the Weave — but can they get an endocrinologist's second opinion, a rare-disease community, a drug that isn't local? What travels over the slow-warm-mail network, and what can't (controlled substances, live diagnosis, an actual body)?

4. **Consent, capacity, and the person who refuses care.** Open Dialogue and Soteria minimize coercion — but what does this world do about the genuinely dangerous crisis, or someone who won't accept help and is dying? A world that abolished the locked ward still has to answer for the hardest cases without quietly rebuilding the asylum. What's the honest, imperfect answer?

5. **Indigenous sovereignty and the health commons.** If Coast Salish medicine is load-bearing, then Coast Salish *nations* have authority over it. How does a bioregional (not national) health commons share power with sovereign tribes — governance, land, knowledge — without appropriating or absorbing them?
