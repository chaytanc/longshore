# Research brief: Music, DIY/queer/punk scenes, and how marginalized people form community

*A grounded brief for The Reality Next Door. Domain: the cultural heart — punk, queer nightlife, dance, and the infrastructure of mutual aid that lets a scene take care of its own. All precedents below are real. Citations at the end of each section.*

The premise of this brief is simple and load-bearing: **the technologies of scene-building that this world runs on already existed, and most of them were built by people who had been pushed out of everything else.** A car-free, un-metered, gift-economy Seattle is not inventing a new way for music to hold a community together. It is inheriting a very old one — the basement show, the sliding-scale door, the house that takes in the kid nobody else wanted — and finally letting it be the *main* thing instead of the underground thing.

## 1. The Pacific Northwest has its own lineage, and it's about *rooms*

The single most important fact about Northwest music history is that **the fights were about access to rooms** — who is allowed to be in the room, and how old they get to be.

### The Teen Dance Ordinance: how a city outlawed its own scene

From **1985 to 2002**, Seattle effectively criminalized all-ages shows. The Teen Dance Ordinance (TDO), passed after a scandal at a club called The Monastery, required any all-ages dance to carry **$1,000,000 in liability insurance**, hire **two off-duty police officers inside plus one outside**, and enforce a bizarre age band (patrons 15–20 only; younger kids needed a guardian; anyone over 20 had to be accompanying a minor). It was designed to be impossible to comply with, and it worked: for seventeen years, the city that produced grunge made it nearly illegal for the people who invented grunge to see a show. The burden fell hardest on **BIPOC youth and poor kids** who had no other venue. The TDO was finally replaced in **August 2002** by the All-Ages Dance Ordinance — written by a citizen Music and Youth Task Force, not by the city — which killed the insurance requirement and defined an all-ages event as legal so long as no alcohol was served.

**The lesson for the world:** all-ages space is not a nicety. It is the thing that had to be *fought for*, repeatedly, against a city that treated young people gathering to hear loud music as a public-safety threat. In a world that has abolished that logic, the all-ages room is the default, not the exception — and old people in the scene remember when it wasn't.

### The Vera Project: the model for a volunteer-run, youth-governed venue

**The Vera Project** (founded 2001 by Shannon Stewart, James Keblas, and Kate Becker; modeled explicitly on **VERA Groningen** in the Netherlands) is the closest real-world template for how this world's venues *actually run*. It is:

- **All-ages, always** — the tagline is literally "Always All Ages."
- **Volunteer-fueled and youth-*governed*** — teenagers don't just attend, they run the board, the door, the sound, and the governance.
- **A whole skill-transfer engine, not just a stage** — the space includes a **silkscreen print studio**, a **recording studio**, an art gallery, and hands-on training in **audio engineering, event production, and leadership.**

Its first show (Jan 27, 2001, at IBEW Local 46 — a *union electricians' hall*, note the salvaged-civic-space instinct) drew nearly 1,000 people for The Murder City Devils, Botch, and The Blood Brothers. It now lives at Seattle Center with a 362-cap room.

**The lesson:** a venue in this world is a place where you *learn to make the thing*, and the sixteen-year-old running the soundboard tonight booked the show and screenprinted the poster. Clout doesn't get you on stage; showing up for load-in does.

### Grunge's roots were all-ages rooms, and then they weren't

Before it was a magazine cover, grunge was **all-ages bills of four bands** at places like the **OK Hotel** (Pioneer Square, 212 Alaskan Way S; where Nirvana debuted "Smells Like Teen Spirit" on April 17, 1991) and the **Off Ramp** (now El Corazon; Pearl Jam's debut as Mookie Blaylock). The structure mattered: four-band bills meant a brand-new group could get stage time under a bigger draw, and because the rooms were all-ages, the *audience* was the same kids who'd start the next band. The OK Hotel dropped all-ages status in fall 1994 when it added liquor — the exact trade-off the TDO forced on every venue, and the exact trade-off this world refuses.

### Olympia, riot grrrl, and the zine as organizing tool

An hour south, **Olympia** built the template for scene-as-movement. **K Records** (founded by KAOS-FM DJ **Calvin Johnson**) and its **International Pop Underground Convention** (April 1991) proved a town of 40,000 could be a capital. Out of the same soil came **riot grrrl** — women fed up with getting shoved to the back of the pit started **loud bands and their own zines** in the same breath. **Bikini Kill** (formed Oct 1990; Kathleen Hanna's band), **Bratmobile** (Molly Neuman, Allison Wolfe), and the zines *Bikini Kill*, *Jigsaw*, and *Girl Germs* were not a marketing apparatus around the music — **the zine and the band were the same gesture.** **Kill Rock Stars** put it on wax with a DIY-distribution ethic. The movement's signature move — Hanna asking **"girls to the front"** — is a spatial politics: it's about who gets to stand where in the room.

**The lesson:** in this world, making a zine, forming a band, and reorganizing who's allowed near the stage are one continuous act, and none of them require anyone's permission.

## 2. Punk's DIY infrastructure: the actual machinery of a self-organizing scene

Punk's most durable invention wasn't a sound; it was a **distributed, permissionless logistics network** for getting a band from one town to the next and keeping everyone fed and housed along the way. Every piece of it maps directly onto this world's ethics.

- **The zine as the pre-internet Weave.** Photocopied, hand-stapled, mailed. Zines listed shows, reviewed records, ran interviews, and — crucially — **carried the addresses and contacts** that made touring possible. Some came with a **cassette sampler** stapled in.
- **Book Your Own Fuckin' Life (BYOFL).** Started in the early 1990s (out of *Maximum Rocknroll*), this was a **crowdsourced, community-maintained booking directory** — a printed database of venues, promoters, bands, radio stations, record stores, and floors to sleep on, city by city. It formalized a decade-old informal favor economy: *a band who'd played a town passed the venue contact and the crash-pad address to the next band coming through.* This is the Weave's ancestor almost exactly — un-ranked, un-metered, built entirely of "here's who to talk to."
- **House shows and salvaged civic space.** Basements, VFW halls, union halls, living rooms with the couch shoved against the wall. The PA is borrowed. The "stage" is the floor. **ABC No Rio** in NYC (Saturday punk matinees) is the canonical model of the multi-use space: venue + silkscreen shop + darkroom + **zine library** + **communal kitchen for mutual aid.**
- **Food Not Bombs.** Started 1983, now in 1,000+ cities; a loose federation of autonomous collectives that recover food that would be wasted and cook **free vegan meals in public.** Structurally punk-adjacent and structurally *identical to this world's gift layer* — abundance recovered and handed out, no means test, no register. Shows and FNB tables share personnel and politics constantly.
- **Sliding-scale / "no one turned away for lack of funds" (NOTAFLOF).** The door is pay-what-you-can; the touring band still gets gas money because the local scene passes the hat. Money is thin and boring; the hospitality is thick.

**The lesson:** every logistical function a music industry normally performs — discovery, booking, distribution, lodging, promotion — punk already rebuilt as a *favor network among peers with no central authority.* That is precisely the Weave's architecture applied to sound.

*Sources: [Wikipedia — DIY punk ethic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DIY_punk_ethic); [Wikipedia — Food Not Bombs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_Not_Bombs); [Anarchist Federation — ABC No Rio benefit](https://www.anarchistfederation.net/endless-fest-gathers-26-bands-across-three-generations-of-nyc-hardcore-to-benefit-abc-no-rio); [The Revelator — Punk Builds a Greener Future](https://therevelator.org/punk-builds-greener-future/); [FāVS — Spokane DIY benefit shows](https://favs.news/spokane-diy-punk-benefit-concerts-support-local/).*

## 3. Disco, house, and techno: dance music as a Black and queer liberation technology

The dance floor in this world is not decoration. Historically, **house music was engineered as a survival space** by exactly the people this world routes toward.

- **The Warehouse, Chicago (opened 1977** by promoter Robert Williams), gave "house" its name and was a **haven specifically for queer Black and Latino dancers.** Resident DJ **Frankie Knuckles** — a gay Black DJ from the Bronx — built the genre by splicing disco breaks on a reel-to-reel and layering drum machines under it. House is, in the words of one scholar, a sound "born in the post-industrial **maroon spaces** of Chicago's queer, Black, and Latino social dancers" — *maroon* as in the self-liberated communities of escaped enslaved people. That etymology is the whole point.
- **The Paradise Garage, NYC (1977–1987),** with **Larry Levan** on the decks, was the East Coast counterpart — a members' club, largely Black and gay, where the crowd and the DJ built the night together over hours. "Garage" as a genre is named for this room.
- **The timing is not incidental:** house and ballroom both crested **during the peak of the AIDS crisis.** These were rooms full of people who were being fired, evicted, and buried, dancing in the one place that wouldn't throw them out — which is why the music is simultaneously euphoric and grief-soaked, and why the DJ's job was closer to a **pastor's** than an entertainer's.

**The lesson:** a great night on a dance floor in this world carries the memory that this form was *built by the dying to keep each other alive*. It's ecstatic because it had to be. The DJ tends grief and joy in the same set.

*Sources: [Wikipedia — The Warehouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warehouse_(nightclub)); [ROLI — LGBTQIA+ roots of House](https://roli.com/blog/the-lgbtqia-roots-of-house-dance-musics-most-liberating-genre); [Grayarea — 10 LGBTQ+ artists who shaped house](https://grayarea.co/magazine/10-lgbtq-artists-who-indelibly-shaped-the-house-music-landscape); [Google Arts & Culture — House Music](https://artsandculture.google.com/story/house-music-a-genre-becomes-a-movement-groove/1gVxpBTZUoQ2CA).*

## 4. Ballroom and the house system: chosen family as institution

**Ballroom culture** is the most complete real-world model of **music-and-performance-as-mutual-aid-infrastructure**, and it should be a structural load-bearing element of this world, not a costume.

- **Roots:** Harlem drag balls trace back to the **Hamilton Lodge (balls possibly as early as 1869)**; the modern **house ballroom** scene coalesced in Harlem in the **late 1960s–70s**, a Black and Latinx LGBTQ+ formation that split off when white-dominated pageants kept rigging against queens of color.
- **The house = a family, not a fan club.** Houses are led by a **house mother** and/or **house father** who "guide and groom" their **children** — many of whom were **thrown out by their birth families** for being queer or trans. A house provides **mentorship, housing, food, discipline, and belonging.** It competes at balls (in **categories** — voguing, realness, runway) but its first job is *keeping its kids alive and housed.*
- **Care networks the mainstream ignored.** Ballroom built **mutual aid and HIV/health advocacy** during a period when gay-rights orgs largely ignored poor Black and brown trans people. **Voguing** itself — the stylized dance born in the Harlem clubs of the late '80s — is the competitive/expressive face of a deep welfare system.

**The lesson:** the "house" is exactly this world's answer to how a scene *literally houses and raises* the kid who shows up alone. It is a chosen family with a name, a lineage, a reputation earned by care, and a place to sleep. The band girl arriving from a flat interior town is, structurally, a **ballroom child** — and someone should already be her mother.

*Sources: [Britannica — Ball culture](https://www.britannica.com/topic/ball-culture); [HISTORY — drag balls to house ballroom / voguing](https://www.history.com/articles/drag-balls-house-ballroom-voguing); [TIME — ballroom's radical freedoms](https://time.com/5941822/ballroom-voguing-queer-black-culture-renaissance/); [Grunge — Harlem ballroom & the Black/Latinx LGBTQ+ community](https://www.grunge.com/1308748/why-harlem-ballroom-scene-important-black-lantinx-lgbtq-community/).*

## 5. Queer nightlife as community-formation: how isolated people found each other

Before apps, the gay bar / lesbian bar / queer club was **the only reliable address** where an isolated person could walk in and discover they were not the only one. Seattle's Capitol Hill has real, still-living examples:

- **The Wildrose** (opened 1985 by five women who wanted a bar "where women would feel comfortable") — one of the **last remaining lesbian bars in the entire country**, which is itself a data point about how fragile these rooms are under market pressure.
- **Neighbours** (1983) — Seattle's oldest operating gay dance club; multi-room, drag, themed nights.
- **Pony** — a tiny queer dive built into the bones of a **1930s gas station**, a block that was literally slated for demolition; it draws the alternative/punk-adjacent queers who don't fit the polished bars. (Note the salvaged-structure instinct again — a gas station repurposed as a queer sanctuary is *extremely* on-theme for this world.)
- **Kremwerk** and others as the space for new drag and electronic subcultures.

The through-line: these are **address-based sanctuaries.** You don't find them by searching; someone *tells* you where to go, or you follow the bassline. That word-of-mouth, address-passed-hand-to-hand discovery is exactly the Weave's logic — and the Weave's canonical purpose (*is anyone like me, is anyone out there → you will not be the only one*) is the **gay bar's function, generalized to the whole networked earth.**

*Sources: [The Ticket / Seattle Times — Queer bars in Seattle](https://theticket.seattletimes.com/city-guides/queer-bars-in-seattle/); [PUNCH — Seattle's queer bars](https://punchdrink.com/articles/seattle-queer-cocktails-tour/).*

## 6. Seattle's own mutual-aid-out-of-grief precedent: Home Alive

This one is specific to Seattle and belongs in the world's marrow. In **July 1993**, **Mia Zapata**, singer of the punk band **The Gits**, was raped and murdered in Seattle. In response, **nine women from the music scene** founded **Home Alive** — a collective offering **affordable, sliding-scale (originally free) self-defense classes**, education, and grassroots anti-violence work, funded partly by benefit compilation records featuring the scene's bigger names. It ran as a nonprofit and then a volunteer collective until 2010.

**The lesson:** this is the template for how a scene *metabolizes violence and loss into infrastructure* — not a candlelight vigil that ends, but a standing, sliding-scale institution the community builds and keeps running. In this world, when something terrible happens to one of their own, a scene doesn't just mourn; it *builds a thing that stays.*

*Sources: [Wikipedia — Home Alive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Home_Alive); [Seattle Times archive — Home Alive benefit CD](https://archive.seattletimes.com/archive/?date=19960301&slug=2316673); [These Streets — Mia Zapata](https://thesestreets.org/mia-zapata/).*

## 7. How scenes value music *without* metrics (this is the Three Refusals in practice)

The First Refusal — **no attention-metrics** — has a rich real-world precedent. DIY music economies have always valued work by means that **can't be reduced to a number**:

- **The cassette as cultural currency.** Tapes were **traded by mail**, passed hand to hand, dubbed and re-dubbed. A tape's worth was measured in *who gave it to you* and *how far it traveled*, not in plays. Zines circulated **photocopied catalogues** and stapled-in samplers. Value was **relational and material**, not tallied.
- **The mixtape as a love letter.** A mixtape is authored *for one person* — the ultimate un-scalable, un-metered artifact. It cannot be ranked because it means nothing outside the specific relationship it was made inside.
- **Word of mouth and the flyer.** Discovery ran on **flyers stapled to poles, the record-store clerk's recommendation, and someone grabbing your arm** — "you HAVE to see this band." No algorithm; a *person* vouching, staking a bit of their own credibility.
- **The favor economy of tape trading and BYOFL** — reputation is built by **reliability and generosity** (did you send the tape you promised? did you put the touring band up?), not by follower count.

**The lesson:** this world already has a fully-worked-out vocabulary for esteem that isn't a scoreboard. A band is "good" because **the right person handed you their tape**, because **the room was sweaty**, because **they always pay the touring band**, because **their mother-house takes in strays.** Worth is witnessed, spoken, and remembered — never counted.

*Sources: [Wikipedia — Cassette culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cassette_culture); [CassettePlayer.net — cassettes & punk](https://cassetteplayer.net/the-influence-of-cassettes-on-the-punk-rock-movement/); [Limn — A Mix for the Ages](https://limn.press/article/a-mix-for-the-ages/).*

## Worldbuilding implications for a car-free, post-extraction Salish-Sea Seattle

Concrete, usable, sweaty details for the fiction writer. Pick and steal freely.

1. **What a show is, physically.** A basement or a salvaged room — a de-commissioned electrical substation, the second floor of a mussel-processing shed, the back of a floating garden barge. The PA is borrowed and everyone knows whose it is. The "stage" is a taped square on the floor, or no square at all. It is **hot** — bodies close, the windows fogged, someone props the fire door with a brick and the cool off the Sound comes in with the moths. You got here by **flagging a tram or walking**, so shows cluster near where people already are, and they **end in time to walk home**, or they don't end and you sleep on the floor. Load-in is done by whoever's around; if you came, you might carry a cab.

2. **The door is not a transaction.** There's a jar or a hat, marked *pay what you can, no one turned away, the touring band eats first.* Money is thin and boring here, so the jar is half coins and half other things — a jar of preserved lemons, a repaired amp cable, an IOU for a haircut. The **touring band still gets gas-equivalent** (a full water-tank, a rail chit, a berth on the ferry) because the scene passes the hat and because someone's house is feeding them tonight.

3. **How the band girl's band actually works.** Nobody in it is "discovered." They **rehearse in a shared, soundproofed commons** you sign up for on a chalk sheet (she's late because the tram she flagged already had a cello in it and no room). They **screenprint their own poster** in the venue's print studio and **wheatpaste it to the moss-softened poles** along the tram lines. They **cut a short run of cassettes or a hand-labeled minidisc** and *give them away*, and the tapes' whole life is being **handed person to person and mailed out on the Weave** — a kid in a flat interior town gets one in a padded envelope with a knit hat, the way she once did. There is **no chart, no play-count, no way to know how far it went** — which is exactly why the envelope coming back with a reply is so heavy.

4. **The venue is a whole apparatus, not a stage.** Like Vera and ABC No Rio: the room where you see the show is also the **silkscreen studio, the recording booth, the zine library, the darkroom, and the communal kitchen** where the Food-Not-Bombs-descended free meal is cooking. The **sixteen-year-old running the soundboard booked the show and runs the governance meeting.** You don't get on stage by being liked online; you get on stage by **showing up for load-in and door shifts** until the room trusts you.

5. **Houses raise the strays.** The **ballroom "house" model is literal social infrastructure.** A newly-arrived kid isn't a fan; she's a *child of a house* within a week — a named lineage with a **mother** who makes sure she eats, sleeps somewhere safe, learns to splice a cable and hem a costume, and has somewhere to be when she's spiraling. Houses **compete** (at balls, at battle-of-the-bands nights, at the shoreline solstice throw-down) but their first duty is **keeping their kids housed and alive.** Reputation of a house = *how well it cares*, spoken and remembered, never scored.

6. **The dance floor remembers its dead.** House/techno/disco nights are understood as **inherited from the maroon spaces of dying queer people who danced to stay alive.** So a night has a shape: euphoria *and* grief in the same set, a DJ who works like a pastor, a moment late in the night where the room holds still for someone no longer there before the beat drops back in. Ecstatic because it had to be.

7. **Grief becomes standing infrastructure (the Home Alive rule).** When violence or loss hits the scene, they don't just hold a vigil that ends — they **build a thing that stays**: a sliding-scale self-defense collective, a walk-you-home network, a fund, a room named for the person. The benefit show isn't charity; it's the community **capitalizing its own institution** with the only currency it fully controls — a sweaty night and a passed hat.

8. **Discovery is a person grabbing your arm.** No feed surfaces the band. Someone **hands you a tape**, or the person mending nets next to you says *you have to be at the substation Thursday*, or a flyer on a mossy pole catches you. The **record-store clerk / instrument-library keeper** is a real node of taste and vouching. To vouch is to **stake a little of your own standing** — which is why it means something.

9. **All-ages is the default and the elders remember when it wasn't.** There is no equivalent of the Teen Dance Ordinance — but the **oldest people in the scene tell the story** of when the city made it nearly illegal for young people to gather and hear loud music, treating a room full of kids as a threat. It's a ghost story they tell to explain why **the door stays open to the fifteen-year-old**, why the room is theirs to run, why "always all ages" is a value people fought and died a little for.

10. **The Weave is the zine and the gay bar, generalized.** A thread sent out (*is anyone like me?*) is doing exactly what the mailed cassette, the BYOFL directory, the flyer, and the address of the one gay bar in town always did — **routing an isolated person toward the room where they're not the only one.** It comes back slow and heavy: an address in a green city, a tape, a knit hat, a house that will take you.

## Open questions for the next contributor

1. **What does this world's music actually *sound* like?** We have the social architecture; we don't have a genre. What happens to sound itself in a car-free, salvaged-material, bioregional city — do the instruments change (tuned to the drone of the Sound, built from salvage), do new hybrid forms emerge from the collision of riot-grrrl punk, house, ballroom, and whatever the flat-interior kids bring in? Someone should invent one specific band and one specific song.

2. **How does recorded music circulate un-metered without recreating scarcity or a black market?** Cassettes/tapes worked partly because dubbing was easy and value was relational. In a high-tech post-slop world, what's the physical or Weave-native artifact that carries a recording *without* a play-count sneaking back in? What stops a reputation-economy from just becoming a slower leaderboard?

3. **Give the band girl's house a name, a mother, and a lineage.** Who took her in? What's the house called, what does it fight over, who did it lose? (Ties directly to the open "band girl" thread in CANON.)

4. **What is the balance between the euphoric-dance strand and the punk/loud strand in *this* city** — are they the same scene sharing rooms, or two neighborhoods eyeing each other? Where do they collide (a night? a feud? a shared benefit)?

5. **Who are the people the scene *fails*?** A utopia stays credible by admitting its edges. Who falls through the house system, who gets frozen out of a room, what happens when a beloved venue-parent turns out to be a creep — and how does a no-central-authority scene handle accountability without a boss or a metric to appeal to?
