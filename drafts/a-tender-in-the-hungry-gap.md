# A Tender in the hungry gap

**Overheard, Central District, a cold March morning —**

Yael walks the six blocks of their *consultorio* the way you walk a known thing — no map, no list, just the rhythm of who needs what and when, held in the body the way you hold the weather. They're a **Tender**: neighborhood health worker for the ~600 people in this cluster, which is fourteen row-houses, the co-op above the ferment market, and the stretch of floating docks where the House of Marrow and six other households live on the water. Home visits on foot. Paper family-charts kept in the neighborhood clinic, a soft-cornered mycelium-and-salvaged-timber corner house you could walk past without noticing except for the kettle always on and the hand-painted sign of who works here.

This is the **hungry gap** — the lean weeks between the winter stores running thin and the first spring greens coming up, when the body is tired and a cold can settle into something worse. Yael has been a Tender for four years. They know the gap by name.

First call: Mr. Okafor, seventy-one, the man who fixed bicycles for forty years and whose knees are done now. Yael knocks the particular knock — two, pause, one — that Mr. Okafor asked for because the surprise knocks scare him since the displacement years. He opens the door already smiling because he knows the knock. That's **access intimacy** `[DEMONSTRATED · draft]` — Mia Mingus's term for the thing that happens when someone *gets* your access needs without your having to explain them from scratch every time. Yael doesn't ask how he's feeling. They ask what he ate yesterday, because Mr. Okafor will say "fine, fine" to spare you and will tell the plain truth about food.

"The lentil stew Tuesday sent over," he says. "Good and plenty."

Yael checks his blood pressure — the cuff and the stethoscope are 1970s durable, the kind of tech that *lasts* because planned obsolescence is a before-times horror this world refuses to repeat `[DEPLOYED · draft]`. Numbers are fine. The chart gets a note: *BP stable, eating well, knees holding. No new pain. Check back Thursday.* Mr. Okafor offers tea. Yael takes it, because the tea is part of the visit — the part where Mr. Okafor feels he gave something back, which matters more than the blood pressure.

Second call: the Vargas household, where the baby is six weeks old and the older parent — who carried — has been tired in a way that isn't resolving. Yael climbs the stairs with a jar of the ferment cellar's best sauerkraut (the vitamin C that smoking and drying destroy, kept alive through the hungry gap by the old, patient lacto-fermentation this bioregion re-learned when the supermarket supply chains became untenable `[DEPLOYED · draft]`). The parent opens the door holding the baby and looking like they haven't slept more than two hours at a stretch in weeks, which is true.

Yael doesn't lecture. They sit. They ask the questions the **Cuban consultorio model** taught the world to ask: *Who's helping? When did you last eat a whole meal sitting down? How's your bleeding?* `[DEPLOYED · draft]` The answers are: nobody since Tuesday Marrow's last visit, yesterday but standing over the sink, and heavier than it should be at six weeks.

Yael makes a note for the midwife to come today — not a referral, not a form, just a thread that will walk across the Weave or by a knock on the midwife's door, whichever is faster. And then Yael makes a second note, this one for the **House of Marrow**, because that House keeps a **care web** `[DEMONSTRATED · draft]` — the self-organized cluster of people (disabled and not, parents and not) who provide each other care as a collective responsibility, not a favor you have to beg for. By dinnertime, someone from the House will bring a hot meal and hold the baby while the parent sleeps. That web exists because the world decided care is not a private problem you solve with money you may not have; it's infrastructure, like the ferry.

Third call: the person Yael does not name in the chart by their old name, because they came to the clinic last fall and said their name was River now, and the paper chart was updated in one pencil stroke, and that was the whole entire bureaucracy of it. In the before-times, changing a name in a medical record could take months, a court order, a doctor's letter, a fight `[DEPLOYED · draft]`. Here it took a sentence and a pencil. River needed testosterone and got it under the **informed-consent model** — no gatekeeping panel, no proof-of-transness trial, just a conversation about what it does to a body and a signature `[DEPLOYED · draft]`. Yael checks in because River mentioned last visit that the night sweats were bad. They're better now. River is smiling more. The visit is five minutes. That's plenty.

Yael finishes the morning round and walks back to the clinic, where the **rest-ledger** is kept.

The rest-ledger is how this world honors care labor without turning it into a metric against the person. It is an openly-kept record of the rest-days the neighborhood owes Yael — not *Yael's productivity score*, not *how many visits Yael did*, but *how much rest the community has promised Yael and not yet given*. It is a **debt the commons carries**, not a number that ranks Yael's worth `[CANON · draft]`. The First Refusal holds: count the rainfall, not the soul.

But here is the knot this world will not tie off:

Yael has been working six days a week since January because the hungry gap is hard and people get sick and there is no one else. The ledger says the neighborhood owes them eleven rest-days. Eleven. Everyone knows it. And everyone also knows that if Yael takes those days *now*, Mr. Okafor's blood pressure won't get checked, and the Vargas household will be alone, and the winter cold working through the damp houseboats will find someone without margin.

So who decides? The neighborhood assembly meets monthly and is *supposed* to re-balance the load — find another Tender-in-training, redistribute the panel, call in a neighboring consultorio's help. But the assembly is made of tired people in the hungry gap too, and good intentions are not the same as capacity. And even if they find the help, someone still has to decide: is eleven days enough? Should it be fifteen? Twenty? How much rest is *owed* before we say a person is rested enough and may work again?

That is a **judgment about a person's needs**. It is the thing the First Refusal exists to forbid. And the rest-ledger, for all its honesty, cannot escape making it.

The world does not pretend to resolve this. It keeps the ledger open, and the debt visible, and the assembly responsible for it — which is better than the before-times market that made care workers invisible and disposable. But "better than the market" is not the same as "solved," and this world refuses to lie about the difference.

Yael puts the chart away, makes a note in the ledger — *six visits today, owed 11.5 rest-days now* — and goes to the Tuesday assembly, because the only thing worse than a hard problem kept visible is a hard problem you pretend isn't there.
