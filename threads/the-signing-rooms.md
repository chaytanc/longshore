**Overheard, north of the ferment market, a Tuesday in the hungry gap —**

Del plays the last set at the Vera room with her back to the amp now. She learned that this spring: the ringing comes anyway, but slower, if the sound hits her spine before her ears. After, on the boards outside, Tuesday Marrow doesn't ask how the show was. She asks what Del can still hear of it. That's a house-mother's question — the House of Marrow keeps the ones the rooms are costing.

The rooms are costing her. All-ages rooms are the thing that saved her — she found Dryland, and a reason to leave the flat interior town, in a room fought into legal existence the way such rooms always were `[DEPLOYED · RND-051]` — and the same rooms are 100-plus decibels an hour, and her audiograms slope the way a working musician's do `[DEPLOYED · RND-052]`. The world that starved the cars could not repeal physics. Loud is still loud.

Here is what this world does that the before-times mostly didn't: it does not treat her ears as a private accident to be privately fixed. When the tender flagged the slope, the Weave carried a thread — not a scroll, not a feed, just a thread that came back heavier for the wait — and three days later a carrier put a locked drawer's contents in her hand: an invitation from a **signing district**, one of the neighborhoods built bilingual in sign, to DeafSpace defaults, where congenital deafness is not a disability and never was `[DEMONSTRATED · RND-053]`. Difference is not tolerated here; it is a resource the world routes toward. So it routed toward her.

She went angry. She did not want a new country. She wanted her old ears.

What she found was not a cure and the district did not pretend to sell her one. There is hearing tech — there was always hearing tech `[DEPLOYED · RND-054]` — and the Deaf houses hold a real, unfinished argument about it that the world keeps open instead of closing: a tool for the one who wants back into the hearing rooms, and, to some, a quiet claim that the way they already live is a lack to be corrected `[DEPLOYED · RND-055]`. Nobody in the signing district decided that argument for her. They handed her the argument whole, which is a different kind of respect than an answer.

What the district gave her that the Vera room can't: a room where the bass lives in the floor on purpose, where the light does the work sound does elsewhere, where she is not a musician going deaf but a person among people, some of whom have never once thought of themselves as broken. A House there — signing, chosen-family, standing to sign for care like any House `[DEMONSTRATED · RND-056]` — left a door open. Two house-mothers, Marrow and the other, will let her belong to both if she wants; Houses don't compete for a child.

The tension the world will not tie off for her: she is not done playing, and playing is still deafening her. She can turn her back to the amp. She can learn to read the set through the floor and her band's hands. She can move, someday, to writing songs she'll feel more than hear. Or she can spend the hearing she has left on the rooms that gave her a life and pay the whole bill knowingly — and the world, which refuses to aestheticize either sacrifice or safety, will not tell her which is the brave one. It will only make sure that whichever she chooses, she is not choosing it alone, and not choosing it broke, and not choosing it silenced by a market that would rather she couldn't afford the question.

Tuesday Marrow's actual advice, on the boards, was one line: *don't let anyone — not the rooms, not the district, not me — tell you your ears are a debt you owe somebody.* Then she walked Del home the slow way, which in this city is not a tax.
