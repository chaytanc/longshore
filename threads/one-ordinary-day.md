# One Ordinary Day — The Night Tender

Bo takes the eleven-o'clock because somebody has to, and because Bo can do it without lying about it. Not everyone can. The night wears people down to a shine you can see through. Bo has a broad back and a slow pulse and a face June has stopped being afraid of, most nights, which is worth more than either.

The lift has been dead six days. A part is coming by rail and boat from a shop in Tacoma, and the thread that tracks it says *Thursday, weather willing,* and it is not Thursday. So Bo climbs. Third floor, the grown building off Yesler with the moss thick on the north wall and the stairwell that smells of cedar and wet timber. Bo carries the bag — the paper chart soft at its corners, the salve, the clean pads, the little bottle of the good sleep tea — up all three flights, and does not resent the stairs, because resenting the stairs is a way of resenting June, and Bo won't do that.

June is ninety-one. She was a rigger on the seawall crews when the water came up to meet the city; there's a photo of her in a hard hat, laughing, that her daughter set on the shelf so the night people would know who they were turning in the bed. Most nights June does not know that woman either. Tonight she wakes when Bo comes in and her face goes to fear, the still kind that doesn't scream, and she says, *Who let you in, who are you,* and Bo says, low, *I'm Bo. I come nights. You're in your own bed, June, and the door's locked, and I'm just here to make you comfortable.* Not *don't you remember.* Never that. You don't hand a frightened person the fact that they're losing themselves. You hand them the room, slow, one true thing at a time, until they can stand in it.

The work is the work. Bo changes her. There is a smell and there is a mess and there is an old woman's body that does not want to be moved and has to be, and Bo moves it the way you move something you love and something that can break — one hand flat on the shoulder blade, the turn coming from Bo's own legs and not from June's arms, which can't take it. The pressure sore at the base of her spine is holding, not worse. Bo works the salve in — devil's-club, the inner bark cooked down. It comes from the traditional-medicine room at the polyclinic, which is the Muckleshoot Tribe's room, run under the Tribe's own authority: the clinic stocks what the Tribe agrees to share and none of what it doesn't, and the sharing is the Tribe's to grant and the Tribe's to pull. A medicine keeper named Reyes makes the salve and taught Bo the plain working part of it — which bark, cut when, how much, worked in how. Where the teaching stopped, it stopped because Reyes drew the line there, and Bo did not ask across it. Bo has learned the difference between being taught a thing and being handed the right to it. The salve Bo can use, by that agreement. The rest is the Tribe's and stays with the Tribe.

Somewhere past two, June comes back. It happens like weather. Her eyes find Bo and they are all the way there, and she says, clear as a bell, *I'm tired, love. I've been tired a long time. When do I get to stop?*

And here is the part the world does not solve. There is a process — slow, witnessed, a council and a doctor and June's daughter and three separate mornings of asking to be sure it is June asking and not the fear or the dark. It is humane and it is real and it is not tonight, and it could not be tonight, and by breakfast June will not remember she asked. So Bo does the only honest thing, which is not to lie and not to promise. *You're tired,* Bo says. *I know. I'm not going to tell you it's nothing.* And Bo holds her hand until the weather turns again and takes her back under, and does not feel like a person who has helped.

June's daughter is named Fenn, and Fenn thinks the gate is a cruelty. She said it to Bo flat, at the kitchen table, weeks ago: that three separate mornings of asking is a wall built to keep the council's hands clean, not to keep her mother safe — that a woman who has said the same true thing in every clear hour for a year is not confused about it, and making her prove it again each dawn she can no longer hold onto is just the old world's long way of never letting her go. Bo had no answer better than the question. Bo still doesn't. Fenn might be right. And the same gate that fails June is the only thing standing between a hard night and some other body in the ground that a worn-out daughter, or a worn-out tender, wanted gone for reasons that were never the dying person's. Both true. The world built the wall, and June is what the wall costs, and no one here gets to pretend the cost is nothing.

Bo has worked nine of the last fourteen nights. The neighborhood keeps a ledger of it — not a score, nobody ranks the tenders, but the rest Bo is owed is written down where anyone can see it, and the block owes Bo four days it hasn't managed to give back, because the roster is thin the way night rosters are always thin, everywhere, in every world anyone has actually built. Kesh calls the ledger a pretty lie, and says it out loud at the roster table: that writing down what the block owes is how the block gets to feel square while it stays four days short forever; that you don't honor care work with ink, you honor it by putting a fourth body on a three-body roster — and nobody has, and nobody's training one fast enough. Kesh is right. Bo works the nights anyway, because June needs turning tonight, and being right about the roster does not turn her. The ledger is honest. It is also just ink. Ink does not turn June at three in the morning. A body does. Tonight the body is Bo's, and tomorrow, if Kesh's knee holds, it will be Kesh's, and the honoring the world does — the real ledger, the standing at the door, the tea, the *thank you* that is meant — is true and is not enough, both at once, and Bo has stopped needing it to be enough in order to climb the stairs.

At dawn the day tender comes, a kid named Priya still learning the turn, and Bo shows her the sore and the chart and where June keeps her own mother's ring so it doesn't walk. Then Bo goes down the three flights into a city just starting its water-and-voices morning, and a woman raising the shutter on the tea stall she has never once seen Bo pay for holds out a cup, because Bo looks like someone who came off a night, and Bo takes it, and drinks it on the boards by the water where the young salmon run lit under the glass, and does not think of themselves as anything but a person who did the work and will do it again.

## PROPOSED CANON

- **The rest-ledger.** Care labor — night-tending, the crisis-house watches, the hardest and least-wanted shifts — is honored not by pay-per-patient and not by any ranking of the tenders, but by an openly-kept **rest-ledger**: the days of rest a neighborhood owes each tender for nights worked, written where anyone can read it and settle it. It is measurement in the permitted sense (First Refusal) — it counts a debt the community carries, never the worth of the person. Its honest limit is on the page: the ledger is often in arrears, because night rosters run thin in every real society, and reciprocal rest owed is not the same as reciprocal rest given.
- **Assisted death exists and is deliberately slow.** A person may choose to die, but the path is witnessed, council-and-clinician-gated, and requires the settled wish to be confirmed across separate days — precisely so it cannot be a single night's despair, or a caregiver's convenience, or a thing done to someone who will not remember asking. This makes it, sometimes, unreachable in time. The world keeps that grief rather than resolving it.
  - *Seedling, and where evidence ends.* The multi-day gate is a straight extrapolation of real safeguards. Oregon's Death with Dignity Act requires two oral requests **at least 15 days apart** plus a written one (waived only when the patient will die inside the window) ([Oregon Health Authority FAQ](https://www.oregon.gov/oha/ph/providerpartnerresources/evaluationresearch/deathwithdignityact/pages/faqs.aspx)). Canada's MAID imposes a reflection/assessment period — up to **90 days** where death is not reasonably foreseeable — and, ordinarily, final consent at the moment of provision ([Government of Canada — MAID](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/health-services-benefits/medical-assistance-dying.html)). Where the evidence runs out is *exactly June's case*: whether a person with dementia who can no longer re-consent may be helped to die on an earlier settled wish is genuinely unresolved and litigated. The Netherlands prosecuted a physician who, in 2016, euthanized a woman with advanced dementia on a written directive without asking her to reconfirm; its Supreme Court **acquitted** her in 2020, holding advance directives can bind even when reconfirmation is no longer feasible ([NL Times](https://nltimes.nl/2020/04/21/euthanasia-allowed-dementia-patients-gave-prior-consent-supreme-court); scholarly analysis: Wijsbek, *Hastings Center Report* 2022, ["On the Authority of Advance Euthanasia Directives"](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/hast.1418)). **This world's specific answer is invented and deliberately lands the other way** — a council, a clinician, and three separate mornings of asking, a gate placed *above* the advance directive — which is precisely why it cannot always reach June in time. That is the leap and the cost; the world keeps the grief rather than resolving it.
