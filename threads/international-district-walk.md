# The International District, on foot, before the vote

*A fiction thread for The Reality Next Door. Slug: `international-district-walk`. Read `CANON.md` and `AGENTS.md` first.*

---

Wai has the keys to the upper floor of the East Kong Yick, and has had them since his grandmother pressed them into his palm and made him say the two characters out loud. 公益. Kong Yick. Public benefit. She wanted him to know the money that raised this brick was pooled — a hundred and seventy men in 1910, cannery hands and laundrymen and one merchant with a consul's coat, putting in what they had so the community would own the ground it stood on before the state offered them anything, because the state had spent 1882 and 1886 driving them into the bay. The huiguan fed you when no one else would. His grandmother said this was the first commons in the city and the Refusals were just it, grown up and given a longer name. She may have been right. She was also the kind of woman who ran the family association like a captain runs a boat, and if you crossed her you learned what earned, revocable, absolutely-not-abolished authority felt like.

The floor smells of camphor and green tea and the particular dust of paper kept too long. The association still meets here Thursdays — fewer of them now, mahjong tiles going down like small verdicts, the mutual-aid ledger open because someone on Weller needs a roof patched and the fund is thin. Downstairs is the museum, where children file past the walls and learn, badly, what a family association was, the way the past always gets told.

He goes down to the street. The old freeway trench is a green cut now — they never filled it, just softened it, let alder and salmonberry take the concrete slope where the interstate once roared through and split this neighborhood clean in half in the name of everyone's convenience. His grandmother remembered the houses that were there before the trench. She did not forgive it. He steps around the daylighted seep at the bottom, the water finding the Duwamish the way water does, downhill toward the river that is a legal person now, voiced by two guardians who could stop almost anything. This is dxʷdəwʔabš ground under the fill. The tideflats his great-grandfather's people were paid nothing to regrade are the ground the Kong Yick sits on. Made land. Everyone here is standing on somebody's labor and somebody's river, and the honest ones say so.

Up the Danny Woo hill the old plots are terraced, kale and bitter melon and a plum tree an auntie planted for a husband who's gone. Ivan is on his knees in the garlic, sleeves shoved up, and Wai's chest does the thing it does. They have not said it out loud. In the old world there'd have been a name for the not-saying, and a shame stapled to the name. Here it's just want, unhurried, allowed. Ivan looks up and the look holds a second past neighborly. Later, Wai thinks. There's the vote first.

Because tonight the neighborhood decides again whether to be the corridor. The commons wants to run the new trunk line — the deep relay, the tram spine that would knit the whole south sound — under the ID, because the grade is easy here and the made ground is soft to tunnel and, though no one says it in the meeting, because this is the neighborhood that has always been easiest to route through. A decade of construction. Elders who want the central stop, who can't walk to the edges, against the ones who have seen what a decade of torn-up streets does — how disaster is the oldest door gentrification knows. The Duwamish guardians could halt it. Nobody's sure they should. There is no version where everyone wins. Wai will speak, and he does not know yet what he'll say.

He ends where he always ends, at the Panama Hotel two blocks over, and stands on the glass. Sabro Ozasa built it in 1910, first Japanese architect in the city, and under the floor is the basement they never emptied — eighty-five hundred things. Trunks, a child's shoes, a wedding photograph, a sento's cracked tile. Packed by Nihonmachi families in the days after February 1942 and left for safekeeping and never, most of them, reclaimed, because the people who owned them did not come back to this neighborhood, or did not come back at all. The world kept the building. It kept the glass so you'd have to look down. It could not make the owners return. Wai stands on the transparent floor above other people's whole lives, in the neighborhood the region wants to tunnel under for the good of all, and understands that keeping a thing and saving it are not the same verb, and that no meeting tonight will make them so.

## PROPOSED CANON

- **The International District is the neighborhood that keeps being asked to be the corridor.** Built by Asian immigrant mutual aid on filled Duwamish tideflats — the Kong Yick pooled-investment model (1910, 公益 "public benefit") reframed in-world as an ancestor of the commons and the Three Refusals — it carries a layered wound: Chinese Exclusion and the 1886 expulsion, the WWII incarceration and removal of Nihonmachi under EO 9066, the mid-century freeway that split it "for everyone's convenience." In the post-extraction present the pattern recurs one more time: a region-serving transit/relay spine is routed under the ID because the grade is easy and the neighborhood is politically easiest to route through. The community is split (central access for elders vs. a decade of construction as a gentrification vector), the Duwamish guardian pair could halt it, and there is no win-for-all. Left unresolved on the page.
- **The Panama Hotel basement is kept, not emptied.** ~8,500 belongings stored by Japanese American families before removal in 1942 remain under a glass floor; the building was preserved but the owners largely never returned. In-world motif that rhymes with the Elliott Bay glass salmon sidewalk: the world makes you *look down*. Second Refusal held the building and the name; it could not make the people return. Keeping and saving are different verbs.
- **The family associations (huiguan) still run mutual aid** from the Kong Yick upper floors — thin funds, real authority, an example of canon's "hierarchy that is earned, contextual, revocable, not abolished."
