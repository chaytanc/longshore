# Political Economy & Governance — how a post-extraction Salish coordinates itself

*A research brief for* The Reality Next Door. *Domain: how a decentralized, non-capitalist, non-authoritarian society actually coordinates — without a ruler and without a central feed. Honors the "never centralize" rule and the Three Refusals.*

The core design problem for this world is this: **coordination without a center.** Capitalism coordinates through price signals and accumulation; authoritarian states coordinate through command. Our world has refused both — no engine of extraction, no single throat to give the orders. So the interesting question is not "is this nice?" but "does it *scale*, and what breaks?" The good news for the writer is that we are not making this up from scratch. Humans have run large, durable, non-market, non-state coordination systems for centuries. Below is the real evidence, the real numbers, and — because credibility lives in the tradeoffs — the real failure modes.

## 1. Elinor Ostrom: the commons is not a tragedy, and polycentricity is how it works

Garrett Hardin's 1968 "Tragedy of the Commons" claimed shared resources inevitably get destroyed by self-interest, so you need either privatization or the state. Elinor Ostrom (first woman to win the Nobel in economics, 2009) spent decades showing this is empirically false. In *Governing the Commons* (1990) she documented common-pool resources — Swiss alpine pastures managed collectively since the 1200s, Spanish *huerta* irrigation systems (the Valencia *Tribunal de las Aguas* has met weekly for ~1,000 years), Japanese village forests, Philippine and Nepali irrigation — that were governed sustainably for centuries by their users, without markets or states running them.

She distilled **eight design principles** that durable commons share:

1. **Clearly defined boundaries** — who has rights to the resource, and what the resource's edges are.
2. **Congruence** between the rules and local conditions (and between what you put in and what you take out).
3. **Collective-choice arrangements** — the people affected by the rules can participate in changing them.
4. **Monitoring** — by monitors who are accountable to the users, or are the users themselves.
5. **Graduated sanctions** — first offense gets a warning, not a ban; punishment escalates only with repetition.
6. **Low-cost conflict-resolution** — accessible local forums to settle disputes fast and cheap.
7. **Minimal recognition of the right to organize** — higher authorities don't override the group's self-governance.
8. **Nested enterprises** — for large systems, govern in layers: small units nested inside larger ones.

Principle 8 is the load-bearing one for us. Ostrom's word for it is **polycentricity**: many centers of decision-making, formally independent, that coordinate through mutual adjustment rather than a top boss. Water, roads, and fire don't respect a neighborhood's boundary, so neighborhoods federate — but authority stays *nested*, not *hierarchical*. Nobody at the top. Problems get solved at the smallest scale that can actually solve them (a principle also called **subsidiarity**), and only the genuinely bigger problems climb to the bigger table.

**Failure modes to keep honest:** Ostrom herself insisted there is no panacea. Commons governance is fragile when boundaries are contested, when the group is too large to monitor each other, when outsiders can raid the resource, or when the state undercuts local rules. Polycentric systems can also produce **coordination gaps** (everyone assumes someone else owns the problem) and **free-riding on effort** — the meetings, the monitoring, the emotional labor of enforcement fall on the same conscientious few. This is real and worth dramatizing.

*Sources: Ostrom,* Governing the Commons *(1990); [Patterns of Commoning — Eight Design Principles](https://patternsofcommoning.org/uncategorized/eight-design-principles-for-successful-commons/); [Ostrom Workshop, Indiana University](https://ostromworkshop.indiana.edu/courses-teaching/teaching-tools/ostrom-design/index.html); [Carlisle & Gruby, "Polycentric Systems of Governance,"* Policy Studies Journal *(2019)](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/psj.12212).*

## 2. Doughnut & degrowth: what the economy is *for* when it isn't for growth

Our world is "post-extraction," which means it has answered a question our own era mostly ducks: what does an economy optimize for if not GDP growth?

**Kate Raworth's Doughnut Economics** gives the shape. Picture two rings. The inner ring is the **social foundation** — the minimum everyone needs: food, water, housing, health, energy, education, political voice, gender equality. Fall below it and people are in the hole. The outer ring is the **ecological ceiling** — nine planetary boundaries (climate, biodiversity, freshwater, nitrogen/phosphorus cycles, ocean acidification, etc.) past which the living systems break. The safe and just space for humanity is the doughnut between them. The goal is not *more* — it's *staying in the ring*: nobody short of essentials, nobody overshooting the planet.

This isn't only theory. **Amsterdam formally adopted the Doughnut as its policy compass in April 2020**, building a "City Portrait" and a circular-economy strategy targeting a 50% cut in new raw-material use by 2030 and a fully circular economy by 2050. Copenhagen, Brussels, and others followed. (Honest caveat: critics on the left, e.g. in *Jacobin*, argue the Doughnut describes the destination beautifully but is vague on how you dislodge the power of capital to get there — it's a compass, not a map of the fight.)

**Jason Hickel's degrowth** supplies the mechanism. His definition: *"a planned reduction of energy and resource use designed to bring the economy back into balance with the living world in a way that reduces inequality and improves human well-being."* Three points matter for the fiction:

- It is **selective**, not a general shrinking — you scale down destructive and unnecessary sectors (fossil fuels, fast fashion, planned obsolescence, advertising, the military, private jets) while scaling *up* care, health, renewables, public goods.
- It is **planned**, not a crash. A recession is degrowth's evil twin; the whole point is to shrink throughput *on purpose* while protecting people.
- Its positive program is **de-commodifying** — "de-enclosing the commons." Ecological economists find that decommodifying public goods (transit, housing, healthcare, water) lets people live well on far less income, which takes pressure off both the planet and the wage. This is exactly canon's "abundance is arranged, not sold."

**Failure mode / tension:** degrowth in one bioregion, surrounded by a growth world, faces the classic problem — capital and cheap goods flow in from outside, undercutting the local arrangement. Our canon dodges this by making the turning global-ish and half-forgotten, but a writer can mine the seam where the Salish trades with places that *didn't* turn.

*Sources: Raworth,* Doughnut Economics *(2017); [Amsterdam City Doughnut, kateraworth.com](https://www.kateraworth.com/2020/04/08/amsterdam-city-doughnut/); [CNBC on Amsterdam/Brussels](https://www.cnbc.com/2021/03/25/amsterdam-brussels-bet-on-doughnut-economics-amid-covid-crisis.html); [Jacobin critique](https://jacobin.com/2021/09/doughnut-economics-raworth-amsterdam-capitalism-socialism); Hickel,* Less Is More *(2020); [Hickel, "What does degrowth mean?"* Globalizations *(2021)](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/59bc0e610abd04bd1e067ccc/t/5f520ea6c72a634453d44d50/1599213254905/Hickel+-+What+does+degrowth+mean.pdf).*

## 3. Who owns the workplace: worker cooperatives and the Mondragón evidence

If there's no capital class extracting profit, who runs the bakery, the shipyard, the clinic? The strongest real-world answer is the **worker cooperative**: the people who do the work own the firm one-member-one-vote, and elect (or rotate through) management.

The flagship is **Mondragón**, in the Basque Country, founded in 1956 by a priest, José María Arizmendiarrieta, and five graduates of his technical school. Today it's a federation of ~80+ cooperatives, roughly 70,000 worker-members, with its own cooperative bank (Laboral Kutxa), its own university, its own social-security and R&D institutions. Two facts are gold for worldbuilding:

- **Compressed pay.** Mondragón caps the ratio between highest- and lowest-paid. The much-cited figure is ~6:1 (some co-ops run tighter, ~5:1; the federation's ceiling has been quoted up to ~9:1) — against a U.S. large-firm CEO-to-worker ratio in the hundreds-to-one. Managers are paid, respected, and *not rich*.
- **The commons absorbs shocks.** When Fagor Electrodomésticos — the *original* co-op, an appliance maker — went bankrupt in 2013 (~€1.1 billion debt, ~1,800 members' jobs lost after the 2008 crash gutted Spanish housing), the federation didn't just fire people. It **relocated the majority of displaced workers into other Mondragón co-ops**, and members' capital was protected ahead of external creditors. Solidarity was institutional, not sentimental.

The general evidence is encouraging and under-appreciated: economist **Virginie Pérotin**'s reviews of French and other data find worker co-ops survive **at least as well as, and often better than, conventional firms** — with more stable employment (they cut wages/hours to save jobs in downturns rather than laying people off), comparable or higher productivity, and lifespans that frequently exceed a century. Survival rates have been found roughly a third higher than conventional enterprises in some datasets.

**Failure modes, told straight:**
- **Degeneration.** Successful co-ops face pressure to hire non-member wage workers to stay flexible/cheap — recreating a two-tier workforce (owners vs. employees). Mondragón wrestled exactly this with its foreign subsidiaries, which are largely *not* cooperativized.
- **Market discipline still bites.** Fagor proves co-ops embedded in a capitalist market can still be destroyed by that market. In *our* world the market is thin and local, which removes some of this pressure — but also removes the discipline that keeps co-ops efficient, so we should expect a different failure: coziness, stagnation, the co-op that can't fire its worst member.
- **Governance fatigue.** One-member-one-vote means meetings. Lots of meetings.

*Sources: [Mondragon Corporation, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mondragon_Corporation); [Christian Science Monitor, "capitalism actually works for the workers" (2024)](https://www.csmonitor.com/Business/2024/0513/income-inequality-capitalism-mondragon-corporation); [Errasti et al., "Mondragon Employment Record 1983–2019,"* Journal of Labor and Society *(2023)](https://brill.com/view/journals/jlso/26/3/article-p336_003.xml); [Pérotin, "What Do We Really Know About Workers' Cooperatives?"](http://efesonline.org/LIBRARY/2016/worker_co-op_report.pdf).*

## 4. Who decides the budget: participatory budgeting, and Kerala's "big bang"

Two real cases prove ordinary people can allocate serious public resources better than technocrats — with measurable results.

**Porto Alegre, Brazil (from 1989).** The city (then ~1.3M people) let residents directly decide a large share of the municipal investment budget through neighborhood assemblies and delegate councils. It ran annually for over two decades. Outcomes:
- Household **water and sewer connections rose from ~75% (1988) to ~98% (1997).**
- Health and education climbed toward **~40% of the city budget**; spending systematically favored poor neighborhoods.
- Over 200 km of roads paved in low-income areas; municipal-school enrollment tripled.
- The rigorous kicker: **Touchton & Wampler**, studying Brazil's largest cities over ~20 years, found municipalities that used participatory budgeting for 8+ years saw **infant mortality fall by roughly 20%**, controlling for other factors — the effect strengthening the longer PB ran. Deliberation, sustained, saves babies.

**Kerala, India — the People's Plan Campaign (launched 17 August 1996).** The most ambitious decentralization anywhere: the state devolved **35–40% of its development budget** to ~1,200 local governments (village, block, and district *panchayats* plus municipalities), planned through open *gram sabha* (village assembly) meetings. Combined with Kerala's earlier land reform, strong public health, and near-universal literacy, this produced the famous "Kerala model": life expectancy and literacy comparable to rich countries on a fraction of the income. Local councils, e.g., mobilized youth and NGOs to plant 100,000+ mangroves for coastal protection — decentralized ecological restoration, which rhymes exactly with our risen shoreline.

**Failure modes:** Porto Alegre's PB **declined and was effectively abandoned** after political change — it depended on a committed party (the PT) and eroded when that support went. This is the central warning: **participatory institutions are not self-sustaining; they atrophy without active defense, and they can be captured or hollowed out while keeping the name.** Kerala's decentralization is also critiqued for uneven quality, bureaucratic capture, and dependence on the state's fiscal transfers. Participation also has a class/time bias: the people who can attend Tuesday-night assemblies are not a random sample.

*Sources: [World Bank, "Participatory Budgeting in Brazil"](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/600841468017069677/pdf/514180WP0BR0Bu10Box342027B01PUBLIC1.pdf); [Touchton & Wampler,* World Development *(2014)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0305750X13000156); [WRI, "Porto Alegre... Sustaining Transformative Change"](https://www.wri.org/research/porto-alegre-participatory-budgeting-and-challenge-sustaining-transformative-change); [Participedia: Kerala People's Campaign](https://participedia.net/case/35); [Heller, Harilal & Chaudhuri,* World Development *(2007)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0305750X06002269).*

## 5. Confederation without a state: Bookchin, Rojava, and their limits

**Murray Bookchin** (social ecologist, d. 2006) gives the political theory closest to our canon. His **libertarian municipalism / communalism** argues: the ecological crisis is *at root a social crisis* (hierarchy and domination), and the antidote is to rebuild the *civic* sphere — **face-to-face popular assemblies** at the neighborhood and town scale, which then **confederate** upward. Delegates to the confederal councils are **mandated and recallable** — they carry the assembly's decision, they don't vote their own conscience, and they can be pulled back at any time. Power flows *up* from the assemblies, never down from the council. This is the anti-centralization engine our world needs, stated cleanly.

**Rojava** (the Autonomous Administration of North and East Syria) is the largest live experiment in Bookchin-influenced governance, running since ~2012 across a region of several million people. Its architecture is worth stealing wholesale:
- **The commune** (a few hundred households) is the base cell — it runs committees for defense, economy, reconciliation, women, youth, services.
- Communes federate into **neighborhood → district → regional councils.**
- **Co-chairs everywhere:** every leadership position is held *jointly by one woman and one man*, and there are **autonomous women's councils** at every level with the power to veto decisions affecting women. Gender parity is structural, not aspirational.
- Disputes go first to **local reconciliation committees** aiming at restoration, not punishment.
- The economy leans on **cooperatives grown out of the communes** (agriculture, bakeries, textiles).

**Failure modes, unromanticized:** Rojava exists under war and embargo, which both forged it and distorts every judgment about it. Critics (including sympathetic ones) note the cooperative "social economy" **coexisted with, rather than replaced, the old private economy** — it won moral legitimacy faster than real economic power. There are real tensions between the bottom-up assembly ideal and the top-down reality of the ruling party (PYD) and its armed forces during wartime; between Kurdish leadership and Arab/other communities; and the sheer question of whether radically horizontal governance survives the transition from insurgency to administration. For us, the usable lesson is the *structure* (communes, mandated recallable delegates, co-chairs, women's veto, reconciliation-first justice), held at arm's length from the war that produced it.

*Sources: [Bookchin, "Libertarian Municipalism: An Overview"](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/murray-bookchin-libertarian-municipalism-an-overview); [Green European Journal on Bookchin's legacy](https://www.greeneuropeanjournal.eu/municipalism-murray-bookchins-legacy/); [Rojava Information Center, "Communes: the building block of democratic confederalism"](https://rojavainformationcenter.org/2020/05/explainer-communes-the-building-block-of-democratic-confederalism/); [TRISE, "Democratic Revolution in Rojava"](https://trise.org/2018/08/27/democratic-revolution-in-rojava/).*

## 6. How a meeting actually reaches a decision: sociocracy and consent

Assemblies are only as good as their decision method. Majority voting produces losers who resent the outcome; full consensus can be held hostage by one holdout. **Sociocracy** (a.k.a. dynamic governance; the S3 and "Sociocracy For All" variants are widely used in co-ops, ecovillages, and schools) threads the needle with **consent, not consensus**:

- A proposal passes when **no one has a *reasoned, paramount objection*** — an argument that it would actively harm the group's aims. The bar is **"good enough for now, safe enough to try,"** *not* "everyone loves it." This is dramatically faster than seeking enthusiastic agreement.
- Work is organized in **circles** (a kitchen circle, a water circle), each with a defined domain and the authority to run it.
- Circles link by **double-linking**: two people sit in both a circle and its parent circle, carrying information both ways — so no single "boss link" controls the flow. (Notice this is the meeting-room echo of Ostrom's nested enterprises and Bookchin's confederation.)
- Roles are filled by **open nomination and consent**, not campaigning — you can be *chosen* for a role you didn't ask for, and there's no popularity contest. (Note how cleanly this satisfies the "no attention-metrics" refusal.)

**Failure mode:** consent still costs time and skill; poorly facilitated, "reasoned objection" becomes a cover for the loudest or most fluent person, and circle proliferation can bury a community in process. Sociocracy assumes good faith and training; both can be scarce.

*Sources: [Sociocracy For All — Consent Decision-Making](https://www.sociocracyforall.org/consent-decision-making/); [Sociocracy 3.0 patterns](https://patterns.sociocracy30.org/consent-decision-making.html).*

## 7. What "money" is when abundance is arranged: gift, debt, and Graeber

Canon says money exists but is "thin and local and boring," and the thick layer runs on gift and mutual aid. Anthropology backs this hard.

**David Graeber**, in *Debt: The First 5,000 Years* (2011), demolishes the economics-textbook origin myth that money arose to fix the "inefficiency of barter." No society running on barter-then-money has ever been found. What anthropologists actually find first is **credit and obligation** — webs of who-owes-whom, tracked socially, long before coins. His key distinctions for us:

- **"Baseline / everyday communism"**: *"from each according to their abilities, to each according to their needs"* is not a utopian program — it's the **default mode of ordinary sociality** already, in every society. If someone asks you for a light, or directions, or to pass the salt, you don't invoice them. Graeber argues this baseline is the bedrock any economy actually floats on; capitalism just hides it. Our world simply *widens the aperture* of everyday communism — the tea you didn't order is baseline communism made civic.
- **"Human economies" vs. debt economies.** In human economies, transactions create and maintain *ongoing relationships*; the "debt" is never meant to be cleared, because clearing it would end the relationship. Money's dangerous trick is that it makes obligations **precisely quantifiable, impersonal, and transferable** — which lets a bond between people be converted into a cold number that can be sold to a stranger. A world that wants warm bonds keeps money *deliberately clumsy* at doing that.

Behind Graeber stands **Marcel Mauss's *The Gift* (1925)**: the gift is never free — it carries the obligation to receive and to reciprocate, and this triple obligation (give / receive / return) is precisely what *weaves people together* rather than settling accounts and parting. A gift economy is not the absence of exchange; it's exchange whose *purpose* is the relationship, not the goods.

**Failure modes to keep honest:** gift economies are not automatically gentle. They can enforce **conformity and obligation-debt** (you *must* reciprocate; refusal is an insult), concentrate soft power in generous "big men," and exclude outsiders who have nothing to give and no standing in the web. Graeber's own point cuts both ways: debt-as-morality is exactly how obligation becomes a weapon.

*Sources: Graeber,* Debt: The First 5,000 Years *(2011), [davidgraeber.org](https://davidgraeber.org/books/debt-the-first-5000-years/); Mauss,* The Gift *(1925); [Red Pepper, "Learning from David Graeber"](https://www.redpepper.org.uk/culture-media/books/david-graeber-debt-first-5000-years/).*

## 8. Redistribution as governance: the Pacific Northwest potlatch

This is the deepest local root, and it's not metaphor — it's the governance system of the very ground the city stands on. Among **Coast Salish** and other Northwest Coast peoples, the **potlatch** was *the primary governmental institution, legal system, and economy at once.* A host family gave away — sometimes destroyed — vast accumulated wealth (blankets, canoes, coppers, food) before witnesses. Crucially, **status came from giving away, not from hoarding.** The feast did real institutional work:

- It **witnessed and ratified** name-transfers, marriages, inheritances, and territorial rights — the guests were the legal record. Governance was *performed and remembered*, not filed.
- It **redistributed** surplus from the rich host down through the community.
- It **rebalanced** rank and settled disputes publicly.

Canada banned the potlatch from **1885 to 1951** (via the Indian Act), correctly understanding it as the load-bearing structure of Indigenous society and therefore a target for assimilation. Enforcement failed; potlatches went underground and persisted, and they are practiced openly again today. For a *Salish-Sea* Seattle, this is not a borrowing to be flattened into "aesthetic" — it's a living regional tradition where prestige-through-generosity, wealth-as-something-you-move-not-hold, and law-as-witnessed-ceremony already exist. Any depiction should route respect and specificity toward the actual peoples (Duwamish, Suquamish, Muckleshoot, and their neighbors) rather than treating it as free set-dressing.

*Sources: [The Canadian Encyclopedia, "Potlatch Ban"](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/potlatch-ban); [Wikipedia, "Potlatch ban"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potlatch_ban).*

## 9. Harm without a cage: restorative and transformative justice

If there are no attention-metrics ranking people and no engine of extraction, there is also — the canon strongly implies — no carceral machine warehousing them. How does harm get handled?

**Restorative justice (RJ)** brings the person harmed, the person who caused harm, and the affected community into a facilitated process centered on three questions: *What happened? Who was harmed and what do they need? Whose obligation is it to make things right?* Prison asks only "what rule was broken and what's the punishment." The evidence is genuinely strong:
- A randomized study of the "Make-it-Right" program found RJ **cut the probability of rearrest within six months by ~19 percentage points — a 44% reduction** versus control, an effect persisting up to four years.
- Meta-analyses find significant (if modest) reductions in general reoffending, and **consistently higher satisfaction for victims** and higher genuine accountability from those who did harm, than the conventional system delivers.

**Transformative justice (TJ)** goes further: it refuses to hand harm to the state at all, and insists that harm grows from conditions (poverty, isolation, untreated trauma, patriarchy) that punishment leaves intact — so a real response must *change the conditions*, not just the offender. TJ built its practice largely in communities that couldn't safely call the police (queer, trans, Black, immigrant, sex-worker communities), through **community accountability** processes and "pods" of trusted people. It's the honest name for what justice looks like when there's genuinely no cage to fall back on.

**Failure modes, stated plainly:** RJ/TJ are labor-intensive, slow, and depend on the person who caused harm actually engaging — a genuine refusal, or a manipulative fake engagement, can leave survivors re-injured with no backstop. They struggle most with exactly the hardest cases: serial abusers, gendered and sexual violence, and situations of power imbalance where "dialogue" can retraumatize. TJ practitioners are the *first* to say the movement has more failed and stalled processes than clean successes, and that "community" is not automatically safe or competent. A world that has abolished the cage must have thought hard about the person who will not stop — and the writer should not pretend that's easy. This is fertile, honest dramatic ground.

*Sources: [NBER, "Can Restorative Justice Conferencing Reduce Recidivism?"](https://www.nber.org/papers/w29150); [Fulham et al., meta-analysis,* Criminology & Criminal Justice *(2025)](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/17488958231215228); [The Sentencing Project, "Restorative Justice Diversion"](https://www.sentencingproject.org/reports/restorative-justice-diversion-a-better-way-to-provide-meaningful-accountability-for-youth/); generationFIVE & Mia Mingus on transformative justice / "pods."*

## 10. The cross-cutting tensions (so the world stays honest)

Every mechanism above shares a family of failure modes. Name them and the world gets more real, not less:

- **The tyranny of the meeting.** Horizontal governance runs on time and emotional labor, unevenly distributed. The retired, the fluent, and the childless-and-energetic show up; the exhausted and the caretaking don't. "Participatory" quietly becomes "the same eleven people."
- **Atrophy and capture.** Porto Alegre is the warning: participatory institutions are *not* self-sustaining. They decay without active defense and can be hollowed out while keeping the name. Utopias die of taking themselves for granted.
- **The exit problem.** Consensus and gift-webs create powerful pressure to conform; the price of dissent is social, not legal, but it is real. Where does the person who *doesn't* fit their commune go? (Our canon's answer is partly the Weave — routing difference outward — but locally, this bites.)
- **Coordinating the boring big stuff.** Assemblies are great at "should we build a garden here." They are bad at maintaining a regional water main, a vaccine supply chain, or a fifty-year seawall — things that need continuity, expertise, and unglamorous upkeep. Polycentricity's honest weak point is the *dull, large, slow* commons.
- **The border.** Every one of these systems is easier inside a bounded community than at its edge — with outsiders, newcomers, and the places that never turned.

## Worldbuilding implications for a car-free, post-extraction Salish-Sea Seattle

Concrete, usable texture. All of this is extrapolated from the real cases above.

1. **A neighborhood decides by circle and consent, not by vote.** A block wants to turn a rusted parking structure into a mushroom farm. It goes to the **block circle** (a few dozen households), which tests for *paramount objections* — not "who's in favor" but "does anyone see this actively harming us." Standard closing phrase, said half-ironically by tired people at 9pm: *"Good enough for now, safe enough to try."* Two people from the block circle also sit in the **watershed circle** upstairs, so the water impact gets carried up without anyone being "in charge."

2. **Money feels like a bus transfer — functional, thin, faintly embarrassing to lean on.** There are local credits (call them *tallies*, or *hours*, or *tokens* — pick one) for the boring necessary trades and for settling with people you don't know. But reaching for money among people you *do* know reads as slightly cold, the way pulling out a calculator to split a dinner with close friends reads now. The thick layer — meals, tools, childcare, a knit hat off the Weave — moves as gift, and *keeping a ledger of who owes whom is the actual rudeness.* You are handed tea you didn't order; you do not, ever, price the tea.

3. **Status is what you give away, not what you hold.** Directly from the potlatch, held with respect for whose tradition it is. The most *respected* people are conspicuous givers — the woman who feeds half the pier, the shipwright who trained forty apprentices for nothing. A hoarder isn't policed; they're just quietly unimportant. Big seasonal give-away feasts ratify things — a new co-op's founding, a name passing to a kid, a boundary between two gardens — with the guests serving as the living record. **The witnesses *are* the paperwork.**

4. **When someone does harm, a "pod" convenes, not a cop.** Every person has an informal, half-acknowledged set of people they'd call if they hurt someone or were hurt — trusted friends, an aunt, a former bandmate. Harm triggers a facilitated circle: *what happened, who was hurt, what do they need, whose job is it to set it right.* Repair is concrete and public — labor owed, a thing rebuilt, a season of showing up. Graduated, Ostrom-style: a first drift gets a warning and help; the sanction escalates only if it repeats.

5. **And there's the person who won't stop — the world's honest open wound.** The community that abolished the cage still has the man who keeps hurting people and refuses every process. What they do is *exclusion*, not incarceration: he loses standing, loses the gift-web, is asked to leave a commune — soft power with hard edges. Everyone is uneasy about it. Some think it's too cruel; some think it's not enough. This unease should never be resolved cleanly; it's where the world's moral seriousness lives.

6. **Delegates are mandated and recallable, and it's a chore, not a prize.** When the neighborhood sends someone to the bioregional water table, she carries *the block's decision*, not her own opinion, and she can be pulled back mid-term if she drifts. There's no campaigning, no follower count, no "rising star" (the no-attention-metrics refusal, enforced at the level of ambition itself). Being picked for a role is often met with a groan. Rotation is constant so no one accretes a fiefdom.

7. **Everything is co-chaired, and there are councils you can't overrule.** Straight from Rojava: paired leadership is the ordinary shape of any role, and there are autonomous councils — of women, of the disabled, of the newest arrivals — with a **veto** over decisions that land on them. It's not politeness; it's structural, and older residents remember arguing bitterly about it before it became just how things are.

8. **The friction is the point, and people complain about it constantly.** Nobody in this world thinks the meetings are fun. "I lost a whole evening to whether the dock gets repainted" is a normal thing to grumble. There's a recognized social role — call them a **facilitator** or a *weaver* — trained in running consent circles, and a good one is quietly treasured because they make the process bearable. Bad facilitation is a real villain: the smooth talker who dresses up his preference as a "reasoned objection."

9. **Restoration work is visible in the built world, and it's decentralized.** Like Kerala's panchayats planting 100,000 mangroves, the marsh-and-mussel-rope shoreline of the canon is maintained block by block — each stretch of floating garden and salt marsh is some circle's adopted domain, logged in nobody's central database, coordinated by neighbors comparing notes. When a seawall section is *nobody's* clear domain, it rots — the coordination gap is a real plot hazard (a storm, a failure, a scramble to figure out whose it was).

10. **The dull big commons is where authority quietly concentrates — and people watch it.** The regional water main, the vaccine cold-chain, the fifty-year sea defense: these need continuity and expertise that assemblies are bad at, so they're run by long-lived **stewarding circles** of specialists. Everyone knows this is where power *could* congeal into something the world swore off, so these circles are the most heavily double-linked, most rotated, most watched bodies in the Salish. A recurring civic anxiety: *are the water people becoming a government?*
