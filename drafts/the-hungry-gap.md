# The hungry gap

**Object-study, the ferment cellar under the Yesler public kitchen, late in the eating year —**

The jars tell you what month it is better than the light does. By deep winter the top shelf is bright — the summer's put-up, tomatoes and beans and the stone fruit somebody's House swore they'd ration and didn't. It's the bottom shelf that runs the city now: the sour, the salt, the long keepers. Kraut gone soft and golden. Whole cabbages in brine. The kelp, cut and dried and cut again, that the ocean farms bring in when almost nothing else is growing `[DEPLOYED · draft]` — because kelp is a cold-water crop, it fattens through the exact months the land gives nothing, which is either good planning or the sea being kinder than the soil, and the farmers argue about which.

This is the hungry gap. Every eating culture that ever lived off a real place knew it: the sharp end of the year, after the stores run thin and before the new green comes, when people learned to preserve or learned to starve `[DEPLOYED · draft]`. The before-times mostly forgot it existed — a grape from the far hemisphere in February, flown in, its true cost billed to someone you'd never meet. This world remembers it on purpose. The gap is not a failure here. It's a season, and it's managed, and managing it so it doesn't land hardest on the people with the thinnest buffer is a live civic discipline, not a solved problem `[DEPLOYED · draft]`.

Which is why, three floors down and a quarter mile out under the working waterfront, there is the Dark Garden.

You don't grow food without the sun unless you have to. The Dark Garden is chemosynthesis — beds of the microbes that, at the real hydrothermal vents off this coast, build living tissue out of the chemistry of the water instead of light, the way the tube worms and their bacteria have done in the black since long before anything had eyes `[PLAUSIBLE · draft]`. Seeded from the vents, tended in the dark, the beds put protein on the bottom shelf when the surface world is closed for the season. In the worst weeks of the gap, the Dark Garden is the reason the rest-ledger for the food tenders doesn't go blood-red, the reason a House with a sick elder and no summer surplus still eats.

It is also heresy, and everyone who works it says so first, before you can.

Because here is the thing the jars won't tell you. The chemistry that feeds those beds is the same chemistry the old world wanted to strip and sell — the metal-rich water at the vents is exactly what the deep-sea miners came for, and coming for it means grinding the vents themselves, the oldest untouched thing left, into slurry `[DEPLOYED · draft]`. The Dark Garden lives one budget cycle, one bad winter, one frightened council vote away from becoming the precise extraction this world turned itself inside out to refuse. Tend the vent and it feeds you forever. Mine the vent and it feeds you enormously, once. The distance between those two sentences is the whole argument, and the world does not get to stop having it.

And it isn't only the vents. The beds run on power — pumps, warmth, the dark kept liveable — and they draw it in the one season this coast has least to spare, a winter-peaking grid with the sun gone by mid-afternoon `[DEPLOYED · draft]`. So the gap sets two needs against each other in the same cold week: heat the homes or run the beds. Ration the power evenly and you've quietly decided that a frail elder on the same share as a healthy 25-year-old can "just wear a sweater" — which is how a fairer-sounding rule lets the market's old victims die of a fairer-sounding cause `[DEPLOYED · draft]`. The assembly weighs it every deep winter, out loud, and does not get to feel clean about the answer.

So the beds are watched — not the way you watch a threat, the way you watch a fire you are choosing to keep in the hearth. The Salish Sea has standing as a legal person, voiced by its guardians, and it can halt the Dark Garden, the harvest, any of it `[DEMONSTRATED · draft]`. That's the brake. But a brake is a thing someone has to decide to pull, in a cold February, with the bottom shelf running low and children who are actually hungry, not hungry in the abstract. Nobody in this world pretends that vote is easy. The wall where they'd write the names, if they ever got it wrong, is already built.

The cellar-keeper — a wide, quiet person the House of Marrow lends out to the public kitchen through the gap — turns a jar a quarter-turn so the brine covers the top of the cabbage again, and says the only thing there is to say about it. *We are not hungry because we agreed to be a little afraid of how we're fed. Trade that away and you get full for a season and then you get the old world back.* Then they hand you the jar to carry up, because down here you don't leave with empty hands.
