# Energy and heat — how a dark, wet, cold Salish winter city keeps warm without extraction

*Domain: the physics and politics of powering and heating a car-free, post-extraction Seattle through the season that breaks every clean-energy plan — the dark, wet, cold Salish winter, when the sun is gone by mid-afternoon, demand peaks, and the rivers you're trying to give back to the salmon are the very thing that used to keep you warm. This brief is the engineering companion to `research/scarcity-and-hard-tradeoffs.md` Part III (which handles the **rationing** of scarce winter energy). That brief asks "who gets the last kilowatt-hour and how do we decide fairly." This one asks the prior question: **what generates and moves the heat in the first place, where it comes from honestly, and where the whole thing physically runs out.** I try not to repeat the rationing argument; I try to build the machine it rations.*

The Reachability mandate (`CANON.md`) is the whole game here: no magic. Every system below traces to a real plant, pilot, or lab result operating now, and says where the evidence ends and the hope begins. A heating engineer, a lineworker, a marine ecologist, or a district-energy planner should close this with a to-do list, not a sigh — and also with a short list of things that genuinely might not work.

---

## 1. The physical fact: this bioregion peaks in the dark, and its clean grid is already built

Start with the constraint that makes the Salish Sea *hard*, not easy, for a post-fossil future — the opposite of the sunny-desert solarpunk default.

- **The grid is already ~88% clean, and that's the trap.** Seattle City Light is roughly 77–90% hydropower depending on the water year, one of the greenest large utilities in America; about 40% comes from its own dams on the Skagit and Pend Oreille. ([Seattle City Light — Creating Our Energy Future](https://www.seattle.gov/city-light/energy); [Powerlines / 2026 IRP](https://powerlines.seattle.gov/2026/07/09/how-city-light-is-creating-our-energy-future-2026-integrated-resource-plan/)) A city starting from coal has easy wins — just stop burning coal. A city starting from hydro has already spent them. The next increment of clean energy here is the *expensive* one.
- **The Pacific Northwest is winter-peaking.** Unlike most of the US (summer-peaking, air-conditioning-driven), demand here is highest in the cold, dark heating months. City Light's own 2026 Integrated Resource Plan (approved by Council in June 2026) warns that electrification of heat and transport is pushing the winter peak up hardest, with the grid most stressed during cold snaps. ([Powerlines / 2026 IRP](https://powerlines.seattle.gov/2026/07/09/how-city-light-is-creating-our-energy-future-2026-integrated-resource-plan/); [EIA — NW winter demand](https://www.eia.gov/todayinenergy/detail.php?id=32612)) **Peak need and minimum solar arrive in the same week.** Solar is near-useless in a Seattle January.
- **You cannot build your way out with more dams — canon forbids it.** The clean baseload backbone *is* the rivers, and this world is giving the rivers back to the salmon (`CANON.md`: the Duwamish and the Salish Sea are legal persons; treaty rights are paramount; `research/scarcity-and-hard-tradeoffs.md` Part II). The real precedent is dam *removal* — the Elwha (largest dam removal in US history, completed 2014) and the Klamath (2024). So the hydro fleet is not a growing asset; it is a **contested, possibly shrinking** one, because every acre-foot spilled for a fish migration or held back from a reservoir drawdown is winter firm power not delivered. The single most important energy fact of this world is that **its cleanest power source and its most sacred ecological obligation are the same water**, and they pull against each other every low-flow, high-demand winter.

So: a city that refuses fossil extraction, refuses new dams, gives water back to fish, and gets almost no winter sun. That is the box. Everything below is an attempt to stay warm inside it — honestly, including the parts where it doesn't quite work.

## 2. Heat is the problem, not electricity — and heat is the winnable part

The largest winter load in a cold-wet city is not lighting or devices; it is **space heating and hot water**. The good news, and it is real and large: the Salish climate is close to the *ideal* case for the one technology that has already won this argument — the heat pump.

### 2.1 Heat pumps: the marine-wet climate is their best case, not their worst

A heat pump doesn't make heat, it *moves* it, so it delivers 2–4 units of warmth per unit of electricity (Coefficient of Performance, COP). The DOE Cold Climate Heat Pump Challenge field validation (PNNL, published January 2025, sited mostly in the Northwest) measured compressor-only heating COPs ranging from **~1.8 at −15 °F to ~3.5 at +45 °F**. ([PNNL — CCHP field results](https://www.pnnl.gov/main/publications/external/technical_reports/PNNL-37127.pdf); [OSTI](https://www.osti.gov/biblio/2503488))

Here is the load-bearing nuance the ecomodernist "it's too cold for heat pumps" line misses: **the Salish lowland almost never sees −15 °F.** A Seattle winter is *wet and mild-cold* — mostly 35–45 °F. That is exactly the band where heat pumps run at COP ~3. The maritime Northwest is one of the best heat-pump climates on Earth; the problem was never the physics of the device. So heating electrification here is not a heroic leap — it is a rollout. The seedling is mature; the gap is capital, installers, and the grid to feed it.

The honest cost: heat pumps are expensive to install, and *upfront cost is an energy-poverty gate* (Section 7). The device that saves the poor money over ten years can be the device they can't afford to buy — which is precisely why, in this world, heat is a **commons install**, not a market purchase (see worldbuilding §11).

### 2.2 District heat and waste-heat recovery: stop generating heat, start *catching* it

The deeper move is to not burn anything at all — to pump warmth out of things that are already warm and pipe it to homes. Two real, operating precedents, one of them next door:

- **Sewage heat recovery — Vancouver, right across the water.** The False Creek Neighbourhood Energy Utility is the **first district-energy system in North America to recover heat from raw sewage.** It captures the warmth of what a city flushes — showers, laundry, dishwater — and heat-pumps it up to usable temperature at **over 300% efficiency (COP >3)**, now heating **47 buildings, ~10,000 residents, a university and a community centre**; two new sewage-to-water heat pumps came online end of 2024. It is **city-owned, self-funded, and sells heat at cost.** ([City of Vancouver — False Creek NEU](https://vancouver.ca/home-property-development/southeast-false-creek-neighbourhood-energy-utility.aspx); [Vancouver — 2025 expansion](https://vancouver.ca/news-calendar/energy-centre-expansion-complete-oct-2025.aspx)) This is the closest thing to a drop-in template for a Salish-Sea city that exists, and it is publicly owned — the ownership model canon already wants.
- **The sea itself is a battery of low-grade heat — Drammen, Norway.** Drammen heats most of a city by pulling water from the fjord at just **8–9 °C**, from 18 m depth, and heat-pumping it up: three units delivering **14 MW, ~67 GWh/yr, 85% of the city's hot water,** using ammonia (zero global-warming-potential) refrigerant and hydropower electricity, so near-zero carbon. It saved ~1.5 Mt CO₂ and €2.69M/yr versus gas. ([Star Renewable — Drammen](https://www.neatpumps.com/case-studies/drammen-district-heating-heatpumps/); [Wikipedia — Drammen Heat Pump](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drammen_Heat_Pump)) The Salish Sea is a cold, deep, well-mixed marine body sitting against the whole city. It is, thermodynamically, an enormous low-grade heat reservoir. **You do not need the water to be warm; you need it to be liquid and abundant, and it is.**

*Extrapolation, flagged:* the seedling is Drammen (14 MW) and False Creek (one neighbourhood); the leap this world makes is a **networked seawater-and-sewage district heat grid** threaded through the shoreline city, using the restored Elliott Bay seawall (canon) as intake infrastructure. The gap is real: marine intake screening to avoid drawing in the very juvenile salmon the seawall's glass-block corridor exists to protect (an ecological conflict, not a footnote — see §8), thermal discharge plumes in a cold-adapted ecosystem, and the fact that seawater heat pumps still need *electricity* to run the compressor, which loops us straight back to the winter power problem.

### 2.3 Storing heat across seasons: the pit and the pile

Heat is far cheaper to store than electricity, and you can store it for *months*. This is the quiet superpower of a district-heat city.

- **Seasonal pit thermal storage — Marstal, Denmark.** A community-owned district heating co-op stores summer solar heat in a **75,000 m³ insulated water pit** (88 × 113 m, 16 m deep) and draws it down through winter — benchmark cost around **€30/m³.** ([Solarthermalworld — pit storage cost](https://solarthermalworld.org/news/seasonal-pit-heat-storage-cost-benchmark-30-eurm3/); [Bankwatch — Marstal case study](https://bankwatch.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/2022-05_Case-Study-Marstal_eng.pdf)) It is **cooperatively owned** — a Danish district-heating tradition of consumer co-ops, which maps onto canon's commons governance far better than a utility monopoly.

The Salish twist: this world has little summer *sun* to store, but it has summer *hydro surplus* (snowmelt, long days, low demand) and shoulder-season tidal energy. A pit or aquifer thermal store lets you move that surplus into January as **heat** — sidestepping the brutal economics of storing it as electricity. The city's winter warmth can be, in part, *last spring's meltwater, kept.*

## 3. The power layer: what actually spins the meter in a Salish December

Heat pumps and district loops all still draw electricity at the coldest, darkest hour. Here is the honest generation stack for a no-new-dams, no-fossil, low-sun winter — best sources first, each with its seedling and its gap.

### 3.1 Tidal — the one renewable that is dark-, calm-, and season-proof, and it's local

Tides don't care about clouds, wind, or the length of the day. In a bioregion whose entire clean-energy problem is *the sun goes away*, a power source driven by the **moon** is close to a gift — and the Salish Sea has world-class tidal currents.

- **The real project, and the real failure.** Snohomish County PUD spent 2007–2014 developing a pilot tidal array in **Admiralty Inlet** (the deep, fast throat between the Kitsap Peninsula and Whidbey Island): two OpenHydro turbines, 300 kW each, spinning ~70% of the time. FERC issued a pilot license in **March 2014** — and the PUD **cancelled it in September 2014** when the DOE withdrew cost-share and the numbers stopped working. ([Snohomish PUD — Tidal overview](https://www.snopud.com/community-environment/our-energy-future/projects/research/tidal-energy/tidal-project-overview/); [Tethys — Admiralty Inlet](https://tethys.pnnl.gov/project-sites/admiralty-inlet-pilot-tidal-project)) It died of cost, not of physics. That is the honest seedling: a fully-licensed, FERC-approved local tidal project, shelved for money.
- **The resource is real and mapped.** Researchers have named the three best tidal sites in the Sound: **Admiralty Inlet, Tacoma Narrows (South Sound), and Rosario Strait (east of Lopez and Orcas).** ([Encyclopedia of Puget Sound — tidal](https://www.eopugetsound.org/articles/tidal-energy-puget-sound))
- **The cybernetic virtue (see §9):** tides are *perfectly predictable decades out* — you know to the minute when Admiralty Inlet will rip. And the three sites peak at *different times* because the tidal wave reaches them at different phases, so a phased array across all three **smooths the four-times-a-day slack-water gaps** into something closer to firm. This predictability is the single most valuable property in a grid otherwise built on guessing the weather.

*Extrapolation, flagged:* the leap is from one cancelled 600 kW pilot to a **phased, distributed tidal fence** across the three Sound sites, matured a decade or two past 2014 hardware. The gaps are not small and this world does not pretend otherwise: **marine-mammal strike risk** (the Southern Resident orcas are legal persons here and can *halt the project* — canon's kin-with-standing clause applies directly and could kill it), **biofouling**, **sediment and larval transport**, cabling, and slack-tide zero-output. Tidal is the most promising and the most *conflicted* source — it is clean power sited in the exact water the whales hunt.

### 3.2 Wind — the honest disappointment, and why it isn't the answer here

The reflexive answer to "no winter sun" is "then use wind." This world has to be honest that **Pacific Northwest wind is politically and physically harder than the brochure**:

- **Offshore floating wind off Oregon collapsed.** BOEM postponed the Oregon lease auction in October 2024 after only **1 of 5 qualified bidders** showed interest, and in **July 2025 rescinded all the West Coast Wind Energy Areas** — 3.5 million acres de-designated — under pressure from fishing fleets, coastal towns, and *tribal advocates* who objected to the siting. ([OPB — WEA rescinded](https://www.opb.org/article/2025/07/30/oregon-wind-energy-wea-development-coos-bay-brookings/); [Offshore Wind — Oregon postponed](https://www.offshorewind.biz/2024/09/30/oregon-floating-wind-lease-sale-postponed/)) The lesson canon should keep: **offshore wind is not automatically the good-guy technology** — here it was opposed by the very coastal and Indigenous communities a post-extraction world centers.
- **The deeper physics: dunkelflaute.** The failure mode of a wind-heavy grid is the "dark doldrums" — a cold, still, sunless high-pressure system where wind *and* solar both flatline for days. In December 2024 UK wind fell to **6% of supply**, and gas ramped to a record **73%** to keep the lights on. ([Electric Insights — wind droughts](https://reports.electricinsights.co.uk/?p=2314)) A Salish-Sea winter high can be exactly this: freezing, foggy, dead calm. **Wind and solar fail *together*, at the coldest hour, for days.** Any honest plan needs something that runs *through* a dunkelflaute — which is the whole nuclear argument (§8), and the whole storage argument (§3.3), and the whole "keep some firm hydro" tension (§1).

### 3.3 Storage — turning surplus into a warm January night

- **Multi-day iron-air batteries — Form Energy.** The lithium battery stores ~4 hours; a dunkelflaute lasts days. Form Energy's **iron-air battery stores ~100 hours** using iron, water, and air — cheap, abundant, non-flammable materials — and its first commercial installation (with Great River Energy, Cambridge, Minnesota) came online **2025**, with more in 2026. ([Form Energy — technology](https://formenergy.com/technology/); [Latitude Media — first batteries on the grid](https://www.latitudemedia.com/news/forms-first-100-hour-batteries-are-hitting-the-grid/)) This is the seedling for **multi-day** storage — the exact duration a winter wind-drought needs, at materials cheap enough to overbuild. The gap: round-trip efficiency is modest and the technology is one product generation into commercial life.
- **Pumped hydro without new rivers.** Closed-loop pumped storage (pump water uphill on surplus, run it down on deficit) can be built off-river between two existing reservoirs or old quarries — storing energy *as water* without damming a salmon stream. It's the oldest grid storage there is; the gap here is siting it without touching a living river.
- **Heat storage is the cheat code (see §2.3):** for the *heating* load specifically, storing warmth in a water pit is an order of magnitude cheaper than storing electricity. A district-heat city stores most of its winter as *hot water*, not electrons.

### 3.4 Deep geothermal — firm, dark-proof heat, if the rock cooperates

The dream source for a sunless winter city is **firm underground heat** — always on, weather-blind, land-light.

- **Enhanced geothermal just crossed from lab to grid — Fervo Energy.** Using shale-drilling techniques, Fervo's Cape Station (Utah) drilled a **15,000-ft, 500 °F well**, hit flow rates NREL didn't expect until 2035, and is targeting **100 MW of baseload clean power for 2026, scaling toward 500 MW by 2028** (near-term dates are the company's projections, not yet delivered fact — flagged per the reachable-now frame). ([Fervo — record production](https://fervoenergy.com/fervo-energys-record-breaking-production-results-showcase-rapid-scale-up-of-enhanced-geothermal/); [MIT Tech Review — 2025 company to watch](https://www.technologyreview.com/2025/10/06/1124292/2025-climate-tech-companies-to-watch-fervo-energy-advanced-geothermal-power-plants/)) Enhanced Geothermal Systems (EGS) is the real, now-arriving seedling for firm clean power *and* firm clean heat.
- **The Salish gap is geological.** Cape Station sits on hot, drillable rock in the Basin and Range. **The Puget Lowland is not a prime EGS site** — the heat resource of the region lives in the **Cascade volcanic arc** (Mount Baker, Glacier Peak, the Mount St. Helens corridor), 100+ km east, which means a *transmission* problem and siting on or near sacred and wilderness ground. So geothermal here is a real hope with a real asterisk: the firm heat exists in this bioregion, but *under the mountains, not under the city.* Extrapolating a Cascade-arc geothermal tie-line is a defensible leap; pretending the hot rock is under Seattle is not.

## 4. The Dark Garden's draw, weighed against a warm home

Canon: through the sunless winter, part of the city's food comes from the **Dark Garden** — chemosynthetic beds seeded from real hydrothermal-vent chemistry, "one budget cycle away from becoming deep-sea mining, which is exactly why it's watched." `research/scarcity-and-hard-tradeoffs.md` §9 already established the trolley: **the beds draw power in the exact season power is scarcest — heat the homes or run the beds.** I won't re-litigate the rationing; I'll add the engineering shape of the dilemma so a scene can play it concretely:

- Chemosynthesis substitutes *chemical energy* (feeding microbes their electron donors) for sunlight, but the pumping, circulation, temperature control, and — if the beds are landside rather than on the seafloor — the artificial chemistry all cost electricity. In deep winter that electricity competes directly with heat pumps.
- This makes the Dark Garden a **swing load** — and swing loads are governable (§9). The honest version: some Januaries the assembly *dims the beds* to keep homes above a survivable temperature, and the winter menu gets thinner (back to the root cellar and the ferment, per `research/food-and-marine.md`). Food security and thermal security are the same budget line, drawn from the same dark-water winter grid.
- The watched line to deep-sea mining reappears here as an *energy* temptation: the cheapest firm power a desperate winter could reach for is under the seafloor, and the same infrastructure that farms the vents could strip them. The kin-with-standing guardians (the Salish Sea is a legal person) are the brake — and the brake has a cost measured in cold houses.

## 5. What the honest winter stack actually looks like

No single source solves a Salish January. The buildable answer is a **layered, diverse, deliberately over-built** system — the opposite of the efficiency-maximizing single-source grid the old world optimized for (and `research/power-and-bounded-inequality.md`'s "firebreak / slack is a civic virtue" applies exactly):

1. **Demand first:** superinsulate and heat-pump the building stock so the load itself shrinks (the cheapest kilowatt-hour is the one not needed). Marine climate = ideal heat-pump COP.
2. **Heat, not electricity, wherever possible:** seawater + sewage district loops (Drammen + False Creek), backed by seasonal pit storage (Marstal) charged on summer/shoulder surplus.
3. **Firm-ish clean electricity:** the *retained* hydro fleet (capped by fish obligations), phased tidal (moon-driven, dark-proof, whale-conflicted), and — if the rock and the treaties allow — a Cascade-arc geothermal tie.
4. **Ride-through storage:** multi-day iron-air banks and closed-loop pumped hydro for the dunkelflaute.
5. **The rationing layer** (handed to `research/scarcity-and-hard-tradeoffs.md`): a need-weighted, tradable, capped winter commons for the shortfall that remains — because in the honest version, **some Januaries the layered stack still isn't enough**, and then it stops being an engineering problem and becomes a decision about whose house stays warm.

The world does not claim the stack always closes the gap. It claims the stack is *buildable now* and that when it fails, the failure is faced out loud, not hidden.

## 6. Intermittency and hard limits, stated plainly

The limits in one place:

- **Wind + solar fail together in winter** (dunkelflaute); a Salish-Sea plan that leans on them is planning to be cold. ([Electric Insights](https://reports.electricinsights.co.uk/?p=2314))
- **Tidal is intermittent-but-scheduled** (four slack tides a day, zero output at each) and sited in orca hunting water, where a legal-person guardian can veto it.
- **Hydro is firm but shrinking and morally mortgaged** to the fish; every drop of firm winter power is arguably a salmon's.
- **Geothermal's firm heat is under the mountains, not the city.**
- **Storage is real but young, and round-trip losses mean you must over-generate to store.**
- **Everything electric competes with everything else electric at the peak hour** — heat, the Dark Garden, medical loads, the Weave's relays.
- **Cold kills quietly and unequally** (Section 7). A grid that is 99% reliable still has a 1% that lands on a specific frail body in a specific cold flat.

There is no arrangement of these that yields a frictionless warm winter. The persuasive version of this world is the one that says so.

## 7. Energy poverty: the limit that lands on a person

Rationing fairness is `research/scarcity-and-hard-tradeoffs.md`'s subject; the *physical* fact of energy poverty is this brief's. Cold homes are a body count. In the UK, fuel poverty drives on the order of **~5,000 excess winter deaths a year**, concentrated in the old, disabled, chronically ill, and poor — the "heat or eat" households ([End Fuel Poverty Coalition](https://www.endfuelpoverty.org.uk/4950-excess-winter-deaths-caused-by-cold-homes-last-winter/)). Two engineering-flavored truths this world must hold:

- **The upfront-cost gate.** The heat pump and the insulation that would *end* a household's energy poverty cost thousands to install. A market leaves exactly the people who most need them unable to buy them. So in this world the retrofit is a **commons build** — installed as public infrastructure, financed collectively, the way you'd pave a street — not a purchase. The First Refusal (no metric turned against a person, no manufactured scarcity) makes warmth-through-capital-access a public work, not a credit score.
- **Efficiency is justice, not aesthetics.** The single most equitable kilowatt-hour is the one an insulated wall means you never needed. Deep retrofit of the *existing, kept* building stock (canon: old buildings kept honest, salvaged, not demolished) is simultaneously the Second Refusal (no erasure to replace) and the best anti-energy-poverty program there is. The salvaged-steel, mycelium-infill, kept-old-building city is *also* the well-insulated one.

## 8. Disconfirming evidence & failure modes

The honest record of what these ideas do when tried:

1. **The greenest grids have the least headroom and the worst optics for the next step.** Because City Light is already ~88% hydro, its cheap decarbonization is *spent*; the marginal clean megawatt is costly and contested, and the temptation is to lean harder on the dams — i.e., on the fish. Starting clean is not the same as staying clean while also giving the rivers back. ([Seattle City Light](https://www.seattle.gov/city-light/energy))
2. **Local tidal died once already, of cost.** Admiralty Inlet was *fully FERC-licensed* and cancelled anyway when the money moved (Snohomish PUD, 2014). ([Snohomish PUD](https://www.snopud.com/community-environment/our-energy-future/projects/research/tidal-energy/tidal-project-overview/)) A world that assumes tidal "just works" is ignoring the actual grave of the actual project.
3. **Offshore wind was killed partly by the good guys.** The West Coast Wind Energy Areas were rescinded in 2025 after opposition that included **tribal advocates and fishing communities** — a reminder that clean-energy siting can itself be a form of extraction from coastal and Indigenous peoples, and that "renewable" is not a synonym for "just." ([OPB](https://www.opb.org/article/2025/07/30/oregon-wind-energy-wea-development-coos-bay-brookings/))
4. **Marine heat pumps fight the salmon seawall.** A seawater district-heat intake screens and entrains water — the same juvenile Chinook the canon seawall's glass-block corridor exists to protect. The heating solution and the salmon-restoration solution can be in physical conflict at the same pipe. Restored-shoreline infrastructure is not automatically compatible with itself.
5. **The nuclear alternative's own record is cost and collapse.** The clearest disconfirmation of the *ecomodernist* answer (§8, steelman) is nuclear's build record: **NuScale's flagship SMR was cancelled in November 2023** as costs ballooned $5.3B → $9.2B (74%), pushing power to $89/MWh with too few buyers ([E&E — NuScale cancels](https://www.eenews.net/articles/nuscale-cancels-first-of-a-kind-nuclear-project-as-costs-surge/)); **Vogtle** came in **~7 years late and ~$35B** ([PowerMag — Vogtle delays](https://www.powermag.com/another-delay-for-new-unit-at-vogtle-nuclear-plant/)). "Just build reactors" has a build-time and a budget the salmon rivers and the ratepayers may not survive.
6. **Seasonal certainty is not universal.** Marstal, Drammen, and Iceland all work in specific geologies and social settings (Danish co-op tradition, Norwegian fjords, Icelandic volcanism). Lifting a template across contexts is how good pilots become failed rollouts. The Salish Sea has *analogues* to each, not copies.
7. **Storing your way through a dunkelflaute means over-building, which costs materials** — iron, copper, concrete — whose mining is itself extraction. A post-extraction grid that needs vast storage has to confront that the storage, too, is dug out of somewhere. (This is the AI-confession knot in mineral form; see provenance.)

## 9. Steelmanned opponents (three camps, disagreeing with each other)

Per the Protocol — opponents at their strongest, pointed at *each other*, so no single opposing camp becomes its own echo. The energy question has an unusually clean three-way disagreement.

**A. The ecomodernist / nuclear-abundance case (Breakthrough Institute; the X-energy–Energy Northwest "Cascade" project; TerraPower).** *At its strongest, and it is strong.* Managed scarcity is a regressive comfort of the already-warm; energy is a bigger share of a poor household's budget, so rationing it hurts them most, and aestheticizing a cold winter is a cruelty (`CANON.md`'s own "aestheticizing slowness is a quiet cruelty" — the same nerve). The moral path is not degrowth but **firm, land-light, weather-blind clean power** — advanced nuclear — so *no one has to be cold and no river has to be dammed.* And crucially, it answers this brief's two hardest problems directly:
  - **It runs through the dunkelflaute.** A reactor doesn't care that it's dark and calm for a week.
  - **It's the fish's ally, not enemy.** Firm nuclear baseload is the strongest argument for taking *more* dams out — replace the firm hydro with firm fission and you can spill the rivers for salmon *and* keep the lights on. The ecomodernist can say, truthfully, that they are the *pro-salmon* energy position.
  - It's local and real: **Energy Northwest's "Cascade" project** plans up to **12 X-energy Xe-100 SMRs (~960 MW) north of Richland, WA**, first module targeted ~2030, Amazon-funded ([Utility Dive — Cascade](https://www.utilitydive.com/news/washington-nuclear-facility-smrs-cascade-amazon-modular/802967/); [PowerMag — Cascade](https://www.powermag.com/amazon-unveils-cascade-energy-northwests-xe-100-smr-project-targeting-construction-by-2030/)). The **Xe-100 is a high-temperature gas reactor** — it makes *process heat*, not just electricity, which is directly usable for **district heating**, the exact winter load this brief is about. And **TerraPower's Natrium** pairs a reactor with **molten-salt thermal storage**, so it can *load-follow* — bank heat when demand is low, surge when the dunkelflaute bites. That's a reactor engineered for precisely the intermittency-matching problem a Salish winter poses. *The strongest version of this world's opponent is not a caricature; it is a serious, salmon-friendly, dark-proof answer. Canon is in fact silent on nuclear — neither `CANON.md` nor `AGENTS.md` names it — so what would refuse the reactor here is not a stated taboo but the Third Refusal's suspicion of concentrated, merchant-owned power (an Amazon-funded SMR fleet is exactly that shape), and that instinct has to earn its position against a genuinely strong case.*

**B. The renewables-plus-storage / anti-nuclear case (post-NuScale realism; degrowth-adjacent).** *Directly opposes A.* Nuclear is a fantasy of firmness that **cannot be built in time or on budget** — NuScale collapsed, Vogtle ran $35B and 7 years late (§8) — so betting the winter on reactors that don't exist yet is betting the poor will be warm *someday.* The same capital buys far more *actual* firm capacity today as efficiency + heat pumps + district heat + multi-day storage + retained hydro, deployed now, owned in common, with no waste stream, no meltdown tail-risk, and no dependence on an Amazon-funded merchant plant (note the ownership: canon's commons vs. a hyperscaler's private reactor fleet). Warmth deferred to 2035 is warmth denied to this January's frail elder.

**C. The reliability engineer / thermal-realist (the darkest steelman, and the one the Tone mandate demands).** *Opposes both A and B.* Both the nuclear optimist and the renewables optimist are hand-waving the dunkelflaute. In the real December 2024 UK dark-doldrums, what actually kept people alive was **dispatchable gas at 73% of supply** ([Electric Insights](https://reports.electricinsights.co.uk/?p=2314)); in the February 2025 Texas cold snap it was record gas generation. Every real grid that has tried to go fully firm-clean still keeps a fossil backstop for the killer week — because the alternative is a body count. A world that *forbids* the backstop on principle is not choosing purity over convenience; it is **choosing, in advance, that some number of vulnerable people will freeze in the worst week of the worst winter**, and it should have the honesty to say so rather than to assume the storage will hold. This is the position canon most wants to refuse and least gets to dismiss: the honest cost of "no fossil extraction, ever" may be paid in specific cold deaths, and the world has to look at that and choose it anyway, out loud, or it isn't telling the truth.

The three cannot all be right. A dissents from managed scarcity; B dissents from unbuildable firmness; C dissents from the whole premise that clean firmness is achievable in the killer week at all. The disconfirming section (§8) is the referee: nuclear has a build-record of collapse, renewables have a dunkelflaute, and rationing has a winter body count. There is no camp whose bill comes due for free.

## 10. Wildcard lens — cybernetics (Beer, Cybersyn, the Viable System Model)

*Rolled ungameably: nanosecond clock reading at the moment of the roll was 867056000; digit sum = 32 → 3+2 = **5** → cybernetics. Not the lens I'd have reached for (thermodynamics is the obvious one for energy); cybernetics turns out to be the more useful and less obvious frame, because the Salish winter grid's core problem is not generating power — it's **coordinating a diverse, intermittent, distributed system in real time without a market and without a central planner.**)*

Stafford Beer built **Project Cybersyn** for Allende's Chile (1971–73): a real-time cybernetic system to coordinate a whole economy's resources through feedback rather than through prices or top-down command, organized by the **Viable System Model** — nested, semi-autonomous units, each managing itself, coupled by information flows, with only genuine exceptions escalating upward. Map it onto a Salish winter grid and three things fall out:

- **Homeostasis over optimization.** The old grid maximized efficiency (single cheapest source, minimal slack). Beer's insight — and canon's "firebreak / slack is a civic virtue" (`research/power-and-bounded-inequality.md`) — is that a *viable* system holds slack and diversity precisely so it can absorb shocks. The Salish grid should be **deliberately over-built and diverse** (tidal + hydro + storage + district heat + geothermal), tuned for ride-through, not for the lowest average cost. Redundancy is not waste; it's the reason the lights stay on in the dunkelflaute.
- **Algedonic signals — the pain channel.** Cybersyn had "algedonic" alerts: signals that bypass the normal hierarchy when something crosses a threshold of pain. A winter energy commons needs exactly this — **a fast, non-market channel that screams when a specific household's heat is failing** (the ventilator, the frail elder), routing warmth to them *before* the slow assembly convenes. This is the technical form of "no metric turned against a person": the system measures kilowatt-hours and temperatures, but the *purpose* of the measurement is to find the person in pain and reach them, never to rank them. Measurement pointed at care, not extraction — canon's exact line, built as a control loop.
- **Recursion — the neighbourhood as a viable system.** The VSM nests: each block-cluster (the "tender"-scale unit from `research/health-and-care.md`) manages its own micro-grid, storage, and district-heat loop autonomously, coupled to the city grid, coupled to the bioregion. No node holds the whole graph — which rhymes exactly with the Weave (canon: "reach is literally not computable"). A cybernetic grid and a store-and-forward network share a spine: **distributed, forgetful, un-centralizable, resilient because no single point can fail the whole.** The honest caution Beer's own project supplies: Cybersyn was **cut down by a coup before it could prove itself** — a reminder that the binding constraint on a good coordination system is usually political power, not the control theory. The grid's real threat is who captures it, not whether the feedback loops close.

## 11. Historical + non-Western precedent (both, as required)

**Historical: Iceland's geothermal district-heating rollout (1908 → today).** A cold, dark, sparsely-populated island moved from imported-oil heat to **~90% of all homes heated by geothermal district heat (Reykjavik ~100%)** — beginning with **one farmer in 1908** laying a pipe from a hot spring to his house, formalized by municipal utilities in the **1930s** (Laugaveita's 14 shallow wells), and built out over decades into a national network that has saved on the order of **135 Mt CO₂ (1944–2022).** ([Green by Iceland — 90% geothermal](https://www.islandsstofa.is/en/news/green-by-iceland---90-of-house-heating-in-iceland/); [VITO — Reykjavik](https://vito.be/en/geothermal-energy/aardwarmte-toepassingen/reykjavik-iceland)) The lessons that transfer: (1) **district heat is a decades-long municipal public-works project, not a gadget** — it started with a farmer and took a century; (2) **it was publicly/municipally driven**, matching canon's commons ownership; (3) a cold, dark, marginal place can *fully* decarbonize heat if the resource is under its feet. The Salish caveat, kept honest: Iceland's resource is uniquely shallow volcanic heat; the Puget Lowland's equivalent is *marine and waste heat* (Drammen/False Creek), not Icelandic geothermal — same public-works *model*, different physical source.

**Non-Western / Indigenous: Coast Salish winter-village thermal architecture.** This is Coast Salish land, and the deepest local precedent for surviving a dark wet winter on little energy is Coast Salish building science — which I name and cite and then step back from, per the ethics of borrowing (`AGENTS.md`); this is publicly documented material culture, not sacred ceremonial knowledge, and it is not mine to embellish.
  - The **shed-roof cedar plank longhouse (lelum̓)** was the permanent *winter* residence of Puget Sound Coast Salish peoples — the Duwamish, Suquamish, Muckleshoot, Puyallup, Snoqualmie, Tulalip, Nisqually and neighboring nations — built in long rows facing the water, each house holding **100–200 people**, with **individual family fires in booths along the walls** rather than one central furnace. ([Vancouver Heritage Foundation — Coast Salish plank houses](https://www.vancouverheritagefoundation.org/house-styles/traditional-coast-salish-plank-houses/); [Snuneymuxw / SD68 — shed-roof plank lelum̓](https://nlpslearns.sd68.bc.ca/wp-content/uploads/Coast-Salish-Shed-Roof-Plank-Houses.pdf)) The design logic is *distributed heating* — many small fires warming many small zones — plus dense **communal occupancy** and cedar's insulating plank walls.
  - **The warmth inside that house was not shared equally, and pretending it was would be exactly the noble-savage flattening `AGENTS.md` forbids.** Coast Salish winter villages were rank-stratified: as the ethnographer Wayne Suttles documented (*Coast Salish Essays*, 1987) and the anthropologist of the Coast Salish Bruce Granville Miller and colleagues carry forward (*Be of Good Mind: Essays on the Coast Salish*, 2007), these were societies of high-status families, commoners, and **hereditary slaves** — war captives and their descendants, held and traded, whose enslavement passed to their children (documented in detail by Leland Donald, *Aboriginal Slavery on the Northwest Coast of North America*, 1997). Position along the longhouse walls tracked that order: the high-status family took the warm, defensible interior end; low-status kin and the enslaved took the cold ends by the doors. "Bodies are heaters" is true, but *whose* body warmed *whose* booth was a fact of rank, not of solidarity. The communal-heating architecture is real and worth learning from; the egalitarian gloss on it is not the source's, it would be mine, and I refuse to add it.
  - Upriver, the **pithouse (sqémél)** — earth-covered, dug partly into the ground, using the soil's thermal mass — was the well-insulated winter form among interior and upriver Coast Salish (e.g. Stó:lō) communities. ([Sq'éwlets — ancient village](http://digitalsqewlets.ca/sqwelqwel/archaeology-archeologie/ancient_village-ancien-eng.php))

The principles that a Salish-Sea future can learn *from the source and credit to it*: **zone your heat, don't centralize it** (the booth fire and the plank house are §9's recursion and §2.2's district-loop-per-cluster, five centuries early); **dense, communal occupancy is an energy strategy** that cuts hard against the isolated single-family-home-per-furnace model the fossil city assumed — but the future has to *supply the equal warmth the longhouse did not*, because the design lesson is worth taking and the rank order that decided who froze is not; **build with the season, into the ground, out of what the place gives** (earth mass, cedar). Sovereignty leads here: these are living peoples' technologies on their own land, and the future grid is *theirs to lead*, not a motif to borrow. The appropriation knot: an AI trained on scraped text describing Coast Salish winter architecture as a template for a warm future is standing exactly where the ethics-of-borrowing warning points — so I cite the nations and named scholars, keep the hard norm (slavery, rank) on the page rather than sanding it into a communal idyll, take only the publicly-documented material principle, and leave the ceremonial and the sacred where they belong, with the people who hold them.

## 12. Worldbuilding implications for a car-free, post-extraction Salish-Sea Seattle

Scene-ready:

1. **The city is warmed by its own waste and its own cold sea.** A neighbourhood's heat comes from a district loop that heat-pumps warmth out of the sewer main and the deep water of Elliott Bay (real: False Creek + Drammen) — so the shower you took this morning is warming your neighbour's flat tonight, at 300% efficiency, and the "power plant" is a quiet building the size of a corner shop, publicly owned, selling heat at cost. A scene: a kid on a school tour putting a hand on the warm outflow pipe and being told *that's the whole street's baths coming back to us.*

2. **Winter is last spring's meltwater, kept.** Under a former parking lot sits an insulated water pit (real: Marstal, 75,000 m³) charged in the long days of summer with hydro-and-tidal surplus, drawn down through the dark months. People speak of the heat-store's level the way they speak of a root cellar's stock — a thing you laid up and are now spending, visible on a public gauge in the square.

3. **The tide is a clock you heat by, and the whales can stop it.** A phased tidal array in Admiralty Inlet, Tacoma Narrows, and Rosario Strait (real sites) feeds the grid on a lunar schedule everyone can read — tide-tables posted like weather, "high water at 3:14, the Inlet's running." But the orca guardians (canon: legal persons) hold a standing veto, and one year a pod's hunting ground overlaps a turbine field and the array is *powered down for the season by law* — clean power switched off for the whales, and the city goes a colder winter for it, and argues about whether it was right. The salmon-seawall intake and the district-heat intake are found to be fighting over the same juvenile Chinook, and an engineer has to redesign a screen or shut a heat loop.

4. **The dam question is the salmon question is the heat question — one argument, not three.** At the seasonal energy assembly, the guardians for the Duwamish and the Salish Sea can move to spill more water or draw a reservoir down for a migration — and everyone in the room knows that vote is *also* a vote to make January colder, because that water was firm winter power. The most sacred ecological act and the warmest house are traded against each other openly, every low-flow year. Nobody gets to pretend the fish are free.

5. **Heat is installed, not bought.** No household purchases its heat pump or its insulation; the retrofit crews come block by block as a public work (canon: old buildings kept, salvaged, honest — here also *insulated*), because the upfront cost is the exact gate that would leave the poorest coldest. A scene: the crew arguing about which century-old kept building gets the deep retrofit this year, and the tender flagging the flat with the ventilator-dependent elder to the front of the queue — the algedonic signal (§10) made human.

6. **The grid has a pain channel, and it overrides the queue.** When a specific home's temperature or a specific medical load starts failing, a fast non-market signal routes warmth to it *ahead* of the slow assembly (real principle: Cybersyn's algedonic alerts; canon's "measurement for care, not extraction"). The system counts kilowatt-hours and degrees, but only ever to find the person in the cold and reach them — never to rank who deserves it. Someone whose job is watching that channel sits up at 3 a.m. because a gauge in a flat three neighbourhoods over crossed a line.

7. **The dunkelflaute has a name and a drill.** A dead-calm, freezing, sunless high-pressure week is a known, named event the city rehearses for — "the still dark," the days when tide is the only thing moving and the iron-air banks (real: Form Energy, 100-hour storage) are drawn down hour by hour on a public countdown. There's a culture around it: the shared warm halls (echoing the communal longhouse, §11 — bodies as heaters), the dimming of the Dark Garden beds to keep homes above survivable, the older people who've been through worse ones telling the newcomers it holds, usually.

8. **The reactor across the mountains is a live, unsettled argument.** The world has *not* comfortably resolved the nuclear question. Somewhere east — the real Cascade/Xe-100 corridor near Richland — SMRs run or are being built, and whether the Salish commons should draw firm, dark-proof, salmon-friendly nuclear heat down a tie-line, or refuse it as another concentrated merchant-owned extraction, is a *recurring, unresolved civic fight*, not a settled taboo. A character can hold the ecomodernist position with dignity and not be a villain. The strongest version of the opponent lives in this world, warm and unrefuted.

9. **Some winters, the stack doesn't close, and the world says so.** The layered system usually holds. Some killer weeks it doesn't, and then it becomes `research/scarcity-and-hard-tradeoffs.md`'s rationing, and then, sometimes, despite the pain channel and the care web, a frail body in a cold flat dies of the gap — named, eulogized, not pretended away (canon's honest-failure clause). The eulogy says *she was cold, and the still dark was long that year, and we did not reach her in time.* The world does not tell this as a triumph.

10. **The old people are the heat experts.** The ones who set the winter energy plan are disproportionately those who've lived inside a hard energy cap their whole lives — the disabled (spoons; `research/scarcity-and-hard-tradeoffs.md`'s crip-knowledge point) and the elders who remember the fossil interregnum's cold. Competence, not sentiment: the person who runs the still-dark drill is the one who's survived the most of them.
