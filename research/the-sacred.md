# The Sacred — a research brief

*Domain: what people hold sacred when it is not money — meaning, ritual, awe, mourning, the numinous — in a car-free, post-extraction Seattle that refuses a state religion but still has to bury its dead and tell its children what a life is for.*

The through-line: **the sacred is not a belief you can install; it is a practice a community does together in time.** The societies that hold meaning best don't agree on doctrine — they agree on what to *do* at a birth, a death, a harvest, a solstice. That is good news for a world with no state church and a hundred kinds of people. It is also the danger, because the same machinery that binds a grieving town around a grave is the machinery that lit the cathedral of light over Nuremberg. This brief tries to keep both in view. The First Refusal governs it throughout: **measurement is fine; the metric turned against a person is not.** A calendar of shared hours is measurement. A leaderboard of who prayed hardest is the thing we refuse.

A note on standing, up front, per the ethics of borrowing in `AGENTS.md`: this brief is written by an AI trained on scraped text that may include sacred knowledge taken without consent, and it is describing the sacred on **Coast Salish land**. Where the material is theirs — the First Salmon Ceremony, specific ceremony, specific song — I name the nation and the source and **step back**; I do not reproduce it or hand it to a settler civic calendar. The sacred life this brief actually designs is the *newcomer's* sacred life: what the un-homed, post-Christian, post-everything arrivals build for themselves, next to and deferring to a sovereign Indigenous ceremonial world they do not get to join by moving in.

## 1. The sacred is a *practice*, not a *creed* — the Durkheim baseline

Émile Durkheim's *The Elementary Forms of Religious Life* (1912) gives the load-bearing definition. Religion, he argued, is not fundamentally about gods; it is the community's division of the world into the **sacred** (things set apart and treated with reverence) and the **profane** (the ordinary run of work and appetite), maintained by **rites** performed together. The engine that charges the sacred is what he called **collective effervescence**: the surplus of energy and unity a group feels when it gathers and acts in unison — a force so real to participants that they attribute it to a god, when its true source, Durkheim says, is *the society itself, experienced as something larger than any member.* Religion, on this reading, is a society worshipping itself in disguise. (Primary: Émile Durkheim, *The Elementary Forms of Religious Life*, 1912 — the sacred/profane division in the Introduction and Book I, collective effervescence in Book II; cited here from the Karen E. Fields translation, Free Press, 1995.)

Two consequences matter for a world with no state religion.

- **You can have the sacred without agreeing on the supernatural.** If the sacred is made by *doing rites together*, then a plural, unbelieving, post-extraction city can still manufacture the real thing — awe, belonging, the set-apart — provided it actually gathers and acts in common. Belief is optional; assembly is not.
- **The sacred is inherently powerful, therefore inherently dangerous.** The same effervescence that consecrates a funeral consecrated the Nuremberg rally. Durkheim's insight cuts both ways, and Section 7 pays that bill.

**The tension this brief refuses to resolve:** a society that worships itself has no vantage point outside itself from which to be judged. The transcendent, whatever else it is, is a *check* — a claim that some things are true whether or not the assembly cheers. Strip it out and you may get a community with no brake. Hold this; the opponents in Section 6 press exactly here.

## 2. Awe is measurable, prosocial, and it shrinks the self

The strongest secular case that you don't need a church to reach the numinous comes from the science of **awe** — Dacher Keltner's lab at Berkeley and collaborators.

- **The small self.** Across five studies (N = 2,078), Piff, Dietze, Feinberg, Stancato & Keltner (2015) found that awe — the emotion at "perceptually vast stimuli that transcend current frames of reference" — produces a **diminished sense of self** ("the small self") and, through it, **more generous and prosocial behavior**: people who had just felt awe shared more, helped more, and reported feeling part of something larger. ([Piff et al., *JPSP* 2015, PDF](https://www.apa.org/pubs/journals/releases/psp-pspi0000018.pdf); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25984788/))
- **Self-transcendent emotions bind us.** Stellar, Keltner et al. (2017) group awe with compassion and gratitude as **self-transcendent emotions** that "shift attention away from the self" and glue individuals into cooperative collectives. ([Stellar et al., *Emotion Review* 2017](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1754073916684557))
- **The overview effect.** Yaden et al. (2016) analyzed astronaut accounts of seeing Earth whole and found a reliable signature: awe with self-transcendent qualities, a reorganization of values toward unity, fragility, and ecological concern, often lifelong. You don't need orbit; you need the *vastness that overwhelms your frame.* ([Yaden et al., *Psychology of Consciousness* 2016](https://www.ovid.com/journals/pctrp/fulltext/10.1037/cns0000086~the-overview-effect-awe-and-self-transcendent-experience-in); [Penn Today](https://penntoday.upenn.edu/news/penn-psychologists-study-intense-awe-astronauts-feel-viewing-earth-space))

For our Seattle this is gold and cheaply available: the sea, the salmon-lit corridor under the seawall, old-growth remnants, the bioluminescent Dark Garden, storm and star. Awe is the one sacred raw material a restored-but-fragile coastal bioregion has in industrial surplus. **The design move is to build the city so its citizens are routinely ambushed by vastness** — but awe also produces *submission*, and a small self is a submitting self, which is the whole reason Section 7b holds the line on where the vastness is allowed to point.

## 3. Monastic timekeeping: the sacred as an attention discipline

*(This brief's assignment leans on the monastic material; here is the load-bearing version.)*

The Rule of St. Benedict (6th c.) organizes the monastery around the **Divine Office** / **Liturgy of the Hours** — eight fixed prayer times across the day and night (Matins, Lauds, Prime, Terce, Sext, None, Vespers, Compline), at which the monks **drop whatever work is engaging their attention** and gather. Benedict gives the principle bluntly: *"nothing is to be preferred to the work of God"* (Ch. 43), and distributes the 150 Psalms across the week so the whole community moves through the same text on the same schedule. (Primary: *The Rule of St. Benedict*, chs. 8–20 and 43; a modern scholarly edition is Terrence Kardong, *Benedict's Rule: A Translation and Commentary*, Liturgical Press, 1996.)

Strip the theology and what remains is a **technology of collective attention**: the community agrees, in advance, on *when everyone will pay attention to the same thing*, and it interrupts productivity to do so. This is the exact structural inverse of the attention economy — and the exact structural cousin of the Weave (`CANON.md`), which refuses the scroll and the rank. The monastery does not farm attention; it *tends* it, on a shared clock, and the interruption of work is the point, not a bug.

The same design recurs far outside Christianity, which is the argument against calling it a church-thing: **Islamic ṣalāt** fixes five daily prayers by the sun (dawn, midday, afternoon, sunset, night), stopping the market five times a day; **Jewish** practice fixes three (shacharit, mincha, maariv). The recurring human pattern is *fixed communal hours that puncture the working day* — a public metronome of the sacred. A car-free Salish city already has the hardware: the **hand-flagged tram**, the **4pm ferry**, the tide table. The sacred hour and the transit schedule can be the same bell.

Jonathan Crary's *24/7: Late Capitalism and the Ends of Sleep* (2013) is the modern indictment that makes this urgent: capitalism's frontier is the abolition of any time — sleep, prayer, the fallow hour — that cannot be monetized. The monastic hours are the oldest defended perimeter against exactly that. ([Crary, *24/7*, Verso](https://www.versobooks.com/products/1570-24-7))

## 4. Death and the numinous when there is no state church — the working models

The assignment's hard core: *a world that refuses a state religion still has to answer death.* Real, recent, secular-compatible answers exist, and one of them is **local to Seattle.**

**4a. Human composting — the local sacred technology.** Katrina Spade's **Recompose** (Seattle) grew out of her Urban Death Project (2014) into the first human-composting funeral home in the US, opening near Seattle in December 2020 after **Washington became the first state to legalize natural organic reduction in 2019/2020**. The body is laid in a vessel with wood chips, alfalfa, and straw; over ~30 days it becomes roughly a cubic yard of soil returned to the family or to conservation land. Thirteen more states have since followed. The flagship's 31 vessels sit in a hexagonal steel frame designed by Olson Kundig — a deliberately *beautiful, non-denominational, urban* death-place. ([Seattle Times](https://www.seattletimes.com/life/recompose-the-first-human-compositing-funeral-home-in-the-u-s-is-now-open-for-business/); [GeekWire 2025](https://www.geekwire.com/2025/inside-recompose-where-the-human-composting-startup-is-ready-to-grow-its-formula-beyond-seattle/); underlying law: WA SB 5001, Natural Organic Reduction, 2019) This is a *made* sacred: it answers "what happens to my body" without a heaven, and it fits the world's ecology (Refusal #2 — the body is not waste to be discarded but a name returned to the soil). It belongs in the world's history as a real Seattle root.

**4b. Continuing bonds — grief without "closure."** Klass, Silverman & Nickman's *Continuing Bonds* (1996) overturned the 20th-century "detach and move on" model of grief. Their finding: healthy mourners **do not sever the bond with the dead; they renegotiate it** into an ongoing, changed relationship — talking to the dead, keeping objects, consulting them. This is the psychological license for a secular ritual life organized around *keeping the dead present*, which maps precisely onto Refusal #2 (*"Names are kept"*). (Primary: Dennis Klass, Phyllis R. Silverman & Steven L. Nickman, eds., *Continuing Bonds: New Understandings of Grief*, Taylor & Francis, 1996.)

**4c. Death Cafés — deliberative mortality.** Jon Underwood's **Death Café** (first held in his East London basement, September 2011, tea and cake, no agenda, just talk about dying) — modeled on Swiss sociologist Bernard Crettaz's *cafés mortels* — has run **~23,000+ times in 97 countries.** No doctrine, no clergy, no upsell; a structured, recurring, communal confrontation with mortality. Underwood, a Buddhist, died suddenly at 44 in 2017; the form outlived him, which is the point — it belongs to no one. ([What is Death Cafe](https://deathcafe.com/what/); [Washington Post 2017](https://www.washingtonpost.com/news/acts-of-faith/wp/2017/07/10/the-founder-of-death-cafe-has-died-but-his-movement-to-accept-the-inevitable-end-of-life-will-live-on/))

Together these three sketch a **non-doctrinal death culture**: a beautiful place to become soil, a permission to keep loving the dead, and a recurring table where the living rehearse their own ending. None requires a shared god. All require a shared *practice* — back to Durkheim.

## 5. Precedents that decenter the Anglophone default

**5a. Non-Western precedent — Japan: high practice, low belief, division of ritual labor.** The proverb runs: *"born Shinto, married Christian, die Buddhist."* Most Japanese people do not claim exclusive membership in one religion; religion there is defined by **rite and practice, not doctrine.** There is an accepted division of labor — Shinto shrines bless the newborn and the New Year (life-affirming thresholds), Buddhist temples handle death and the ancestors. **Obon** (midsummer) welcomes the ancestral dead home for a few days each year. Surveys routinely find low rates of professed *belief* alongside high rates of *participation* — people visit the shrine, tend the grave, keep the household altar, without "believing" in a way a Western pollster can capture. (Scholarly: Ian Reader, *Religion in Contemporary Japan*, University of Hawai'i Press, 1991 — the "belonging without believing," practice-not-creed reading; on ritual division of labor across the life cycle, Ian Reader & George J. Tanabe, *Practically Religious*, 1998.) **This is the single most useful model for our world:** a functioning, meaning-rich, ritual-dense society that is *not organized by a creed and not policed by a state church* — belonging-by-doing, plural by default. (Keep the nuance: Japan's system also carries State Shinto's 20th-century weaponization into nationalism — the cautionary case is baked into the same tradition. See Section 7.)

**5b. Historical precedent — the French Revolution's manufactured religions, and why they died.** When the Revolution tried to *design a state religion from scratch* it failed twice, fast. The atheistic **Cult of Reason** (1793) never took; Robespierre replaced it with the deistic **Cult of the Supreme Being**, staged a grand Festival on the Champ de Mars (8 June 1794) with himself as de facto pontiff — and the sight of Robespierre playing high priest helped get him guillotined six weeks later. The cult **died with its patron**; Napoleon banned both in 1802. The parallel **Republican Calendar** (ten-day weeks, renamed months, abolition of Sunday) was imposed to erase Christian time and was itself abolished in 1806 — people would not live by a clock handed down by decree. (Scholarly: Mona Ozouf, *Festivals and the French Revolution*, Harvard University Press, 1988 — the definitive study of the Revolution's engineered civic cults and their failure to take root.) **The lesson for our world is sharp and disconfirming: you cannot legislate a civil religion from the top.** The sacred that sticks is *grown from below and un-owned* (Death Café, human composting, the Japanese division of labor); the sacred that is *decreed to serve the state* is dead on arrival and smells of the tyrant. This is Refusal #1 in the register of meaning: a sacred *ranked and administered from above* is the counterfeit.

**5c. Coast Salish sovereignty — named, and left sovereign.** After the 1974 *Boldt* decision restored treaty-guaranteed fishing rights, Coast Salish nations — among them the **Tulalip, Lummi, Swinomish, Upper Skagit, and Stillaguamish** — revived the **First Salmon Ceremony**. I do not describe its practice, and what little I state here I relay only from what these nations have themselves published in their own outlets — the Tulalip and Lummi accounts of the revival's cultural pillars, and of youth participation framed as tribal healing — adding nothing of my own. ([Lummi Nation Celebrates First Salmon — NW Treaty Tribes](https://nwtreatytribes.org/lummi-nation-celebrates-first-salmon/); [The Salmon Ceremony — Tulalip News](https://tulalipnews.com/2022/06/15/the-salmon-ceremony/)) It is not raw material for a settler civic calendar; it is sovereign practice on sovereign land, and its meaning is inseparable from the treaty rights and the nations that hold it. The relevant fact for *our* worldbuilding is a structural one: **in this Seattle the deepest, oldest, most continuous sacred life is Indigenous and sovereign, and the newcomers' invented rituals live alongside it as guests, not as its replacement or its flattening.**

## 6. Rule Zero — the opposing view, steelmanned (and two opponents who disagree with each other)

**Opponent A — Tom Holland: secular values are Christianity's afterlife.** In *Dominion: How the Christian Revolution Remade the World* (Little, Brown, 2019), Holland argues that the West's supposedly secular values — human dignity, equality, care for the weak, universal human rights, even the Marxist concern for the oppressed — are **not self-evident products of reason** but the specific, contingent inheritance of Christianity, whose most radical claim was that a tortured slave is the image of God. His hard question: *can a society keep the values after abandoning the beliefs the values grew from?* Call it, in this brief's shorthand, living in the shadow of a corpse: a project like ours — awe without transcendence, ethics without a god, a sacred we *manufacture* — may be spending down inherited moral capital it can no longer replenish, with the account eventually running dry. (Primary: Holland, *Dominion*, 2019, esp. the closing chapters on secular humanism as Christian residue.) **Steelmanned:** the Sunday Assembly data (Section 8) is real evidence he's onto something — *engineered* sacred community, absent a transcendent claim that outranks the members' preferences, tends not to hold. A world that refuses a state religion may be quietly living on the moral savings of the religions it refused, and may not notice the balance falling until a genuine crisis asks the sacred to demand a real sacrifice.

**Opponent B — the anti-sacred left, who disagrees with Holland: Ronald Purser and the Durkheim-of-fascism read.** Purser's *McMindfulness* (2019) argues the opposite of Holland: the danger isn't *losing* the sacred, it's *manufacturing* it. When you extract a contemplative practice from its community (*sangha*) and ethics and repackage it as a private technique, you get a **tool of self-pacification** that "helps people adjust to the very conditions that caused their problems" and "perpetuates neoliberalism by individualizing" what was collective. On this view a designed civic sacred — a curated awe, a scheduled bell of attention — is not spiritual capital; it is *soma*, an opiate that defuses the anger that should have built a picket line. ([*McMindfulness* review — Secular Buddhist Network](https://secularbuddhistnetwork.org/a-review-of-ron-pursers-mcmindfulness-how-mindfulness-became-the-new-capitalist-spirituality/); [LARB interview](https://lareviewofbooks.org/blog/interviews/panopticon-mind-talking-ronald-purser-mindfulness-today/)) **The two opponents genuinely collide:** Holland says our sacred is *too thin* (no transcendent brake, will fail); Purser says any institutionalized sacred is *too dangerous* (it will be captured to pacify). A world can't fully satisfy both — more transcendence for Holland is more capture-surface for Purser. The honest position is to sit in that vise, not to pretend a design resolves it.

## 7. Disconfirming evidence & failure modes (the tone mandate, paid in full)

**7a. Engineered secular congregations tend to dissolve.** The **Sunday Assembly** ("church for atheists," founded London 2013) spread to ~70 congregations at its peak and had collapsed to **~22 by 2023.** The autopsy is brutal and specific: running a congregation "basically meant putting on a big show on a regular basis" — booking bands, finding speakers, setting up chairs — with no transcendent obligation to sustain the labor when volunteers burned out; COVID finished off many. The recurring diagnosis: *"people don't merely need community — they need an energizing purpose,"* and community assembled around the *absence* of a claim struggles to generate one. ([Religion News 2021](https://religionnews.com/2021/09/24/the-sunday-assembly-hopes-to-organize-a-godless-future-its-not-easy/)) This is the single hardest fact for our thesis. **Any sacred institution the Salish builds must answer: what keeps people setting up the chairs the fifth year, in the rain, when it's a chore?**

**7b. The sacred is the master technology of fascism.** Durkheim's collective effervescence has no built-in moral valence. The **Nuremberg rallies** manufactured it deliberately — synchronized marches, chanting, Speer's "cathedral of light," flag-consecration rites — precisely to "supplant traditional religion," dissolve individual agency "into collective ecstasy," and produce leader-worship in an atomized population. Awe produces the *small self* (Section 2) — and a small self is a **submitting** self. Everything this brief recommends for consecrating a funeral can consecrate a Führer. ([Documentation Center Nazi Party Rally Grounds](https://museums.nuernberg.de/documentation-center/nazi-party-rallies/what-were-the-nazi-party-rallies); scholarly frame: Emilio Gentile, *The Sacralization of Politics in Fascist Italy*, Harvard University Press, 1996 — the "political religion" thesis that modern regimes manufacture the sacred to secure obedience.) **The design rule that falls out: the vast thing at the center of the rite must never be a person.** Point the awe at the tide, the salmon run, the star field, the dead — never at a living leader on a platform. Term-limit, rotate, and de-center every human role in every ceremony (this is the sacred-life application of `CANON.md`'s "power is contextual, earned, revocable"). State Shinto (Section 5a) is the same warning in a non-Western key: a beautiful plural practice tradition was captured by the state and turned to war.

**7c. "Spiritual but not religious" often means privatized and thin.** The drift from religion in the West has not produced a flowering of communal secular sacred; it has substantially produced **atomized, consumerist, individually-curated "wellness,"** which travels *with* declining associational membership rather than replacing it. Christian Smith's diagnosis of *Moralistic Therapeutic Deism* — God as a distant life-coach who wants you to be nice and happy — names the low-calorie meaning that fills the vacuum. Purser (Section 6) shows how that privatization gets monetized. The failure mode is not jackboots; it's **narcissism and a candle subscription.** A commons-first world has to build the *communal* sacred deliberately, because the market's default sacred is a solo product.

**7d. Death rites can coerce, too — the gift has a shadow.** `CANON.md` already names it: gift economies carry obligation and coercion, ritual carries rank. A town that keeps the dead present, holds the annual grief-rite, expects everyone at the composting grove — can also **surveil private grief, enforce a "correct" way to mourn, and shame the person who wanted to leave and not look back.** Continuing bonds (4b) is a *permission*, not a mandate; the instant a community *requires* you to keep talking to your dead, it has become another metric turned against a person. The person who wants no ritual, no grave, no keeping — the atheist who wants only to be gone — is a hard case the world must protect, not absorb.

**7e. Awe science is real but oversold.** The awe literature sits inside psychology's replication problem; effect sizes for one-shot "small self" inductions are modest and context-dependent, and some findings have been contested or fail to replicate cleanly. Awe is a real, useful raw material — but it is not a reliable civic machine you can pull a lever on. Build for it; don't *depend* on a dose of it fixing anything.

## 8. Wildcard lens (rolled): **crip theory & disability justice** — the sacred that refuses the cure

*Entropy: last hex group of this session id (`…b8a204`) → last digit 4 → lens 4, disability justice & crip theory. I would not have reached for it on "the sacred"; it turned out to be the sharpest tool in the box, and it goes somewhere the care brief did not.*

Most religion smuggles in an **eschatology of cure** — a promised future where the lame walk, the blind see, the broken body is made whole, death is undone. Disability theorists name the everyday version of this **"curative time"** (Alison Kafer, *Feminist, Queer, Crip*): the assumption that *any future containing disability is a future to be avoided*, that the only acceptable trajectory is toward repair. Against it, **crip time** (Ellen Samuels; Kafer) is time that "bends the clock to the body" rather than bending the body to the clock — flexible, non-linear, and, in Samuels' unforgettable formulation, *"crip time is grief time… is vampire time,"* a temporality of loss and recurrence, not of forward-marching progress. ([Ellen Samuels, "Six Ways of Looking at Crip Time," *DSQ*](https://dsq-sds.org/index.php/dsq/article/download/5824/4684); ["What is Crip Time?" — The Polyphony](https://thepolyphony.org/2024/01/26/medhums-101-what-is-crip-time/); ["Crip Time, Sacred Time" — Political Theology](https://politicaltheology.com/crip-time-sacred-time-and-holding-history/))

Mapped onto our world, this produces three sharp, un-obvious moves:

1. **A sacred that does not promise a cure.** The great trap for a post-extraction utopia is to let its meaning-system quietly imply that in the good world everyone is well, whole, and long-lived — which makes the disabled, the chronically ill, the dying, and the grieving into *unfinished business*, people the utopia hasn't gotten to yet. A crip sacred **refuses redemption-as-repair**: it holds that a life with pain, limitation, and an early death is a *complete* life, not a rough draft awaiting fix. This is the theological correction to the whole solarpunk register.
2. **Grief time *is* sacred time.** Samuels' "crip time is grief time" and the 19th-c. idea of grief as a set-apart temporality (Dana Luciano) converge with the monastic hours (Section 3): mourning is not a productivity problem to be closed out on a schedule (7d), it is a *legitimate non-linear time* the community holds open. The Salish should have publicly protected slow time — fallow hours, mourning that is allowed to loop and recur and take years — as a *sacred category*, exactly the time capitalism's 24/7 tried to abolish.
3. **Access as a sacred act.** Mia Mingus's **access intimacy** — the felt sense of someone *getting* your access needs without your having to explain — is, in a world that treats interdependence as the truth of being human, a *numinous* encounter, not a logistics task. The holiest thing one person does for another here may be building the ramp before being asked.

The crip lens also **audits Section 2's danger**: the "small self" of awe is fine when the vastness is the sea, but a body already told daily that it is small, wrong, and in the way does not need a rite that shrinks it further. The sacred owes the disabled *enlargement* — you are whole, you are held, your time is real — not another dose of humbling.

## 9. Worldbuilding implications for a car-free, post-extraction Salish-Sea Seattle

Concrete, scene-ready details (the sacred *shown*, never preached):

1. **The Hours are transit and prayer at once.** A public bell (or a low mesh chime on the Weave relays) marks a handful of fixed daily moments — dawn, the noon slack tide, the 4pm ferry, dark — when the ordinary rule is that you *may stop*. Nobody checks that you did. Some people pray, some just put the tools down and watch the water; the tram timetable and the contemplative hour are the same schedule. (Section 3.)
2. **The composting grove is the town's death-place.** A Recompose-descended civic **Ground**: hexagonal timber-and-salvaged-steel vessels where the dead become soil over a month, and a grove of young trees, each fed by someone, each carrying the name on a plain marker. Free, un-sold, non-denominational. You visit your dead by visiting a *living tree.* (Section 4a.)
3. **Names are kept, literally, in a Book of the Water.** A continuing-bonds register (cousin to the whale death-book in `research/wildcards.md`): the dead are entered by name, and once a year the town reads them aloud at the shore. You are encouraged, never required, to keep talking to yours. (4b, and the opt-out of 7d is real — some names come with the note *"wished not to be kept."*)
4. **A monthly Death Table in every neighborhood.** Tea and something sweet, no clergy, no agenda, anyone may come to say the unsayable thing about their own dying. Modeled on Death Café; it belongs to no one and needs no belief. (4c.)
5. **Awe is designed into the streetscape and rationed to the non-human** — per the rule held in §7b. The salmon corridor lit under the seawall glass; a plaza that stays dark until people gather then kindles (the quorum-lamp from `wildcards.md`); a night once a year when the city dims its counter-illuminated lights to let the stars back. The vast thing is always the sea, the run, the sky, the dead. (Sections 2, 7b.)
6. **No state religion, a plural ritual calendar, and a hard sovereignty line.** The newcomers' invented rites (Hours, Ground, Book, Table) run on the *settler* side of a bright line; Coast Salish ceremony is sovereign, led by the nations, and **not** folded into the civic calendar or reproduced as civic content. The city's official stance toward the deepest sacred life on its land is *defer and protect,* not *adopt.* (Section 5c.)
7. **Grief gets protected slow time.** A mourner can claim *crip/grief time* — a recognized, non-linear leave from the productive clock that may loop and recur for as long as it takes, held open by the mutual-aid web rather than closed out by an HR calendar. Mourning that takes three years is not a disorder here. (Section 8.)
8. **The sacred has term limits.** Every ceremonial role — who reads the names, who tends the grove, who rings the hour — rotates and is revocable, precisely so no one accretes a priesthood. The failure mode being designed against is Robespierre at the Festival and the man in the cathedral of light. (Sections 5b, 7b.)
9. **The un-homed arrival's first sacred is a small self by the water.** A newcomer (the band girl, or the next one) with no inherited faith is not handed a creed; she's handed a *practice* — a bell she may answer, a table she may sit at, a name she may keep — and the awe is free, downhill, at the shore. Belonging here is by *doing with*, not *believing that.* (Sections 1, 5a.)
10. **The heretic is protected.** The person who wants no ritual, no grave, no keeping, no grove — who wants only to be gone and forgotten — is honored *exactly* by being left alone. The world's proof that its sacred is not a metric turned against a person is that you are allowed to want none of it. (7d.)

## 10. Open questions for the next contributor

1. **What keeps the chairs getting set up in year five, in the rain?** Sunday Assembly died of volunteer burnout absent a transcendent obligation (7a). What is the Salish answer — rotation? mutual-aid reciprocity? a genuinely load-bearing *purpose* the assembly serves — that a godless congregation can sustain without either a god or a paid clergy? (A fiction thread: the person whose turn it is to ring the hour, who no longer wants to.)
2. **Does a world that refuses transcendence quietly spend down Holland's inherited moral capital — and when does the account come due?** Write the crisis that asks the secular sacred to demand a *real* sacrifice (a death, a war at the edge, a famine year) and see whether "society worshipping itself" can ask for more than society already wants to give.
3. **Where exactly is the sovereignty line drawn in a shared public square?** When Coast Salish ceremony and settler civic ritual occupy the same waterfront on the same day, who decides what is shared, what is witnessed-in-silence, and what is closed? A thread for careful hands, developed *with* the nations' terms centered, not resolved by an AI.
4. **What is the rite for a body that is composted but a person who is *not* to be kept?** Design the ceremony of deliberate forgetting — the honored erasure — that respects Refusal #2 ("names are kept") *and* the heretic's right to vanish. This is a genuine collision of the world's own values; don't tidy it.
