# Harm, Violence, Justice — a world without prisons, and the cases that break it

*A research brief for* The Reality Next Door. *Domain: how a post-extraction Salish handles harm — from a stolen boat to a rape to a murder — when it has refused the cage. Follows the Anti-Echo-Chamber Protocol (`AGENTS.md`). The assignment is explicit that this must not resolve neatly, and that one case must end badly. It does.*

The Three Refusals give this world its economy and its network. They do not, on their own, tell you what happens the night someone puts another person in the emergency ward, or the year a man in the co-op keeps finding girls to corner. A world that abolished the prison still has to answer the oldest question a society asks: *what do we do with the one who hurts us, and the one who will not stop?* The honest answer is that the best real-world thinkers on this — the abolitionists themselves — do **not** have a clean answer, and say so. This brief takes their strongest work seriously, then walks straight into the cases it struggles with, because that seam is where the world gets real or gets slop.

A framing note that binds everything below: the intellectual leaders of prison abolition and transformative justice are disproportionately Black women and women of color, and their work is grounded in defending survivors of exactly the violence a naive reader assumes abolition ignores — domestic and sexual violence. Mariame Kaba, Danielle Sered, Ruth Wilson Gilmore, adrienne maree brown, INCITE!. Cite them by name; do not flatten them into "activists."

## 1. What justice without prisons actually is (not the absence of a response)

The first correction, and the one the world must internalize: abolition is **not** "no consequences." Ruth Wilson Gilmore's formulation is that *"abolition is about presence, not absence"* — building the housing, health, income, and repair that make cages unnecessary, not simply deleting the cage and walking away ([Gilmore, On Being](https://onbeing.org/programs/ruth-wilson-gilmore-where-life-is-precious-life-is-precious/); [Dissent, "Abolition as Method"](https://dissentmagazine.org/article/abolition-as-method/)). Mariame Kaba is blunt that harm does not end: *"humans, as long as we're still humans, are going to harm each other. The difference is we will have other ways of addressing harm that are not prisons, policing, and surveillance"* ([Kaba, The Nation](https://www.thenation.com/article/culture/mariame-kaba-interview-til-we-free-us/)).

The working machinery has real names and real evidence:

- **Transformative justice (TJ)** — responses to violence that try to do what punishment fails to do: build safety for the person harmed, understand the conditions that produced the harm, and change them so it's less likely to recur, all without creating new harm through the response ([Kaba, TransformHarm](https://transformharm.org/resource_author/mariame-kaba/)). Its own literature is candid that accountability *cannot be forced*: "there is no such thing as making someone be accountable" — the person who caused harm has to choose it, which is precisely where the hard cases live.

- **Common Justice** (Danielle Sered, Brooklyn) is the most rigorous real-world proof-of-concept for *violent felonies*. It diverts cases — assault, robbery — out of prison into a survivor-led accountability process, *only if the survivor consents*. Sered's counterintuitive finding: restorative work does **best** on the most serious harms. *"The inclination to apply restorative justice to the lowest-possible-level harm is actually exactly backward"* ([Democracy Now!](https://www.democracynow.org/2019/3/15/danielle_sered_to_end_mass_incarceration)). When offered the choice, about **90% of eligible survivors choose the restorative process over the defendant going to prison** — a devastating fact for the assumption that victims want cages ([Common Justice model](https://www.commonjustice.org/common_justice_model)).

- **Circles of Support and Accountability (CoSA)** is the sharpest instrument for *the person the world is most tempted to throw away*: released sex offenders at high risk of re-offending, with no supervision left. Four to six trained community volunteers form a circle around the "core member." The covenant runs both ways, and its two mottos are the whole ethic in six words: **"No More Victims"** and **"No One Is Disposable."** The Canadian national replication study found an **83% reduction in sexual recidivism** versus matched controls ([national replication, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19901236/); [CoSA Canada](https://www.cosacanada.com/)). This is the model closest to what our world would actually run: not therapy, not surveillance — *chosen, sustained, accountable relationship as the containment.*

Sered's deeper argument is the load-bearing one for worldbuilding, because it explains *why* this can work at all. Violence, she argues, is driven by shame, isolation, exposure to violence, and an inability to meet one's economic needs — and **prison delivers all four in concentrated form.** Prison is thus not the opposite of violence but "a breeding ground for it." A post-extraction world that has already removed the economic desperation and built dense mutual care has, in theory, drained the reservoir that produces the violence in the first place. *In theory.*

## 2. The cases that break it (the assignment's spine)

### 2a. The person who will not stop

Every honest practitioner circles this one. Accountability is voluntary; some people will not volunteer. The community's folk-name for this figure is the **"missing stair"** — the person everyone privately knows is dangerous, whom the group has silently learned to route around instead of confronting, warning newcomers in whispers rather than fixing the step ([Geek Feminism Wiki](https://geekfeminism.fandom.com/wiki/Missing_stair); coined by Cliff Pervocracy, 2012). The missing stair is the specific pathology of a *no-metrics, high-trust, gift-based* community — exactly ours. Because the culture refuses to rank and refuses to surveil, it can also refuse to *name*, and a charming predator can dine out for years on everyone's reluctance to be the one who makes it official. The Weave, which "routes difference toward" belonging, can route a predator toward fresh victims with the same frictionless warmth.

The abolitionist toolkit's terminal answer for the one who will not stop is some form of **separation** — and this is where the movement's own language goes quiet and careful, because separation without a prison is **exile**, and exile is ancient, and exile is not clean (§4, §5).

### 2b. The irredeemable, and the limit Common Justice draws on itself

Here is the most important single fact in this brief, and it comes from the abolitionists' own flagship program. Common Justice — the model that proves restorative justice can handle armed robbery and serious assault — **excludes** intimate-partner violence, domestic violence, sexual violence, and familial violence from eligibility. It also screens out defendants with severe untreated mental-health or substance conditions ([Common Justice, Refer a Case](https://commonjustice.org/refer-a-case)). The people running the most credible alternative to prison have looked at the cases involving the most entrenched, intimate, repeating harm — and *drawn a boundary around them.* Not because those survivors matter less, but because the dynamics of coercive control make a facilitated face-to-face process dangerous. **The frontier of "no prisons" is not the murderer. It is the abuser in the next room.** Any version of this world that pretends otherwise has not read its own sources.

### 2c. Defense against bad actors, and against a hostile outside

Two different threats, both real:

- **The internal predator with no scruple.** A society with no coercive apparatus is, by construction, soft against someone who has neither. The whole point of an accountable, revocable, term-limited authority (CANON: "there is a captain in the storm") is that *somebody, sometimes, has to be able to physically stop a person* — restrain the man with the knife, remove the child from the house — and the world has to grant that power *and* keep it from becoming the police it abolished. That is a live wire, not a solved problem.
- **The hostile exterior.** The turning was "long and uneven"; some places didn't turn. A car-free, low-hierarchy bioregion has to survive next to a growth-state, a warlord, a trafficking network that sees a rich, soft, un-policed coast as prey. The anthropology of stateless defense (dispersal, federation, the high cost of conquering something with no central throat to seize) is the honest resource here — but it is a *harder* life than the brochure, and it means the Salish keeps some capacity for violence it would rather not have.

## 3. Wildcard lens — thermodynamics & entropy (rolled: last digit of Unix timestamp `1785876010` → **0**)

I did not choose this lens and it turned out to be the right blade. Justice is a thermodynamic problem.

**Harm is entropy, and entropy is not free to reverse.** The Second Law says you cannot un-break the glass, un-drown the child, un-rape the survivor. A murdered person does not come back in *any* justice system — retributive or restorative. This is the quiet truth under the whole debate: **no justice is reversible.** Prison pretends to "pay back" a debt that cannot be paid; restorative justice at its most honest admits the harm is irreversible and asks the only remaining question — *what can be built forward from here?* Repair is not reversal. It is the construction of new order alongside the permanent scar. A world that markets restorative justice as *healing* — as if the harm can be undone — is lying the same way the prison lies. What it can offer is *metabolism*: turning the fixed fact of harm into something a life can be built around, rather than a wound the system keeps re-opening.

**Order requires continuous energy input, or it dissipates.** A prison is a *low-energy* way to store a person: lock the door, and the "safety" maintains itself with a guard and a wall. CoSA is a *high-energy* structure: four to six volunteers, every week, for years — an enormous, continuous throughput of human attention to hold one dangerous person in safe relationship. This is the hidden thermodynamic cost of abolition, and it is the thing that burns out (§5). **The cage is cheap because it dissipates the person; the circle is expensive because it sustains them.** A post-extraction world that refuses the cheap solution is signing up for a permanent, distributed *energy bill* — paid in the exhaustion of the people who show up. If it will not pay that bill honestly, the abolition is a fantasy and the missing stair is the interest.

**Exile is entropy export.** Banishing the person who won't stop does not destroy the danger; by conservation it *moves* it — downstream, to the next town, the next circle, the people with less power to refuse it (§5, the drumming-circle case). A closed system can't get rid of entropy; it can only pump it across a boundary onto someone else. This is the thermodynamic indictment of exile, and the world has to face it.

## 4. Decentering the default — historical and non-Western precedent

**Historical: medieval Iceland and early Germanic law — outlawry and wergild.** Long before the penitentiary (a 19th-century invention), stateless and near-stateless societies ran justice with **no prisons at all.** The Icelandic Free State (930–1262) had courts but no executive, no police, and no jails. Its two instruments were **compensation** — *wergild*, the "man-price," a scheduled payment from offender's kin to victim's kin, the original "restorative" ledger — and, for the one who would not pay or would not stop, **outlawry**: *skóggangr*, "going to the forest." The full outlaw lost all legal personhood; anyone could kill him without consequence. Outlawry was exile as a death sentence outsourced to the wilderness — a society with no prison still needed a way to expel the irredeemable, and the way it found was terrible ([overview: *Grágás* and Icelandic outlawry, standard legal-history sources]). The lesson for our world is sobering: *the prison is not the only cruelty a justice system can reach for, and the alternative to the cage has historically been banishment or death.* Compensation-plus-outlawry is elegant and it is also blood-feud's engine — when kin owe kin, an unpaid debt becomes a cycle of reprisal.

**Non-Western, contemporary: Diné (Navajo) peacemaking — *Hózhóójí Naat'aanii*.** After a century of imposed Anglo-American courts, the Navajo Nation deliberately returned to traditional process in 1982. *Hózhóójí naat'aanii* is loosely "peacemaking" but closer to *"people talking together to re-form relationships with each other and the universe"* — *hózhó* is the balance/beauty/rightness the harm disrupted, and the naat'aanii (a respected community member, not a judge with power over the parties) guides talking-things-out until relationship is restored, drawing in family on both sides ([Robert Yazzie, "Life Comes From It," canonical account, via IIRP](https://www.iirp.edu/news/the-dynamics-of-navajo-peacemaking); [Tribal College Journal](https://tribalcollegejournal.org/making-peace-navajo/)). Crucially it handles real cases — DUI, family violence, some violent offenses — and it is *sovereign Indigenous law*, not a technique borrowed for flavor.

**Non-Western, at civilizational scale: Rwanda's *gacaca*.** After the 1994 genocide, Rwanda faced ~120,000 detained suspects and a shattered court system. It revived village *gacaca* ("justice on the grass") courts — ~12,000 of them tried **over one million cases** in a decade, with confession-and-repentance offering reduced sentences and community labor instead of prison ([UN Background Note](https://www.un.org/en/preventgenocide/rwanda/assets/pdf/Backgrounder%20Justice%202014.pdf)). It is the largest experiment in mass community justice in modern history, and it is **not a success story to romanticize** — see §5. Both precedents keep sovereignty and specificity central: name the nation, cite the nation's own jurists, do not extract the sacred.

**On Coast Salish law — sovereignty first, and a deliberate step back.** This is Coast Salish land and the Coast Salish nations are sovereign here (CANON). Their legal traditions are theirs, living, and not ours to systematize into a worldbuilding mechanic. Two things can be said with cited care. First, the *modern* institutions are real and worth naming: the **Tulalip Tribes'** Alternative Sentencing / **Healing to Wellness Court** grounds accountability in mental, physical, and spiritual health and cultural values, "the way tribes have been resolving disputes for generations" ([Tulalip, Harvard Honoring Nations](https://nnigovernance.arizona.edu/keywords/tulalip-alternative-sentencing-program); [UW Boundless](https://www.washington.edu/boundless/tribal-healing-court/)). Second, scholarship names the hard parts *without redistributing the sacred*: Bruce Granville Miller's *The Problem of Justice: Tradition and Law in the Coast Salish World* and Andrée Boisselle's *Law's Hidden Canvas: Teasing Out the Threads of Coast Salish Legal Sensibility* (UVic, 2017) both show a legal sensibility rooted in family, witnessing, and relationship rather than in a state — and the historical record also holds **rank, hereditary slavery from raiding, and exile** ([UBC Coast Salish governance guide](https://guides.library.ubc.ca/indigtraditionalgovt/coastsalish)). The world inherits the difficult norms too; it does not get to keep only the beautiful ones. When the fiction touches this, it names the nation and the source and steps back.

## 5. Rule Zero — the opposing view, steelmanned (two opponents who disagree with *each other*)

Per Protocol, the strongest disagreement — and I've chosen two opponents who would also fight each other, so no single counter-camp becomes its own echo.

**Opponent A — the incapacitation realist (from the center/right).** The steelman is *not* retribution; it's safety. Some fraction of people commit serious violence repeatedly, are not deterred, do not "choose accountability," and are not reachable by any circle in time to spare the next victim. For that "dangerous few," incapacitation — physically preventing access to victims — is not cruelty, it's the survivor's protection, and it works: removing a persistent serial offender does empirically reduce the harm they would have done. Thomas Frampton, himself sympathetic to abolition, concedes in *The Dangerous Few* that abolitionists "don't hold one uniform vision" and their answers to this question are "often less than satisfactory to the skeptic: they either offer too few assurances or present watered-down versions of abolitionism that recast the project in decidedly reformist terms" ([Harvard Law Review](https://harvardlawreview.org/print/vol-135/the-dangerous-few-taking-seriously-prison-abolition-and-its-skeptics/)). His honest defense is only that "the absence of a bulletproof rejoinder is not a reason to dismiss the project" — which is a real answer and also, notably, not a *guarantee* to the person waiting to be hurt.

**Opponent B — the survivor-centered feminist skeptic (from the left, and she despises Opponent A).** She is *also* against prisons, or at least deeply distrusts them — but she is furious at TJ's romance, because in practice it puts the *burden of the offender's redemption on the victim.* A voluntary, community-based process depends on the community, and communities protect their own — the popular organizer, the beloved musician, the man who books the shows. She points at TJ's own honest literature: practitioners admit the work "usually fails," that one of the biggest barriers is simply that "abusers don't want to be accountable" ([Beyond Survival notes, Le Hub](https://en.wiki.lehub.ca/index.php/Notes_on_accountability_from_Beyond_Survival:_Strategies_and_Stories_from_the_Transformative_Justice_Movement)). She notes that Common Justice — the *good* program — won't touch domestic or sexual violence, i.e. the violence women most face (§2b). And she reads adrienne maree brown's own warning back to the movement: that call-outs meant as accountability curdle into "a culture of fear, secrecy and isolation," spectacle that "doesn't necessarily serve the survivors" ([brown, via KQED](https://www.kqed.org/arts/13893734/a-roadmap-away-from-cancel-culture-and-towards-transformative-justice)). Her charge: a no-metrics, high-trust commune is *structurally worse* at believing victims of its insiders, because it has refused the very tools — records, formal complaint, an outside authority — that a survivor without social capital can appeal to over the heads of the abuser's friends. **A and B agree the current answer is failing and agree on nothing about the fix** — A wants a hard floor of incapacitation; B wants believing-the-victim and a way *out* over the community's head. The world needs an answer that survives both.

## 6. Disconfirming evidence & failure modes (real evidence it goes wrong)

- **The case that already happened, in our world.** A drumming teacher committed multiple sexual assaults across an international circle. The transformative-justice response: he was "chastised, sent on a course, then allowed to rejoin the circles. The women he assaulted never came back" ([reader account, Autostraddle review of *Beyond Survival*](https://www.autostraddle.com/beyond-survival-book-review/)). The process protected the community's comfort and the offender's belonging; it *exiled the survivors instead of the harm-doer.* This is the single most important cautionary tale in the file.
- **Mutual-aid burnout is the thermodynamic bill coming due.** The 2020 mutual-aid wave — the closest natural experiment to gift-based care at scale — was gutted within a year by "volunteer fatigue," "unsustainable overwork, exhaustion, shame," networks folding "despite ongoing need" when the key organizer broke ([mutual-aid burnout study](https://apsw-ojs-uh.tdl.org/apsw/article/download/13/7)). The high-energy structure (§3) dissipates when the energy runs out — and accountability circles are more draining than food delivery.
- **Gacaca's dark side.** Mass community justice at scale produced coerced confessions, judges with personal ties or preconceived views, "trumped-up charges linked to the government's wish to silence critics," and survivors who called it "government-enforced reconciliation" — *forced* to give forgiveness ([Human Rights Watch, *Justice Compromised*](https://www.hrw.org/report/2011/05/31/justice-compromised/legacy-rwandas-community-based-gacaca-courts)). Community justice is not automatically gentle; a community can be a mob, and "reconciliation" can be coerced from the victim as easily as accountability is refused by the offender.
- **Exile just relocates the predator (entropy export, §3).** Banishment's structural failure is that it exports risk to whoever has least power to refuse it. Medieval outlawry solved this by making the outlaw killable; a world that won't kill has no clean endpoint for the person it banishes — he simply arrives, unannounced, in a town with a shorter memory.
- **The missing stair is *stable*.** Communities reach a low-energy equilibrium where routing-around a known predator is cheaper than confronting him, and stay there for years. No-metrics culture makes this *worse*, not better: nothing is written down, so each new victim starts from zero.

## 7. The case that ends badly (mandated — no tidy resolution)

*Written for the world, drawn straight from the failure modes above. Voice per `AGENTS.md`: plain, concrete.*

> Deraan ran sound for half the all-ages rooms on the south shore. Good at it. Generous with gear, first to help a new band load in, the kind of person a scene is built on. The first thread came from a sixteen-year-old who'd crossed on the Weave from an interior town — the kind of arrival the world is proud of. She said he'd cornered her in the loft after load-out. She used the old word for it and then took it back and then said it again.
>
> The circle convened the way it was supposed to. Facilitators from two different houses so no one was judging their own. Deraan came — that was the thing, he *came*, which everyone said was a good sign. He said the word *misunderstanding* and then, coached, said harder words. He agreed to the whole covenant: no closed rooms with anyone under twenty, a witness at every load-out, six months of Wednesday nights with a men's circle across the water. For a while it held. People exhaled. The scene told itself it had done the thing the old world couldn't.
>
> The second one didn't come to the circle. She came to the first girl, months later, in a kitchen, and what she described was the same loft and the same word and a night that fell inside the six months. The witness rule had a hole in it the size of a man everyone liked.
>
> Here the world had no good move and made the bad one slowly. There was no cage to put him in and no one willing to be the one who put him somewhere. The circle couldn't force a second covenant; accountability you can't force is the whole doctrine, and the doctrine is right, and the doctrine did not help. Someone raised separation and the room went cold, because separation with no prison is a word that means *make him someone else's problem.* In the end it half-happened on its own — the shows stopped booking him, the gear requests dried up, doors closed one at a time until he was standing outside a scene that no longer said his name. Not banished. Just starved, the way the cars were starved. It felt almost clean.
>
> He crossed to a north-shore town with a shorter memory and a room that needed a sound guy. The Weave could have carried the warning and didn't, because no one node holds the whole graph and no one wanted their name on a thing that couldn't be un-sent. That is the same forgetfulness the world loves for the queer kid — *reach is not computable* — working in the other direction, routing a predator toward people who hadn't heard.
>
> The first girl left the coast entirely. Not driven out by anyone; there was no one to blame, which was worse. She just could not be in a scene that had kept its warmth for him and spent its discomfort on her. She took the fast boat. Nobody raised an eyebrow, exactly the way the world promised — and that was the cruelest kept promise in the whole story, because the thing she needed to get away from fast was *them.*
>
> No one was caged. No one was healed. The harm moved north and the survivor moved away and the scene kept its story about itself for another year, until the north shore's own first thread came in. This is not a failure of the vision to be fixed by a better circle. It is the standing cost of refusing the cage, and the world pays it in exactly this coin, and the ledger does not zero out.

## 8. Worldbuilding implications for a car-free, post-extraction Salish-Sea Seattle

Concrete, scene-ready:

1. **The circle is a room, and it has a smell.** Accountability process here isn't a metaphor — it's a specific building (a longhouse-adjacent hall, a cleared co-op back room), a rota of trained facilitators, tea that someone made, and a covenant written on real paper and *kept on paper*, because the one place this world tolerates a record is the ledger of who agreed to what and whether they did it. Not a metric turned against a person — a promise held to a person.
2. **"No More Victims / No One Is Disposable"** — lift CoSA's double motto directly as a load-bearing civic phrase, painted somewhere public. It contains the entire unresolved tension in six words, and the world argues about which half is winning.
3. **The witness at load-out.** Show the small machinery of a covenant in ordinary life: a musician who, by the terms of an old process, is never alone in a closed room with anyone young, and the friend whose quiet job is to be the witness. Render it as neither shameful nor forgotten — just a fact of how the scene runs, sometimes lovingly, sometimes with a hole in it.
4. **The starving, not the banishing.** The world's exile is the same mechanism as its traffic policy — not a gate slammed but a slow withdrawal of the gift-web until a person is standing in a place that no longer feeds them. Make a character name the resemblance out loud and be disturbed by it: *we starved the cars and we starve the men and I don't always know the difference.*
5. **The forgetful Weave, weaponized.** The same non-computable reach that saves the lonely kid *fails to carry the warning north.* One thread should turn on this exact double edge — someone trying, and failing, to make the Weave do the one thing it structurally cannot: broadcast "this person is dangerous" to everyone downstream.
6. **The captain who can restrain.** Establish, in canon-adjacent texture, the accountable-and-revocable authority who *is* allowed to physically stop violence in progress — a harbor-warden, a house-elder on a night rota — and show the term limit and the recall that keep them from becoming police. The power to restrain the knife exists; it is watched like the Dark Garden is watched.
7. **The survivor takes the fast boat.** Tie justice to the movement canon: the person harmed gets the fast boat, the front of the care queue, the benefit of every doubt — and let a character clock the bitter version, that sometimes the thing you need to leave fast is your own beloved community.
8. **Coast Salish sovereignty is jurisdiction, not flavor.** When harm crosses into or out of a nation's people or land, the nation's own law governs, full stop — a Tulalip-style Healing-to-Wellness process, a naat'aanii-guided talking-out — and the settler-descended commons defers, cites, and steps back. Write the *handoff* between systems as a real, sometimes awkward, sovereign boundary.
9. **The missing stair as a named social fact.** Give the world a folk-word for the predator everyone routes around, and a countervailing folk-practice — a "naming night," a deliberately uncomfortable ritual of saying the quiet thing — that the community invented *because* it learned the hard way that no-metrics warmth breeds missing stairs.
10. **The energy bill is visible.** Show a circle-keeper burning out — the person who has held someone dangerous in safe relationship every Wednesday for three years and is running on empty — and the rotation/relief that either catches them or doesn't. The cost of refusing the cage is paid in this exhaustion; put it on the page.

## 9. Open questions for the next contributor

1. **Where is the floor?** This world refuses the cage — but does it refuse *all* confinement? Is there some accountable, revocable, humane form of "you cannot be near people right now" for the one who will not stop, and how is that *not* just a prison with a nicer name? Draw the line, in a scene, and let a character challenge it.
2. **Who does the world kill, if anyone?** Medieval outlawry ended in death; abolition refuses death. If the Salish will neither cage nor kill nor cleanly exile the irredeemable, what is the *actual* terminal answer for the person who keeps hurting people — or does it honestly admit it doesn't have one, and live with the body count of that refusal?
3. **Defense without a standing army.** What stops a trafficking network or a growth-state predator that sees the soft coast as prey? Write the stateless defense — federation, dispersal, the cost of conquest — as a lived, harder-than-the-brochure reality, and find where it strains.
4. **The survivor who wanted the cage.** Common Justice works because survivors *choose* it — but ~10% don't. Write the person harmed here who *wants* punishment, wants the old retribution, and finds a world that has abolished the thing they need. Do not make them wrong. Do not resolve it.
5. **Can the Weave ever warn?** Is there a commons-governed, non-ranking, non-surveilling way to carry "danger" across the network without rebuilding the registry, the watchlist, the scarlet letter — or is the forgetful Weave's inability to warn a permanent, accepted cost of refusing the surveillance state? This is the sharpest unsolved design problem in the file.

## PROPOSED CANON

*(World-level facts I'm proposing; a human merges these into `CANON.md`. I did not edit canon directly.)*

- **Justice runs on chosen accountability, not the cage.** The Salish has no prisons. Serious harm is met by survivor-led accountability circles (Common Justice / CoSA lineage): a real room, trained facilitators from more than one house, and a covenant *kept on paper* — the one record this world permits, because it binds a promise to a person rather than ranking the person. Accountability cannot be forced; that limit is understood as real and costly, not glossed.
- **"No More Victims / No One Is Disposable"** is a load-bearing civic phrase (from real CoSA practice). Both halves are held at once; the tension between them is never declared won.
- **Exile is the terminal move and it is not clean.** For the one who will not stop, the world's last resort is the same starving-out it used on cars — a slow withdrawal of the gift-web, not a gate. Everyone knows this only *exports* the danger downstream, and the forgetful Weave cannot reliably warn the next town. This is named as an open wound, not a solution.
- **The accountable restrainer exists.** Someone (harbor-warden, house-elder on rota) holds the revocable, term-limited, recallable power to physically stop violence in progress — granted precisely so the world isn't defenseless, watched precisely so it doesn't become the police it abolished.
- **Coast Salish law is sovereign jurisdiction over harm touching its people or land** — a Tulalip-style Healing-to-Wellness / naat'aanii-guided process governs, and the settler-descended commons defers and steps back. (Names/specifics per the nations themselves; not ours to systematize.)
