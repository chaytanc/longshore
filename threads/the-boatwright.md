# The Boatwright

Hale scarfs the new plank into the *Dovetail*'s hull the way you set a bone — slow, both hands, feeling for the place it wants to go before you make it go there. Cedar over oak frames, copper rivets peened cold. The old plank had rot at the garboard and Hale cut it out clean rather than nurse it another season, because a hull you have to trust in a February crossing is not a place for hope. The saltmarsh smell comes up off the tideflat. A hundred feet down the boards, daylight drops through the glass blocks set in the seawall to the young Chinook running the lit corridor, and the number is thin this year. The number is not Hale's to keep; it belongs to the Suquamish council that co-manages this water and stencils the season's count, dated, where the tourists used to read plaques. Half of a small thing. Everyone reads it and no one is proud of it.

They are broad through the shoulders and forearms in the way of people whose work is lifting, and they hold it without display — strength like a tool set down within reach, not brandished. They caulk. They splice. They keep the north seawall's pilings scraped and the two working packets sound, and three afternoons a week they run the Weave's heavy freight across the water: the bundles too private to flood the mesh, the hashed drives and the mailed things, riding in the locked courier drawer under the *Dovetail*'s stern thwart. Hale is a carrier. The word is old and means what it says — the freight is other people's, and it crosses because a pair of hands takes it across.

The harbor leans on them and they let it, because letting people lean is work too, and Hale is good at work. The band kids bring their broken instruments. Marie brings the Suquamish clam-garden co-op's winch when the winch won't hold — it is their beach and their season to set, and Hale is only the hands that mend the gear. Widows bring the boats their dead didn't finish. Hale sets it all right and asks for tea and keeps no tally of who owes what, because a number turned toward a person is the one thing this place agreed never to build. What no one brings is Hale. That is understood between Hale and the water and no one else.

Because the water took Del.

Del was the other carrier, the only person in the whole harbor whose weight Hale could hand back. A winter run, a squall off the point the forecast underplayed, a boat found and a body not. The sea here is restored, not healed — nursed daily, prayed over, and still, some Februaries, hungry. People said the true things afterward; people are kind here, it is one of the things that survived. Hale nodded and thanked them and went back out on the next run, because the freight still had to cross, and because moving is easier than the other thing.

In the courier drawer, under the working bundles, there is one Hale has not delivered. An answer — *you will not be the only one, come to the green city* — that came back weeks-late on Del's last boat, bound for a kid on a far island who sent *is anyone out there like me* into the dark and then, in the slow interval the network is so proud of, before the warmth could walk home to them, drowned off their own dock. The Weave is proud that slowness is the point. Hale has learned what the pride costs. Some afternoons the slowness is a mercy and some afternoons it is only late, and the difference is a child. Hale cannot deliver the bundle and cannot burn it. It stays in the drawer.

And then there is Bec, who runs the tea-stall at the Bainbridge slip, who has a laugh that goes off like a halyard slapping a mast and a way of setting the cup down that Hale watches for and pretends not to. Hale wants — plainly, bodily, the want unashamed and unhurried, no old curse loaded onto it, just the pull of one person toward another across a plank of wet cedar. Wants the weight of a hand. Wants to be, once, the one leaned on. The wanting is easy. It is the leaning Hale cannot yet do — to set a living person where Del was, in a drawer already holding one thing it can't put down. So Hale takes the tea, and lets their hand rest a half-second past the cup, and goes back out three afternoons a week, sound hull under them, the salmon count thin, the freight of other people's longing dry in the drawer, and no one, still, tending Hale.

Widowed of their fellow carrier **Del**, lost to a winter squall. The one the harbor leans on, who has no one to lean on in turn.
