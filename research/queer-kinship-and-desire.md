# Queer kinship and desire — a research brief

*Domain: how love, desire, kinship, and the raising of children and care of elders actually work in a car-free, post-extraction Salish-Sea Seattle. Register per CANON: Baldwin's ache and interiority, stripped of the era's shame and transphobia — longing uncursed, but not un-costed.*

This brief is the connective tissue between two that already exist. `research/music-and-scenes.md` establishes the **ballroom house** as scene infrastructure — the mother, the children, the reputation earned by care. `research/health-and-care.md` establishes the **care web** and disability justice as the substrate beneath the clinic. This brief asks the questions those two leave open: *who loves whom here, who raises the children, who wipes the elder, who is left out, and what does jealousy do in a world that refused the couple-form as law?* The load-bearing claim: **chosen family is not a metaphor and not a consolation prize — it is a documented kinship system with its own labor, its own hierarchies, and its own casualties.** The hard part was never imagining it. The hard part is who's tired, who's alone, and who gets to be wanted.

I keep one tension on the page throughout and refuse to resolve it: **care is love made into labor, and labor falls unevenly.** A world that abolishes the wage on care does not abolish the fatigue, the resentment, or the fact that it is usually the same few people carrying it.

## 1. Chosen family is real, and it grew from exile

The anthropologist **Kath Weston** did the fieldwork that named this. *Families We Choose: Lesbians, Gays, Kinship* (Columbia UP, 1991) — interviews across the San Francisco Bay Area in the AIDS years — was the first study to take seriously that gay men and lesbians, cast out of blood kinship, were not *without* family but **building** one: from lovers, ex-lovers, friends, and the people who showed up. Her sharpest finding is the one worth carrying: **chosen families cannot be understood apart from the families people were exiled from.** The chosen family is shaped like the wound. It is not a frictionless utopia of pure affinity; it carries the grief of the door that closed, and it often reproduces, in miniature, the very things it fled — favoritism, silence, the parent who takes too much. ([Columbia UP](https://cup.columbia.edu/book/families-we-choose/9780585380902/); [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/familieswechoose0000west))

The AIDS crisis made this concrete and deadly. When blood families refused to visit dying sons, and hospitals refused to recognize a partner of fifteen years, the chosen family became the only people in the room — and had no standing to make a decision, sign a form, or claim a body. Chosen kinship in the queer world was forged at exactly the point where the state and the biological family both failed a dying person at once. That origin is not decoration; it is why these bonds are fierce and why they distrust paper.

**For the world:** in this Seattle, chosen family is not the underground alternative — it is the ordinary, load-bearing unit, the way the music brief made the house ordinary. But the Weston finding stays: **it is shaped like the wound.** The kid who arrives from the flat interior town is not a blank slate joining a happy commune; she is carrying the family that let her go, and it will show up in how she loves.

## 2. The house as institution: gender, platonic parenting, overlapping kinship

The music brief covered ballroom as scene infrastructure. The deeper anthropology is **Marlon M. Bailey's** *Butch Queens Up in Pumps: Gender, Performance, and Ballroom Culture in Detroit* (U Michigan Press, 2011) — a first-person performance ethnography by a scholar who was himself a member of the scene. Three of Bailey's findings should be structural in this world, not costume: ([U Michigan Press](https://press.umich.edu/Books/B/Butch-Queens-Up-in-Pumps2); [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/butchqueensupinp0000bail))

- **A gender system, not a binary.** Bailey documents a **six-part gender/sexuality system** the Ballroom scene uses to organize itself — it does not map onto the two-box world and does not need to. This is the lived, working version of what CANON already asserts: gender runs wider than two and always did. Ballroom didn't theorize its way there; it *needed* the categories to house real people, and built them.
- **Platonic parenting and "overlapping kinship."** Bailey's term. A house **mother** or **father** is a parent *without* being a lover — the roles are deliberately de-sexualized so that care and mentorship don't collapse into the thing that so often exploited these same kids. And people hold **overlapping kinship**: you can be a child in one house, a mother in another arena, someone's lover, and someone's mentee, all at once. Kinship here is a **role you perform through labor**, not a slot you're born into. You become a mother by mothering.
- **Care as the actual product.** The balls are the visible spectacle; the invisible product is **survival** — housing the thrown-out kid, running the HIV/AIDS peer education the mainstream gay-rights orgs wouldn't fund, feeding people. Bailey is explicit that the performance labor and the care labor are the same labor.

**The appropriation knot (name it):** ballroom is a specifically **Black and Latinx** queer/trans creation, forged against the anti-Black colorism of white-run drag pageants. Its aesthetics have been repeatedly stripped and sold — Madonna, *Paris Is Burning*, fashion houses sending scouts to balls for next season's looks — while the people who built it stayed poor and unhoused. ([INTO — The Colonization of Ballroom Culture](https://www.intomore.com/culture/the-colonization-of-ballroom-culture/); [Excalibur](https://www.excal.on.ca/arts/2025/03/08/ballroom-culture-a-vibrant-history-and-lasting-legacy/)) A world that makes "the house" its default kinship unit inherits an obligation not to do the same thing at the level of worldbuilding: keep the lineage attached, keep it Black and brown and trans in origin, and don't sand it into a generic "found family" aesthetic. That flattening is itself the extraction we refuse.

## 3. Desire in Baldwin's register — the ache stays; only the shame goes

The CANON voice note asks for **Baldwin's *Giovanni's Room*** — masculine ache, interiority, the weight of desire — minus the shame and transphobia. It's worth being precise about what that surgery removes and what it must *not*.

Scholarship reads *Giovanni's Room* as **fundamentally a study in shame**: David's inability to love without shame corrodes the love itself, distorts him, and drives him to destroy the thing he most wants. The shame is specifically about **masculinity as heterosexuality** — David experiences desire as emasculation, being-the-object-of-a-gaze as feminizing and therefore unbearable. The "room" starts as sanctuary and becomes suffocating; the closet is not a place he's in but a thing he *is*. ([Chagrin d'amour: Intimacy, Shame, and the Closet — JSTOR](https://www.jstor.org/stable/48664824); [LitHub — On the Virtuosic Shame of Giovanni's Room](https://lithub.com/james-baldwin-in-paris-on-the-virtuosic-shame-of-giovannis-room/))

Here is the writerly danger, stated plainly: **if you remove the shame, you can accidentally remove the ache too, and get a flat, frictionless, post-conflict queerness that is dead on the page.** That would violate the Tone mandate as badly as any sanitized utopia. So the discipline for this world:

- **Keep the interiority and the weight.** Desire is still overwhelming, still humiliating in the way *all* real desire humiliates — the exposure of wanting, the loss of composure, the fear the other doesn't want you back. That is not shame; that is the human condition, and it survives the abolition of the closet.
- **Move the friction from *society* to *the self and the other*.** In the old world the obstacle was the law, the family, the risk of death. Remove those and the obstacles that remain are the **honest** ones: I want two people and can hold only one gaze at a time; I am afraid I am unlovable; I love someone who is dying; I love someone who cannot love me back the way I need. Baldwin's ache, rerouted through problems the world genuinely does not solve.
- **The strong masculine nonbinary person** CANON names is precisely the anti-David: strength as competence and protection, at home in the body, capable of being the object of a gaze without it costing them anything — *and still* undone by wanting someone. That's the target. Not shamelessness as flatness; shamelessness as the freedom to finally feel the full weight.

## 4. Wildcard lens (rolled: crip theory / desirability politics) — who gets to be wanted

My session id ends in **4** → wildcard lens **4: disability justice & crip theory**. **An honest flag on the Protocol first:** the wildcard is meant to be a lens I *would not* have chosen — one that destabilizes. Crip theory rolled onto queer desire and kinship sits squarely *inside* this domain's expected frame — the sibling health brief already leans on disability justice — so it did no destabilizing work here; it deepened the obvious rather than forcing a foreign angle, and the entropy claim ("session id ends in 4") is not independently verifiable. I keep it because the *question* it opens is the right one, not because the roll was a stretch. The health brief used disability justice for care *logistics* (pods, access intimacy as infrastructure); I turn it on the part it left untouched and the part this brief is actually about: **desire** — the least-comfortable question in the whole domain.

Crip theory (Robert McRuer; and the desirability-politics strand below) refuses the sorting of bodies into desirable and undesirable, and insists that a truly liberated world has to reckon not just with *access* but with **who gets loved, wanted, and fucked** — because a world can grant a disabled person every ramp and still leave them structurally unwanted. ([Crip Theory and the subject of abledness — Scandinavian Journal of Disability Research](https://sjdr.se/articles/10.16993/sjdr.1067); [Transforming Cultures of (Un)Desirability — Erickson, GJSS](http://gjss.org/sites/default/files/issues/chapters/papers/GJSS%20Vol%2012-1%20Erickson.pdf))

Two load-bearing ideas for the world:

- **Desirability is a distribution, and it's political.** Fatness, disability, transness, dark skin, age, visible difference — the old world sorted these to the bottom of the erotic hierarchy and called it "just preference." **Mia Mingus's** "Moving Toward the Ugly" argues for abandoning the fight to be included in the category *beautiful* and instead building solidarity with **the ugly, the not-magic, the not-cute** — the ones desirability politics will never rescue. A world that "routes toward difference" (CANON, the Weave) has to route toward it *erotically*, not just charitably, or it has quietly rebuilt the hierarchy one layer down. ([Leaving Evidence context; Erickson GJSS](http://gjss.org/sites/default/files/issues/chapters/papers/GJSS%20Vol%2012-1%20Erickson.pdf))
- **Access intimacy as erotic, not just logistical.** The health brief framed access intimacy (Mingus) as the design goal of a *care encounter*. Cripped onto desire: the person who *gets* your body without the exhausting briefing — who already knows how to move your chair, who is unbothered by the catheter, who finds the scar and the tremor and the slow speech not-in-spite-of but simply part of the person they want — that fluency is itself a form of intimacy the abled world rarely offers. In this world it's ordinary. And **Eli Clare's** work (*Exile and Pride*, *Brilliant Imperfection*) insists the disabled body is not a problem awaiting cure but a site of desire and history in itself. ([Clare, *Exile and Pride* — Duke UP](https://www.dukeupress.edu/exile-and-pride); [*Brilliant Imperfection* — Duke UP](https://www.dukeupress.edu/brilliant-imperfection))

**The knot I will not tie off:** the "devotee" problem. There exist people whose desire fixates on disability *as* the attraction — and disabled writers are genuinely split on whether that's affirming (finally, desired) or a fresh objectification (wanted *as* a wheelchair, not a person). A world that made disability ordinary and desirable still has this argument inside it, and it does not have a clean answer. Good. Put the argument in a character's mouth, not a resolution.

## 5. Non-monogamy, honestly: love isn't finite, but time and attention are

If the couple-form is no longer the legal container of care, what organizes intimacy? The most worked-out real framework is **relationship anarchy**, coined by **Andie Nordgren** (2006, "The short instructional manifesto for relationship anarchy"). Its principles map cleanly onto the Three Refusals: **love is abundant, not a scarce resource restricted to a couple; love and respect instead of entitlement and control; don't rank and file people into tokens of relationship-types; build for the lovely unexpected over obligatory duty; trust is better than surveillance; customize your commitments rather than inheriting a script.** ([The manifesto — Anarchist Library](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/andie-nordgren-the-short-instructional-manifesto-for-relationship-anarchy)) This is genuinely the anti-extraction ethic applied to love: no ownership, no metric of who ranks first, no scarcity manufactured to bind someone.

**And here is where I keep the tension, because the manifesto elides it.** "Love is not a finite resource" is true and load-bearing. But **time, attention, energy, and care-labor ARE finite** — brutally so. The honest critique of non-monogamy in practice is not that it's immoral; it's that:

- **Compersion** (feeling joy at a partner's other joy, the poly community's prized emotion) is real but not free — it sits *next to* jealousy, not instead of it, and demanding people feel it can become its own coercion, a new orthodoxy you're ashamed to fail.
- **Emotional labor distributes unevenly** — the sociologist **Elisabeth Sheff's** multi-year ethnography of polyamorous families found that in practice the scheduling, the check-ins, the processing, the reassurance, and above all the childcare still fell disproportionately on women, exactly as in the nuclear family; the structure changed, the tired person didn't. ([Sheff, *The Polyamorists Next Door*, Rowman & Littlefield 2014 — Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/polyamoristsnext0000shef); [review in *Sexualities*, SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1363460715583453))
- **Anti-hierarchy can hide hierarchy.** "We don't rank partners" can quietly mean *the person with more security and free time gets more, and calling it flat just removes their partners' standing to object.* Structurelessness has a tyranny (CANON already knows this about power); it applies to love too.

**For the world:** intimacy here is genuinely plural and un-owned — but the world is honest that **jealousy did not get abolished with capitalism.** It shows up. People negotiate it badly, sit with it, sometimes lose to it. The couple still exists as one shape among many, chosen and revocable, not mandatory and not forbidden. And the care-labor asymmetry is a real, ongoing, un-fixed argument between people who love each other — the thing they fight about at the sink.

## 6. Raising children without the nuclear unit — four precedents and one failure

**The evolutionary baseline (this is not utopian; it's the human default).** **Sarah Blaffer Hrdy**, *Mothers and Others: The Evolutionary Origins of Mutual Understanding* (Harvard, 2009), argues humans are **cooperative breeders** — we evolved raising children through **alloparents** (anyone who is not the biological parent investing care). Unlike other great apes, ancestral mothers let others hold and provision their infants, and Hrdy argues this *shared* rearing is what selected for human empathy, perspective-taking, and our large brains in the first place. The isolated nuclear mother-child dyad is not the natural state we're departing from — it's a recent, historically weird, and stressful *deviation* from how humans actually raise young. ([Hrdy, *Mothers and Others* — Belknap/Harvard UP](https://www.hup.harvard.edu/books/9780674060326); [cooperative breeding — Boise State Pressbooks](https://boisestate.pressbooks.pub/evolutionhumanbehavior/chapter/10-3-cooperative-breeding/)) "It takes a village" is not a slogan; it's the evolutionary record.

**Non-Western precedent I: Mosuo "walking marriage" (matrilineal China).** The Mosuo (~40,000 people near Lugu Lake) organize around **matrilineal households** where three generations of siblings live together their whole lives; there is often **no marriage and no cohabiting father** — partners meet through nocturnal *sese* ("walking") visits, relationships form and dissolve without contract or property entanglement, and **children are raised by the mother and her brothers**, in the mother's house, belonging to the maternal line. Crucially, evolutionary anthropologists link this specifically to **communal breeding**: the extended maternal household is a highly effective child-rearing machine *because* romantic pairing is decoupled from the economic unit of care. Desire and child-rearing are simply not the same institution. ([Communal breeding promotes a matrilineal social system — PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3619460/); [The World of Chinese](https://www.theworldofchinese.com/2021/05/chinas-last-matrilineal-society-the-mosuo-ethnic-group/)) *(Named specifically, not as a monolith; the Mosuo are a living people with their own sovereignty over their story — cited, not appropriated.)*

**Non-Western precedent II: West African child fostering.** The anthropologist **Esther N. Goody** (*Parenthood and Social Reproduction*, Cambridge, 1982) documented that among the **Gonja of northern Ghana**, nearly **a fifth of children** were being raised by aunts, uncles, or grandparents rather than birth parents — not as a crisis or an orphan-system, but as *normal, purposive kinship*. Goody split "parenthood" into five separable functions — bearing, rearing, educating/sponsoring, naming/status-giving, and holding rights over a child — and showed these routinely get **distributed across different adults**. Fostering circulates children to strengthen kin ties and match children to the households best able to teach them a trade. Raising a child is a role that can be *delegated and shared* without anyone being a failed parent. (Goody, *Parenthood and Social Reproduction*, Cambridge UP 1982; [Child Fostering in West Africa — Brill](https://brill.com/display/title/23906))

**Indigenous / on-this-land precedent: extended kinship among specific Coast Salish nations (sovereignty central).** First, the correction this brief owes its own method: *"Coast Salish" is not one people or one kinship system.* It is a **language-family grouping of many distinct, sovereign nations** — Musqueam, Katzie, Stó:lō, Lummi, Suquamish, Duwamish, Puyallup, the Hul'qumi'num nations, and more — with real differences in practice; an AI must not flatten them into a single "the Coast Salish." So, attributed and nation-specific rather than pan-tribal: among the **Musqueam and Katzie** Halkomelem-speaking communities Wayne Suttles worked with, the winter longhouse held an **extended family of many related households** under one cedar-plank roof, and prestige and belonging ran through **affinal ties across households**, not the isolated couple (Suttles, *Coast Salish Essays*, 1987 — esp. "Affinal Ties, Subsistence and Prestige"). Among the **Stó:lō**, descent is reckoned through **both** lines and **names are inherited intangible property** carrying ceremonial standing and lineage — documented not only by the outside ethnographer **Crisca Bierwert** (*Brushed by Cedar, Living by the River*, working with Stó:lō) but, load-bearingly and first, by **Stó:lō scholars themselves**: Albert "Sonny" McHalsie (Naxaxalhts'i) and Keith Thor Carlson's community-authored *A Stó:lō–Coast Salish Historical Atlas*, and the **Hul'qumi'num Treaty Group's** own account of names as intangible property. Cite the nation on its own kinship before the ethnographer. ([Suttles, *Coast Salish Essays* — Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/coastsalishessay0000sutt); [Suttles/Bierwert via Bruce Miller, *Be of Good Mind*, UBC Press intro PDF](https://www.ubcpress.ca/asset/9568/1/9780774813235.pdf); [*A Stó:lō–Coast Salish Historical Atlas* — Carlson & McHalsie/Naxaxalhts'i, SRRMC](https://www.srrmcentre.com/publications); [Intangible Property within Coast Salish — Hul'qumi'num Treaty Group, WIPO paper](http://www.hulquminum.bc.ca/pubs/paper-wipo.pdf)) **The knot:** these are *living, sovereign* peoples' kinship systems, on whose land this fiction sits. They are not ours to redistribute, and the specifics of names, ceremony, and adoption are governed by each nation, not by a bioregional commons and certainly not by an AI. CANON's sovereignty clause binds here hardest: name the *specific* nation and the scholar, prefer the nation's own scholars, keep the authority with them, and step back. And the CANON caution on **Paula Gunn Allen / "two-spirit"** applies — a modern pan-Indian umbrella can erase nation-specific realities; use ballroom's *documented* six-gender system (§2) as the load-bearing model and let each nation's gender be spoken *by* its own voices, not ventriloquized.

**The failure that must stay on the page: the kibbutz.** From the 1920s, Israeli kibbutzim tried the most thorough real experiment in **collective child-rearing**: children lived in **children's houses**, slept communally away from parents, and visited their parents only a few hours a day, explicitly to raise a "new man" free of the bourgeois family. It was abandoned — and the developmental research on *why* is precise and load-bearing. Aviezer, van IJzendoorn, Sagi, and Schuengel's review of seventy years of the data localized the damage to **communal *sleeping*** specifically — the severed nighttime bond, night care handed to whoever was on shift — which correlated with **insecure attachment**; high-quality *daytime* multiple-caregiving did **not** interfere with close parent–child bonds. By the late 1980s virtually every kibbutz had **returned children to sleep in their parents' homes** — and the ones who insisted on the change were *the generation that had been raised in the children's houses.* ([Aviezer, van IJzendoorn, Sagi & Schuengel, "'Children of the Dream' Revisited: 70 Years of Collective Early Child Care in Israeli Kibbutzim," *Psychological Bulletin* 116(1), 1994 — PDF](https://www.marinusvanijzendoorn.nl/wp-content/uploads/2012/07/Aviezer-Van-IJzendoorn-Sagi-et-al-1994.-Psychological-Bulletin.-Collective-early-childcare-kibbutzim.pdf)) **The lesson is exact and it is a warning to this whole project:** distributing care is the human norm (Hrdy; the Mosuo; the Gonja; specific Coast Salish nations, §above) — but **severing the specific, primary, attached bond** (here, at night) is not the same thing, and children punished the difference. Alloparenting *around* a durable core works; replacing the core with a rota does not. A world that gets high on "abolish the family" has to metabolize the children's house or it will hurt kids.

## 7. Elder care and the labor question — who actually does it

The child-rearing precedents all cut the other way too: the Mosuo maternal household, the Coast Salish longhouse, and the West African compound are also **elder-care** systems — the grandmother is not warehoused, she is the center of the house and the keeper of the children while the middle generation works. Hrdy's cooperative-breeding thesis has a famous corollary, the **grandmother hypothesis**: human females live decades past fertility, uniquely among primates, *because* post-reproductive grandmothers materially improve their grandchildren's survival. Elders are not a cost the system carries; in the cooperative-breeding frame they are load-bearing. ([Hrdy, *Mothers and Others* — Belknap/Harvard UP](https://www.hup.harvard.edu/books/9780674060326))

**But keep the tension sharp, because this is where the romance dies.** Multigenerational care is beautiful in a brief and grinding in a body. The honest facts the world must not hide:

- **It is overwhelmingly gendered and it exhausts people.** In every one of these systems, the day-to-day of feeding, washing, and turning the frail elder falls on women and femmes, and the "warmth of the extended family" is often one specific tired daughter's unpaid decade. Removing the wage does not remove the exhaustion; it can *hide* it, because now there's not even a paycheck to point at.
- **Chosen family is worse at slow, un-fun, un-glamorous care.** Weston's chosen families excelled at the *crisis* — the deathbed, the eviction, the ER. They are documented to be **weaker at the twenty-year grind** of dementia, incontinence, and boredom, because friendship networks thin exactly when a person stops being able to reciprocate or be fun. The dance floor remembers its dead (music brief) — but who sits with the living elder who no longer knows the songs?
- **The person with no web.** Every model here assumes you *have* kin, chosen or blood. The disability-justice literature is blunt that the people most in need of care are often those **least able to build and maintain the reciprocal networks** that care runs on — the abrasive, the isolated, the cognitively disabled, the one nobody's house wants. A gift-and-web economy has a specific, dark failure mode: **it can leave the least lovable person to die alone, and call it community.**

This is the trolley problem the health brief flagged (the person who refuses care) rerouted through kinship: **a world that makes care a gift rather than a right has to answer for the person no one chooses.** The honest answer is not a warm one. It's a standing, boring, *obligatory* backstop — the thin reliable layer, like the money — that catches the unloved precisely *because* love failed them. Care as a gift for most; care as a floor-level right for the ones the gift skips.

## 8. Rule Zero — the steelmanned case for the family, from opponents who disagree with each other

The domain's radical pole is **family abolition**. **Sophie Lewis**, *Abolish the Family: A Manifesto for Care and Liberation* (Verso, 2022), makes the strongest recent case: the privatized nuclear family is a "scaled-down reproduction of the brutalities of capitalist social relations," it hoards care behind a front door, it makes a child's whole fate depend on the lottery of which family they're born into, and its unpaid labor is "endless and exhausting." She traces the demand back through Fourier, Kollontai, Firestone, and gay liberation. Family abolition, properly understood, does not mean *ending love or care* — it means **un-privatizing** them, so no one's survival depends on the family they happened to land in. ([Verso](https://www.versobooks.com/products/2890-abolish-the-family); [The Case for Abolishing the Family — IAI](https://iai.tv/articles/the-case-for-abolishing-the-family-auid-2267)) This is basically the world's own instinct, and I could stop here and preach. Rule Zero forbids it.

So — the opposition, steelmanned, from **two camps that despise each other**, so neither becomes its own echo:

**Opponent A — Christopher Lasch (the tragic-conservative left).** *Haven in a Heartless World* (1977). Lasch is routinely misread; his actual argument is *harder* than the slogan. He held that the family was **the last institution not yet colonized by the market and the therapeutic-managerial state** — and that "liberating" people from it mostly meant **handing intimate life over to experts, agencies, and commodified services** that answer to no one and love no one. His warning to a project like this one is precise: when you "socialize" care, you don't necessarily get a warm commune — you often get **the state and the professional** in the crib, and a thinner, more surveilled, more administered intimacy than the flawed family you replaced. ([Haven in a Heartless World — Norton](https://wwnorton.com/books/9780393313031); [The Radical Lasch — American Conservative](https://www.theamericanconservative.com/the-radical-lasch/)) Lasch is uncomfortable precisely because he's a *leftist* making the anti-abolition case — the family as a fragile refuge from capital, not an arm of it.

**Opponent B — Melinda Cooper (the anti-conservative left).** *Family Values: Between Neoliberalism and the New Social Conservatism* (Zone, 2017) — and she would reject Lasch flatly. Cooper's disquieting finding: the family is **not** capital's victim or its refuge; it is capital's **preferred instrument.** Neoliberalism didn't atomize us into individuals — it **re-privatized the welfare state back onto the household**, making "family responsibility" (student debt guaranteed by parents, elder care dumped on daughters, the family as the unit of debt) the mechanism by which the public safety net was dismantled. ([Family Values — Princeton UP](https://press.princeton.edu/books/paperback/9781935408345/family-values); [Modern Family — Dissent](https://dissentmagazine.org/article/family-values-melinda-cooper-review/)) **Here is the trap Cooper sets for THIS world, and it's the sharpest thing in the brief:** a post-extraction society that says "care should be done by neighbors and chosen kin, not the state" is using *the exact rhetorical move* neoliberalism used to gut public provision. "The community will care for its own" is the sentence that closed the public hospitals. A bioregional commons that devolves all care to houses and webs and *doesn't* fund a hard floor is not the opposite of Reaganism — it can be its mirror image with better aesthetics. Cooper's warning and Lasch's warning point in opposite directions and both are true, which is exactly why neither can be waved away.

**Opponent C (from inside our own coalition) — the disability-justice critique of chosen-family romance.** Even sympathetic disabled writers warn that valorizing *chosen* family can quietly punish the people who **can't** perform the reciprocity, charm, and scene-participation that earn you a chosen family in the first place. "Found family" can become a new meritocracy of lovability. (Follows directly from §7 and the health brief's care-web literature — [Care Work, Piepzna-Samarasinha](https://arsenalpulp.com/Books/C/Care-Work).)

**The synthesis the world should actually hold** (not resolve): abolish the family as the *privatized, mandatory, only container* of care — yes. But keep a **durable, primary, attached bond** for children (the kibbutz lesson), and keep a **funded, obligatory, un-chosen floor** under the unlovable (the Cooper lesson). The utopia is not "everyone is in a warm web." The utopia is "the web is real for most, *and* there is a floor for the ones the web drops, *and* nobody pretends the floor is love."

## 9. Disconfirming evidence & failure modes

Real evidence this vision has failed, has a dark side, or won't scale:

1. **The kibbutz reversed itself** (§6). The most serious collective-child-rearing experiment ever run was abandoned by its own graduates for causing attachment damage. Any "abolish the family" enthusiasm has to answer this and mostly doesn't.
2. **Ballroom houses carry real exploitation and abuse.** The house is not a safe utopia. Economic desperation pushes members into survival sex work with documented violence and murder; power runs steeply downhill from parent to child, and the same de-sexualization norm that protects (Bailey) is violated in practice by predatory "parents." Chosen family concentrates power over vulnerable kids with *no external accountability at all* — the music brief's open question ("what happens when a venue-parent turns out to be a creep") is not hypothetical. ([Excalibur](https://www.excal.on.ca/arts/2025/03/08/ballroom-culture-a-vibrant-history-and-lasting-legacy/); [INTO](https://www.intomore.com/culture/the-colonization-of-ballroom-culture/))
3. **Chosen family is documented to be shaped like the wound (Weston, §1)** — it reproduces the exclusions and favoritisms of the blood family it fled, and it is weakest at slow un-glamorous long-haul care (§7).
4. **Non-monogamy's labor and jealousy don't abolish; they redistribute or hide** (§5). Compersion-as-obligation is its own coercion; the emotional-labor asymmetry survives the structural change intact.
5. **The Cooper trap** (§8): "community cares for its own" is historically the *precise* language used to dismantle public care. A gift-economy that skips the floor can be austerity in solarpunk clothing.
6. **The unlovable are the load-bearing failure** (§7). Every web-and-gift model leaves the person no one chooses, and can dress abandonment as community. This is the domain's darkest and least-resolved edge.
7. **Scale and enforcement.** Mosuo, Gonja, and Coast Salish kinship webs are *dense and geographically stable* — everyone is enmeshed for life, which is what makes them work *and* what makes them inescapable (the same density that catches you also surveils you, arranges you, and won't let you leave). Import the warmth and you import the enclosure. A high-mobility city of arrivals-from-flat-interior-towns may not be able to grow webs that thick — the very rootlessness that lets the queer kid escape is the rootlessness that makes deep kin webs hard to build.

## Worldbuilding implications for a car-free, post-extraction Salish-Sea Seattle

Concrete, scene-ready, and not sanded smooth.

1. **The house is a legal-ish person, and it holds the standing the AIDS wards denied.** A house — chosen-family unit with a name and a mother — is what signs for you at the clinic, holds your care directives, claims your body, and raises your kid if you go. The *entire point*, remembered out loud by the elders, is that in the before-times a partner of fifteen years had no standing at a deathbed and a blood father who never called did. The house exists so that never happens again. It is registered at the neighborhood commons the way a boat or a workshop is — visible, revocable, real.

2. **You become kin by doing kin-labor, not by category.** Mother, father, child, auntie are **roles you perform** (Bailey's overlapping kinship). The sixteen-year-old is a *child* of a house within a week (music brief) and may be an *auntie* to a smaller kid by the next winter. Nobody is born into their slot and nobody is stuck in it. Kinship is a verb here.

3. **Care of a child is split into named jobs, and different adults hold them.** Straight from Goody: bearing, rearing, teaching-a-trade, name-giving, and standing-for are **separable functions**, and a well-cared-for kid has a different beloved adult in each. The birth mother might not be the one who teaches you to splice a cable or the one whose name you carry. This is normal, not tragic — around the **one durable primary bond** the synthesis (§8) holds fixed, never instead of it.

4. **Desire and the raising of children are simply not the same institution** (Mosuo). Who you want, who you sleep with, who you love this year — decoupled from the durable household that holds the kids and the elder. A lover moving on doesn't detonate a child's home, because the child's home was never the couple. This removes the single largest engine of the old family's cruelty without pretending desire got tidy.

5. **Jealousy is a weather event, not a sin and not a solved problem.** People here are largely non-monogamous and un-owning (Nordgren's ethic is the water) — *and* they still get the hot ugly clench of it, still negotiate it badly, still sometimes lose to it. The scene-ready detail is two people who love each other genuinely, both committed to the ethic, fighting at the sink at 1am about who does the check-ins and the childcare and the remembering — because "love isn't finite" is true and "your time and attention are finite" is also true, and that argument never fully ends.

6. **Desirability is being *fought* toward redistribution — and the old hierarchy keeps trying to reassemble.** Mark this as the file's largest leap, evidence-vs-extrapolation on the page: the real seedlings are fat-liberation and disability-desirability organizing, Mia Mingus's actual community praxis, and ballroom's own re-valuation of the Black, brown, trans, and poor bodies the white pageants ranked at the bottom (§2) — but **no society has ever abolished the erotic hierarchy of lookism, ableism, fatphobia, and colorism,** and this world hasn't either. What it has is an ongoing, effortful, *incompletely-won* fight. The fat body, the disabled body, the scarred and trembling and slow-speaking body, the old body are *often* wanted, flirted with, taken home, not as charity — and just as often the reflex the old world drilled in reasserts itself, in a hesitation, a room that quietly sorts, a want that runs to the same few faces. Access intimacy is erotic here when it happens: the lover who already knows how to move the chair, who found the catheter unremarkable, who wants the whole apparatus of the person. Where the evidence ends and the hope begins is exactly here, and the world knows it. **And the argument runs underneath even the wins** (the devotee knot): is being wanted *for* your difference love, or a subtler objectification? Two characters hold opposite answers and both are right. Not a solved paradise — a place that decided to keep fighting a fight it has not won.

7. **The grandmother is the center of the longhouse, and one specific daughter is exhausted.** Multigenerational care is the norm and the elder is load-bearing, not warehoused (grandmother hypothesis, Coast Salish longhouse, Mosuo house) — *and* the day-to-day turning and washing and boredom falls, right now, on one tired person, usually a woman or femme, and the world has not fixed this, only made it visible. A scene: the beautiful full house at dinner, and afterward the one who stays to change the bed while the music starts up in the other room.

8. **The floor under the unlovable (§7–8) is a scene, not just a principle.** The full argument is made once above; here it only needs a body. Concretely: the abrasive one, the one who burned every web, the one too cognitively gone to charm anyone, in a plain room with a tender who is present on rotation and obligation, not love — and nobody in the room pretends otherwise. That is what the funded, un-chosen floor *looks like*, so the synthesis stays visible on the ground and not only in the argument.

9. **Specific Coast Salish nations' kinship is present, sovereign, and NOT the commons' to copy.** Each nation's longhouse extended-family web and inherited-name system is alive and governed by *that* nation — Musqueam and Katzie via Suttles, Stó:lō via McHalsie/Naxaxalhts'i, Carlson, and Bierwert (§6) — with the authority held by the people, not the ethnographer and never a pan-tribal "the Coast Salish." The bioregional houses are a *queer settler-and-arrival* institution that learned from ballroom, not a borrowing of anyone's Coast Salish kinship. The fiction names the *specific* nation and scholar and steps back; when it wants a six-gender system as ordinary, it uses **ballroom's documented one**, not a ventriloquized Indigenous one.

10. **The ache survived the liberation.** Nobody here is ashamed of wanting — but wanting still undoes them (Baldwin, rerouted). The strong masculine nonbinary dockworker, at home in their body, capable of being looked at without it costing anything, is *still* wrecked by loving someone who's dying, or someone who can't love them back the way they need. The friction moved from the closet to the honest human problems the world does not solve. That's the register: not shamelessness as flatness, shamelessness as the freedom to finally feel the full weight of it.

## Open questions for the next contributor

1. **What is the house's actual accountability mechanism when the mother is a creep?** Ballroom concentrates power over vulnerable kids with no external oversight (§9), and this world abolished the boss and the metric. So what catches a predatory house-parent — a council of other mothers? A revocation ritual? The children's own testimony weighed how? Answer this without rebuilding either the state's family court *or* a mob. (Ties to music brief open Q5.)

2. **Who is the person no house chose, and what is the floor actually like?** Write the unlovable one — the one the web dropped — and write the obligatory floor that catches them, honestly, un-warmly. This is the domain's darkest edge and the least explored. Don't redeem them into lovability; that's cheating.

3. **How does a child experience the split-parenting in the body?** Goody's separable functions and the "one durable core bond" rule are a design; a child's felt life is not a design. Write the kid who loves their name-giver more than their birth-mother, or who resents the rota, or who wishes desperately for the one boring nuclear family the world decided was cruel. Let the kid disagree with the utopia.

4. **What does a long, un-glamorous, non-crisis illness do to a chosen family** — the twenty-year dementia, not the dramatic deathbed? Weston's data says chosen families are strong at crisis and weak at the grind (§7). Write the grind. Who's still there in year eight, and who quietly stopped coming?

5. **Give the band girl a lover, and a jealousy she loses.** CANON and the music brief want her a house and a mother; this brief wants her a *desire* — the Baldwin ache, the first real wanting, and a compersion she can't feel no matter how correct the ethic says she should. Let it not resolve.

## PROPOSED CANON

*(World-level facts I'd propose; a human merges. Nothing here contradicts existing CANON — it deepens the "Culture, queerness, and care" section.)*

- **The house is the primary kinship-and-care unit** — a named, registered-at-the-commons, chosen-family formation (learned from ballroom, Black/Latinx/trans in origin, kept attached to that lineage) that holds legal-ish standing: it signs for its members' care, holds directives, claims bodies, and raises children. Founded specifically in memory of the AIDS-era wards where chosen family had no standing. Revocable and visible, never a slot you're born into — kinship is performed through labor.
- **Desire and child-rearing are decoupled institutions.** Who you love is not the container that holds the children; the house (or extended web) is. A relationship ending does not detonate a child's home. Children have **one durable primary bond that does not rotate**, with other care functions (teaching, name-giving, standing-for) distributed across named others.
- **Care runs on a gift layer plus an obligatory floor.** Most care is gift and web (thick, warm, chosen). Under it runs a thin, funded, *un-chosen, obligatory* care floor for the person no house chose — explicitly not called love, existing so the gift economy cannot abandon the unlovable and call it community. (This is the kinship-domain application of the existing thin-money / thick-gift structure.)
- **Desirability is treated as political and actively contested, not abolished.** The world *fights* the erotic hierarchy of lookism/ableism/fatphobia/colorism rather than having won — disabled, fat, old, trans, scarred bodies are ordinarily desired and not charity-included, access intimacy is erotic, *and* the old sorting keeps trying to reassemble. This is an incompletely-won project extrapolated from real fat- and disability-desirability organizing (§6, evidence-vs-hope marked there), not a finished fact. The devotee argument (wanted *for* difference vs. *as* a person) stays a live unresolved argument.
