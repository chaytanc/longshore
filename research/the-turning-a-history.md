# The Turning — a history

*A research brief for* The Reality Next Door. *Domain: how the world got from the extraction economy to the post-extraction Salish — decade by decade, grounded in real transition scholarship. Per `CANON.md`: the turning "was neither clean nor heroic," the working class "were turned against each other by the same media and politics that profited from their division, and mostly did not notice they were being used until it was too late," solidarity "was always fractured," and "before the accountable systems existed there was a lethal interregnum." This brief is the load-bearing history behind those single sentences. It does not tell it as a triumph.*

---

## A note on dates, and on who tells this

Canon fixes the *when* as "deliberately unfixed" — far enough that the turning is complete and half-forgotten, close enough that people still grieve the before-times. So this brief does **not** pin calendar years to the future. Instead it uses the periodization the world's own people argue about — a rough sequence of named decades that historians, elders, and the recall-councils fight over the boundaries of, the way we fight over when "the sixties" started. Every phase is mapped to a **real** historical transition, revolution, or collapse, cited, so the shape is buildable and not invented. Where a real event or figure is named (the Boldt decision, the WTO protests, Billy Frank Jr.), it is real and belongs to the actual record of this actual coast; where the future is extrapolated, the seam is marked.

And a discipline the whole brief holds: **the turning did not invent Coast Salish resistance to extraction — it belatedly deferred to it.** The Nisqually, Puyallup, Muckleshoot, Suquamish, Duwamish, Tulalip, Lummi and their neighbors had been fighting the extraction economy — the dams, the canneries, the state that jailed them for fishing their own treaty waters — for a century before any settler-descended crew picked up a crowbar. The people who "turned" were, in large part, latecomers to a fight the sovereign nations of this coast never stopped waging. Keeping that straight is the difference between history and flattery.

---

## The core claim, stated flat

There is a comforting version of this story and it is a lie. The comforting version is that people saw the extraction economy was killing the world, organized, and heroically built something better. That is not what the evidence of real transitions shows, and canon forbids us from telling it.

What real transitions show is uglier and more useful:

1. Market society **disembeds** — it tears economic life out of social life until land, labor, and money are treated as things to be bought and sold, and people are left exposed. This is Karl Polanyi's diagnosis in *The Great Transformation* (1944), and it is the precondition, not the turning.
2. Society reacts — a "**double movement**," a protective countermovement — but that reaction is *not automatically progressive*. Polanyi's whole terrifying point is that the countermovement to unregulated markets in the 1930s produced the New Deal in one place **and fascism in another**. The same wound can be dressed by a union hall or by a strongman. ([Polanyi's double movement — Development and Change, 2022](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/dech.12699); [The Great Transformation and the emergence of illiberalism — Illiberalism Studies Program](https://www.illiberalism.org/karl-polanyis-the-great-transformation-the-critique-of-liberalism-and-the-emergence-of-illiberalism/))
3. Between the dying order and the one that "cannot be born," there is an **interregnum** in which "a great variety of morbid symptoms appear" — Antonio Gramsci's line from the *Prison Notebooks* (c. 1930), and the single best three words for what canon calls the lethal gap. ([Gramsci, morbid symptoms — Wikiquote](https://en.wikiquote.org/wiki/Antonio_Gramsci); [Brill, "Morbid Symptoms: What Did Gramsci Really Mean?"](https://brill.com/view/journals/powr/1/2/article-p379_379.xml))

So the turning is the story of a countermovement that *could have gone either way,* went partly the wrong way first, cost a great many people their lives and livelihoods and dignity in the going, and only slowly, unevenly, and never completely bent toward the world we now inhabit. Now the decades.

## Decade −2 and −1: The Long Refusal (the manufactured division)

Before anything turned, the ground was prepared — for division, not for repair.

**The job blackmail was engineered, and it worked.** The phrase the labor movement used for it was "**job blackmail**": the ultimatum that a worker must choose between a paycheck and a livable world. It was not a natural fact; it was a strategy. When the U.S. Congress came close to climate legislation around 2009, fossil-fuel lobbyists ran **astroturf** campaigns — fake grassroots "consumer" groups secretly funded by the Western States Petroleum Association (whose members included BP, Shell, ExxonMobil, Chevron) — to manufacture the appearance of working-class opposition to climate action. The Union of Concerned Scientists documented the front groups by name. ([UCS, "How Fossil Fuel Lobbyists Used 'Astroturf' Front Groups"](https://www.ucs.org/resources/how-fossil-fuel-lobbyists-used-astroturf-front-groups-confuse-public); [Just Transition Alliance, "Beware Fossil Fuel Industry 'Astroturf' Campaigns"](https://jtalliance.org/2023/07/05/beware-fossil-fuel-industry-astroturf-campaigns/)) *This is the mechanism canon names — "the same media and politics that profited from their division."* The port worker and the marsh restorer were told they were enemies by the people who owned both the port and the marsh's poison.

**The one true alliance flickered here — in Seattle — and then it broke.** In the 1999 WTO protests, the "**Battle of Seattle**," Teamsters marched (loosely, incompletely) alongside environmentalists in sea-turtle costumes: "**Teamsters and Turtles, together at last.**" It is our own city's founding image of a labor-ecology alliance. But the honest record is that the alliance was thinner than the slogan: AFL-CIO leaders worked to keep the official labor march *away* from the occupied downtown, and the coalition frayed within years. The Blue-Green Alliance (Steelworkers + Sierra Club, 2006) formalized the hope and never delivered the power. ([MOHAI, "Teamsters, Turtles, and Beyond"](https://mohai.org/exhibits/teamsters-turtles-and-beyond-the-legacy-of-the-seattle-wto-protests/); [HistoryLink, "WTO Meeting and Protests in Seattle (1999)"](https://www.historylink.org/File/9183); [Briarpatch, "Teamsters and turtles, ten years on"](https://briarpatchmagazine.com/articles/view/teamsters-and-turtles-ten-years-on)) The world remembers this as the road not taken — the decade the countermovement could have been *joined* and instead was *split.*

**Who paid, this decade:** the deindustrialized working class, in their bodies, before any transition arrived. Anne Case and Angus Deaton documented the "**deaths of despair**" — the rise in mortality from suicide, alcohol, and drug overdose among working-age adults without a college degree, concentrated exactly in the deindustrialized regions, driven by "the declining relative position of white working-class households since the 1970s." U.S. life expectancy stalled in 2010 and fell in 2014 — a peacetime society killing its own abandoned workers by inches while the extraction economy still ran at full tilt. ([Case & Deaton, *Deaths of Despair and the Future of Capitalism*, Princeton UP](https://press.princeton.edu/books/hardcover/9780691190785/deaths-of-despair-and-the-future-of-capitalism); [Boston Review, "Deaths of Despair"](https://www.bostonreview.net/articles/joshua-cohen-angus-deaton-deaths-despair/)) **The crucial, un-triumphal fact: many of these people died before the turning, killed by the extraction economy itself, and the turning's histories are tempted to forget them because they don't fit either side's story.** Canon's crane-operator father — "the turning came and made his hands worthless, and then it made them criminal" — has his real prologue *here,* dying of despair while the cranes still swung.

## Decade 0: The Break (the Morbid Decade, the lethal interregnum)

This is the decade nobody agrees how to date and everybody agrees was the worst. Gramsci's interregnum. The old order was materially dying — climate shock, supply shock, the compounding failures of a growth economy that could no longer grow — and the accountable new systems did not yet exist. Into that gap fell the bodies.

**Two things are true at once, and the world refuses to collapse them.**

*First, the hopeful truth, steelmanned against my own darkness:* disasters do not usually produce the war-of-all-against-all that films promise. Rebecca Solnit's *A Paradise Built in Hell* (2009) surveys catastrophe from the 1906 San Francisco earthquake to Katrina and finds the ordinary response is "spontaneous altruism, self-organization and mutual aid" — neighbors and strangers feeding and housing each other, a "peak experience" of solidarity. The mutual-aid webs that became the care-web and the pods were *born in the Break,* not designed after it. ([Solnit, *A Paradise Built in Hell* — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Paradise_Built_in_Hell); [Mutual Aid Disaster Relief on Solnit](https://mutualaiddisasterrelief.org/news-items/a-paradise-built-in-hell-rebecca-solnit-on-the-extraordinary-communities-that-arise-in-disaster/))

*Second, the lethal truth canon insists on:* Solnit's own warning is that the deadly danger in disaster is usually not the crowd but the **elite panic** — authorities who imagine the public as a mob and respond with force, hoarding, and abandonment. Both happened. Some neighborhoods self-organized and saved each other; some frightened neighborhood boards rationed the last of the medicine "the way frightened people always decide: we helped the ones who were like us" (`threads/the-elder-remembers`). **There was no single interregnum. There was a patchwork — a block that became a commune next to a block that became a checkpoint — and which one you were born onto decided whether you lived.** This is the honest shape of the lethal gap: not uniform darkness, but a lottery of geography and luck, which is worse, because it means the deaths were contingent, not fated. Someone three streets over would have lived if the line had fallen differently.

**Who paid, this decade:** whoever the ad-hoc boards decided was *not like us,* and — this is the wildcard lens below, and it is not decoration — **disabled people first and worst.** The evidence from real climate disasters is unambiguous: people with disabilities are two to four times more likely to die or be injured in climate emergencies, and disaster planning routinely fails them; in Hurricane Katrina, disabled people were abandoned in evacuations and, in at least documented cases, killed by medical professionals rather than evacuated. Disability-justice scholars name the lived condition bluntly: to be disabled in a disaster is to be "mentally prepared to be abandoned and left to die." ([Health Affairs, "Global Disability Justice in Climate Disasters"](https://www.healthaffairs.org/doi/10.1377/hlthaff.2022.00474); [Sins Invalid, "Disability Justice is Climate Justice"](https://sinsinvalid.org/disability-justice-is-climate-justice/)) The lethal interregnum's first and least-counted dead were the people the old world had already built its buildings, its evacuations, and its triage protocols to leave behind. The world we now inhabit built the autonomous disabled-persons' councils *with a veto* (per `research/political-economy.md` §7, from the Rojava model) precisely because it remembers this — the veto is a scar.

## Decade 1: The Starving (car-starving, depaving, and its violence)

Now the physical turning — and canon is emphatic it "was violent, and the violence ran both ways." This is the decade of the crowbars.

The method was real: not banning cars but **starving** them — closing shortcuts, pulling parking, letting roads rot until driving became "slow and stupid," the Pontevedra model (~80,000 vehicles/day downtown to ~7,000; per `CANON.md` and `research/car-free-urbanism.md`). And the depaving — tearing up the asphalt to reach the soil "that had been holding its breath the whole time" — was real practice too. But `threads/the-elder-remembers` establishes the ground truth the brochures omit: it was **physically contested.** "A crew I wasn't on got beaten with their own bars in Georgetown. A woman I knew lost an eye. And we gave it back." The people who fought the crews were, overwhelmingly, "the men whose whole lives were the engine and the road" — and they were not villains. They were the ones the Long Refusal had spent two decades teaching to see the marsh restorer as the enemy instead of the owner.

**This is where the redundancy-to-criminalization pipeline runs, and it is the moral center of the whole history.** The crane operator who "could set a forty-ton box on a truck bed soft as you'd set down a sleeping child" was first made *worthless* (his skill de-valued), then made *criminal* (his resistance to that de-valuation prosecuted). Canon: "Some were made redundant, then criminal; their grief is legitimate and still largely unaddressed."

**The counter-model the world could have used and largely didn't (in time):** a real, planned **just transition** — the concept coined not by environmentalists but by *labor,* by Tony Mazzocchi of the Oil, Chemical and Atomic Workers union, who called first for a "Superfund for Workers" so that "workers... will not have to pay for clean air and water with their jobs, their living standards or their future." ([SMEA/UW, "A Beginner's Guide to a Just Transition"](https://smea.uw.edu/currents/a-beginners-guide-to-a-just-transition/); [Labor Network for Sustainability, "'Just Transition' — Just What Is It?"](https://www.labor4sustainability.org/uncategorized/just-transition-just-what-is-it/)) The tragedy the world carries is that just transition *existed as a fully-formed idea for decades* before it was applied at scale — the crane operators were owed it, by name, and the Break came before the promise was kept. When the world finally built its just-transition institutions (Decade 2), it built them partly out of guilt for the men it had already broken.

**And here is the honest disconfirmation of the whole violent-interregnum frame** (steelman #2, a case that argues the lethal break was *avoidable*): Germany's **Ruhr** shows a coal phase-out managed over *sixty years* without a bloodbath — employment fell from over 500,000 in the 1950s to the last mine closing in 2018, cushioned by decades of retraining, economic diversification, early retirement, and massive public investment, "handled comparatively well... in a socially just way." ([WRI, "Germany: The Ruhr Region's Pivot"](https://www.wri.org/snapshots/germany-ruhr-regions-pivot-coal-mining-hub-green-industry-and-expertise); [Oei et al., "Lessons from Germany's hard coal mining phase-out," *Climate Policy* 2019](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/14693062.2019.1688636)) The Ruhr is the reproach the world lives with: *it did not have to be this bloody.* The violence of our Starving was a **failure**, not a necessity — the price of doing in a panicked decade what Germany did over three careful generations. The world does not get to call its dead unavoidable. It got there faster, and the speed was paid in bodies. Some in-world historians argue the Break's violence was the tragic cost of a real deadline (the climate would not wait sixty years); others argue it was the cost of *not starting the Ruhr-slow work two decades earlier, during the Long Refusal.* Canon leaves this argument unwon, and so does this brief.

**Who paid, this decade:** the engine trades — drivers, mechanics, longshore and terminal workers, the whole culture of men who were "somebody" because of what they could do with a machine — made redundant, then criminal, then, some of them, priced out past the walkshed to Kent and Renton (`threads/central-district-walk`, `the-elder-remembers`), dying "out past the walkshed" while the city that broke them congratulated itself on its clean river.

## Decade 2: The Long Repair (the Settling — institutions built late, and out of guilt)

Only now — after the Break's deaths and the Starving's beatings — do the accountable systems canon depends on get built. The order matters and it is not flattering: **the beautiful institutions came after the bodies, not before.** They are, in large part, monuments to what was done without them.

This is the decade of everything in `research/political-economy.md`: the consent circles and sociocracy, the mandated-recallable delegates (Bookchin/Rojava), the participatory budgeting (Porto Alegre, Kerala), the worker co-ops with the compressed pay ratio (Mondragón's ~6:1), the gift-and-tally economy (Graeber/Mauss), the restorative and transformative justice pods that replaced the cage. The rationing that had been done by frightened ad-hoc boards in the Break became the accountable **Hard-Choices Protocol** — public, revisable, forbidden from scoring human worth, ties broken by lottery, with the 1962 Seattle "God Committee" named as its founding warning and a wall of names for those it fails to save (per `CANON-PROPOSALS.md` #6 and `research/scarcity-and-hard-tradeoffs.md`). *Every one of these was a lesson learned from a specific death.*

**Sovereignty did not "arrive" this decade — it was finally deferred to.** The legal architecture was already there, and it was Coast Salish and it long predated the turning. The **Boldt decision** (*United States v. Washington*, 1974) had already affirmed the treaty tribes' right to half the harvestable salmon and their standing as **co-managers** of the fishery — won by the **Fish Wars** of the 1960s–70s, the civil-disobedience "fish-ins" for which Nisqually leader **Billy Frank Jr.** was arrested some fifty times. When the settler-descended commons finally organized itself around watersheds and restraint, it was adopting a coordination model the sovereign nations of this coast had been forced to defend at gunpoint for generations. Canon's treaty co-management (Boldt form), the salmon-seniority order un-outvotable by the settler majority (`CANON-PROPOSALS.md` #7), and the more-than-human legal personhood (Whanganui model) are the *maturation* of that deferral. The un-triumphal note: it should not have taken the near-death of the whole system for the settler world to defer to the law that was always there.

**The just transition, kept late.** The retraining, the co-op placements, the honoring of the old engine-trade skills (the crane operator's precision re-valued as the boatwright's, the depaver's strength re-valued as the seawall-keeper's) — this is the Mazzocchi promise finally kept, a generation after it was owed. Mondragón's real precedent matters here: when its flagship co-op Fagor went bankrupt in 2013, the federation **relocated the majority of displaced worker-members into other co-ops** rather than firing them (`research/political-economy.md` §3). The world's just-transition guilds work the same way — but they were built too late for the first generation, who mostly died unreconciled. **Solidarity was rebuilt as an institution precisely because it had failed as a sentiment.**

**Who paid, this decade:** the ones too old or too broken to be retrained — the interregnum's survivors who got a soft kept life they never wanted and could not forgive, like the elder who "would give a year of this soft kept life to drive one more hour through a city that did not love me." The repair came for their children, not for them.

## Decade 3 and after: The Tending (unfinished, and it does not heal)

The world we now inhabit. Canon: "It is not finished." The sea is "restored but not healed." The Tending is not an ending; it is a permanent condition of maintenance, and it carries at least three unresolved wounds forward:

- **The border problem.** Degrowth in one bioregion, surrounded by places that never turned, has a permanent hard edge — cheap goods and capital press in from the growth-world, and someone must do "the ethically compromising work of that exchange" (`research/political-economy.md` open Q2). The Tending never closes this seam; it staffs it.
- **The unreconciled dead and the unreconciled living.** The mother "three streets over who would spit on the word *worth*" gets a vote that "weighs the same as mine," forever. The world does not require reconciliation and does not get it.
- **The fragile restoration.** Wild Chinook and the orcas that need them are "still fragile, and everyone knows it." The Dark Garden is "one budget cycle away from becoming deep-sea mining." The turning bought a reprieve, not a victory.

## The wildcard lens: disability justice & crip theory

*Rolled, not chosen. Entropy source: the last hexadecimal digit of this session's id (`a872f444-…-04`) is `4` → lens 4 on the AGENTS.md table. This is emphatically not a lens I would have picked for a political-economic history, which is the whole point of rolling.*

Crip theory reframes disability not as a defect in a body but as a **relation between a body and a world built to exclude it** — and it insists on reading history from the position of the bodies a transition treats as *surplus.* Applied to the turning, it changes what counts as the story:

1. **Every transition has a triage, and triage is where a society's real values are written.** The Break's lethal interregnum was, structurally, a mass triage event — and disability-justice scholarship shows triage protocols encode who is considered "worth" saving, which is exactly the metric `CANON.md`'s Refusal 1 forbids. The Hard-Choices Protocol's **ban on quality-of-life/QALY worth-scoring** (`CANON-PROPOSALS.md` #6) is a *directly crip-theoretical* institution: it exists because the old world's triage math counted a disabled life as less. The lottery is not squeamishness; it is the refusal of the calculation that killed people in the Break.
2. **"Slowness is not a tax" is a disability-justice principle before it is an aesthetic one.** Canon's fast-boat rule — "the disabled traveller... takes it with *nobody's* eyebrow raised... Aestheticizing slowness into a duty is a quiet cruelty this world refuses" — is the turning's hardest-won lesson from crip theory. A solarpunk world that romanticizes the slow crossing as virtue would rebuild the exact exclusion it claims to have left. The world knows this because in the Break, the fetish for the slow and the local *killed the people who couldn't do slow.*
3. **Disabled and chronically-ill people were disproportionately the mutual-aid *builders,* not just the victims.** Disability-justice organizing — pods, care webs, "all we have is each other" — long predates and directly seeds the care infrastructure this world runs on. ([Teach the Future, "All We Have is Each Other: Climate Change and Disability Justice"](https://www.teachthefuture.uk/blog/all-we-have-is-each-other-climate-change-and-disability-justice)) The "tender," the "house," the "rest-ledger" (`CANON-PROPOSALS.md`) are crip-designed infrastructure. Reading the turning through this lens, the people the extraction economy discarded as unproductive turn out to have been carrying the blueprint for the world that replaced it. The turning did not save disabled people; disabled people's survival practices helped save the turning. That inversion should be visible in the fiction.

## The non-Western precedent, load-bearing: Cuba's Special Period

The single closest real-world rehearsal of a forced, rapid degrowth — and it is Global-South, not Anglophone, exactly as AGENTS.md's decenter-the-default rule demands.

When the Soviet Union collapsed (1991), Cuba lost ~80% of its imports and exports almost overnight and its GDP fell by more than a third — a sudden, unchosen amputation of its fossil-fuel supply. Cubans call it the **Special Period.** What followed had, in scholars' words, "elements of an experiment in degrowth": a forced reorganization onto low-input, local, labor-intensive production, most famously the explosion of **urban organic agriculture** (the *organopónicos*) that now rhymes exactly with our floating gardens and mussel-rope shoreline. ([*The Power of Community: How Cuba Survived Peak Oil* — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Power_of_Community:_How_Cuba_Survived_Peak_Oil); [Borowy, "Degrowth and public health in Cuba," *J. Cleaner Production* 2013](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0959652611004963))

**But — and this is why it is load-bearing rather than inspirational — the Special Period was brutal, and the brutality is the lesson, not a footnote.** People went hungry; the average Cuban reportedly lost weight; and in 1994 the **rafter crisis** (*balseros*) saw more than 35,000 people flee on makeshift rafts across shark water, many drowning, rather than endure it. ([1994 Cuban rafter crisis — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1994_Cuban_rafter_crisis)) Cuba is proof that a society *can* survive the amputation of fossil energy and even build something admirable from it — **and** that the survival is a horror in the living, that people vote against it with their bodies and their boats, and that "resilience" narratives (the popular documentary *How Cuba Survived Peak Oil* leans warm) can quietly launder real suffering into an inspiring lesson for Northern audiences. The world's Break was its own Special Period, and the *balseros* are its own "out past the walkshed" — the people for whom the turning was so unbearable they risked death to leave it. **A history that forgets the rafters is propaganda.**

## Disconfirming evidence & failure modes

Where this whole history is weakest, told straight:

1. **The interregnum may not have been necessary at all — see the Ruhr.** The strongest disconfirmation of canon's "lethal interregnum" is that managed transitions *without* mass death are real and documented (Ruhr, 60 years, cited above). This means the Break's body count is an indictment, not a law of nature — and a lazy telling could use "the interregnum was tragic but necessary" as an alibi. The world must not. The honest position: the deaths were a *failure of foresight and solidarity,* avoidable in principle, and calling them necessary is itself a small betrayal of the dead.
2. **Disaster may make solidarity, not slaughter — Solnit disconfirms the dark frame.** If Solnit is right that catastrophe usually elicits altruism, then a relentlessly grim interregnum is *empirically* overdrawn — the real danger is elite panic and top-down force, not neighbor-against-neighbor. Canon should (and, in `the-elder-remembers`, does) hold both: real mutual aid *and* real rationing-by-tribe, patchworked. But a writer who paints only darkness is getting the disaster sociology wrong.
3. **Degrowth might just be immiseration with better branding.** The steelmanned economic objection: capping or shrinking output in a still-poor, still-unequal world may simply mean less for the poor unless redistribution is nearly perfect — and perfect redistribution has never happened. Branko Milanovic argues degrowth is infeasible without redistribution no society has managed; from the *left,* Matt Huber and other eco-productivists argue "a politics of less" bleeds into austerity and abandons the working class that needs *more,* not less. ([Milanovic critique summarized, with Hickel's rebuttal](https://www.jasonhickel.org/blog/2017/11/19/why-branko-milanovic-is-wrong-about-de-growth); ["The illusion of degrowth in a poor and unequal world," Global Policy Journal](https://www.globalpolicyjournal.com/blog/13/12/2023/illusion-degrowth-poor-and-unequal-world)) *Note these two opponents disagree with each other* — Milanovic from liberal economics, Huber from the productivist left — so neither becomes its own echo. Both land on the world: if the Salish's abundance-by-arrangement ever falters, the turning curdles into exactly the "politics of less" its enemies predicted, and the border-world's cheap goods start to look like mercy.
4. **The transition itself can be the thing that kills — the post-Soviet warning.** The most sobering real datum: the *wrong kind* of transition is a mass-casualty event. Post-Soviet "shock therapy" — rapid privatization and liberalization — is linked to on the order of **several million excess deaths** across the 1990s (Russian male life expectancy fell from 64 in 1990 to 57 by 1994), with peer-reviewed work tying the mortality spike specifically to *mass privatization,* not merely to the end of communism. ([Stuckler, King & McKee, "Mass privatisation and the post-communist mortality crisis," *The Lancet* 2009](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0140673609600052); [Ndefru, Sullivan & Hickel, "Excess Mortality During the Dissolution of the Soviet Union," SSRN 2024](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6148078)) This is the darkest lamp on the whole road: a society *can* end an extractive order and, by doing it in a panicked, top-down, unaccountable way, kill millions of the very people it claimed to free. The turning's lethal interregnum is not sci-fi; it is a documented category of historical event, and it is what happens when the countermovement is captured by shock instead of stewarded.
5. **The countermovement can go fascist — Polanyi's warning, unresolved.** Nothing guarantees the double movement bends toward commons rather than strongman. The same disembedding that produced our Weave and pods produced, in the real 1930s and arguably again in the 2020s, the far-right authoritarian version. Canon's world is the branch that went the *other* way — but a full history must admit that in the wider world (and possibly in the Salish's own back-country and border-towns), the countermovement *also* produced checkpoints, purity politics, and strongmen, and that some of "the places that never turned" didn't fail to turn — they turned *fascist.* The Weave routes difference outward toward safety precisely because the alternative was real.

## Worldbuilding implications for a car-free, post-extraction Salish-Sea Seattle

Scene-ready texture, all extrapolated from the cited history above.

1. **The Break is periodized differently by different people, and the argument is itself a scene.** An elder dates "the Break" from the year the medicine stopped; a co-op historian dates it from the first depaving; a Nisqually fisher dates the whole extraction economy's death to Boldt, 1974, and finds the settlers' "turning" a belated and slightly comic latecoming. When these three are in a room, the disagreement over *when it started* is really a disagreement over *whose suffering counts as the beginning.* No calendar on the wall; the dating is contested out loud.

2. **There is a physical archive of the manufactured division — the astroturf letters.** Somewhere (a library, a Weave relay, a shoebox) survive the fake "grassroots" mailers and consumer-group pamphlets from the Long Refusal, the ones that told the crane operator the marsh restorer was coming for his job. People bring them out to teach the young how the division was *made,* not born. A scene: a teacher reading a slick 2009 astroturf flyer aloud, and the room going quiet at how well it worked.

3. **The engine-trades grief has a name, a season, and rituals that don't fully console.** The men (mostly men) made redundant-then-criminal are memorialized — but uneasily, because some of them beat depaving crews and some of the depavers beat them. A commons that composts almost everything has *not* composted this. There is a day, or a wall, or a guild rite where the boatwrights and seawall-keepers acknowledge they are the re-valued descendants of the broken engine trades — and someone always walks out of it.

4. **The fast boat is a monument to a specific death.** Everyone knows the fast-boat-for-the-grieving rule exists because in the Break and after, the slowness-fetish *killed people* — the disabled, the too-late, the elder who wouldn't cross fast to her dying father (`the-elder-remembers`). The rule is enforced with a particular fierceness, and a young person who sighs at someone taking the fast boat gets corrected sharply by an old one who remembers why.

5. **The disabled-persons' council veto is understood as a scar, not a courtesy.** Older residents can tell you the specific abandonment in the Break that the veto answers — the block that evacuated and left someone, the board that rationed by "worth." The veto "is not politeness; it's structural, and older residents remember arguing bitterly about it before it became just how things are" (`research/political-economy.md` §7). A scene: the council using the veto, and an old person weeping not at the decision but at the fact that the power now exists.

6. **The rafters are remembered — the people the turning was too much for.** In the Break and the Starving, some people fled the turning itself — out past the walkshed, or off the coast entirely, choosing the growth-world's poison over the Salish's austerity. They are the local *balseros.* There is grief and defensiveness about them; the world's confidence in itself wobbles at their memory. A returning rafter, or a rafter's child come back, is a loaded arrival on any dock.

7. **The just-transition guilds carry institutional guilt in their founding documents.** The retraining halls and co-op placement circles that re-valued the engine trades were built a generation too late, and they know it. Their charters (extrapolated from Mondragón's Fagor relocation and Mazzocchi's Superfund) open with an admission of who they failed first. Being placed by one is a mixed feeling: gratitude tangled with *this should have existed for my father.*

8. **The Boldt line is load-bearing and visibly older than everything else.** In any scene touching salmon, shoreline, or water, the treaty co-management structure is the *oldest* institution in the room — predating the turning, won by the Fish Wars, un-outvotable by the settler majority. Characters treat it not as a gift the turning gave the nations but as the pre-existing law the turning finally stopped fighting. A settler-descended water-steward defers to a Muckleshoot or Suquamish co-manager not out of etiquette but because the law was always theirs.

9. **Nobody says "we won."** The load-bearing verbal tic of the whole world: the turning is spoken of in the register of survival and cost, never victory. "We got through it" and "it cost what it cost" and "the river runs clean and a boy died three streets over" — the grammar is elegiac, not triumphant. A character who speaks of the turning as heroism marks themselves as young, naive, or selling something.

10. **The morbid-symptoms decade left physical patchwork in the built city.** Because the interregnum was a block-by-block lottery, the city's fabric still shows it: this street's buildings survived because its board self-organized; that street's gaps are where a checkpoint or a hoarding board let it rot. Walkable urban archaeology of who-helped-whom in the worst decade — legible to anyone who knows how to read it, invisible to those who don't.

## Open questions for the next contributor

1. **What actually broke first?** This brief keeps the Break's *trigger* deliberately vague (climate shock + supply shock + growth's failure to grow). A brave contributor could pick the specific cascade — was it a Cascadia earthquake, a fisheries collapse, a food-import cutoff, a currency failure? — and walk one concrete Break decade in real Seattle geography, honoring the patchwork (a saved block beside an abandoned one) rather than uniform apocalypse.

2. **Where did the countermovement go fascist, and how close did it come here?** Polanyi and canon both insist the turning *could* have gone strongman. Someone should write the back-country or border-town that turned *the other way* — the checkpoint that never came down, the purity politics — and the Salish character who has kin there. The Weave routes difference toward safety; what is it routing them *away from?*

3. **What is owed to the broken engine trades, concretely, and who pays it?** Canon says their grief is "legitimate and still largely unaddressed." *Unaddressed* is a standing debt. Is there reparation, a pension-in-gift, a formal apology, a guild that carries their names — or is the honest answer that the world simply lets a generation die unreconciled and calls it the cost? Walk one crane operator all the way to his grave.

4. **Whose history is this, and who gets to periodize it?** If a Nisqually or Duwamish historian dates the "real" turning to the Fish Wars and Boldt, the settler-descended periodization (Long Refusal → Break → Starving) is provincial. A contributor working *with* the sovereignty-leading mandate could write the turning from a Coast Salish frame in which the settler crisis is a late chapter of a much longer story — carefully, naming the nation and source, not appropriating.

5. **Does the Tending ever end — and what is the failure mode of permanent maintenance?** If the world is never "finished," what is the specific way that a maintenance-society *fails*? Burnout of the tenders? Atrophy of the institutions (Porto Alegre's warning)? A generation that never saw the Break and takes the whole thing for granted? The turning's final open wound might be that its own success erases the memory that made it possible.

## PROPOSED CANON

*World-level facts proposed by this brief. Nothing here contradicts `CANON.md`; it deepens the single canon sentence that "the turning was neither clean nor heroic." For human review.*

- **The turning has an in-world periodization, and it is contested, not fixed.** The rough sequence — **the Long Refusal** (the manufactured division), **the Break / the Morbid Decade** (the lethal interregnum), **the Starving** (contested depaving and car-starving), **the Long Repair / the Settling** (institutions built late), **the Tending** (unfinished present) — is how the world's own people narrate the turning, and *when each phase began is argued about,* the argument itself encoding whose suffering counts as the start. Dates stay deliberately unfixed per canon.
- **The lethal interregnum was a patchwork, not uniform darkness.** Real disaster sociology (Solnit) means the Break produced spontaneous mutual aid *and* frightened rationing-by-tribe *side by side, block by block* — the care-web and pods were born here — and which block you were on was a lottery that decided who lived. The deaths were contingent, not fated, which is worse.
- **The Break's body count was a failure, not a necessity.** Managed transitions without mass death are real (Ruhr, 60 years) and lethal transitions are real (post-Soviet shock therapy, millions of excess deaths). The world does not get to call its dead unavoidable; calling the interregnum "tragic but necessary" is a named, refused alibi.
- **Just transition existed as a fully-formed idea (Mazzocchi's "Superfund for Workers," coined by labor) for a generation before it was applied — the engine trades were owed it by name and the Break came first.** The world's just-transition guilds carry founding guilt for the first generation they failed.
- **Coast Salish sovereignty was deferred-to, not granted.** The treaty co-management architecture (Boldt, 1974; won by the Fish Wars and figures like Billy Frank Jr.) predates the settler turning and is the oldest institution in the post-extraction order; the settler "turning" was a belated latecoming to a fight the Nisqually, Puyallup, Muckleshoot, Suquamish, Duwamish and neighbors never stopped.
- **The Salish has its own "rafters" — people for whom the turning was so unbearable they fled it,** toward the growth-world's poison, out past the walkshed or off the coast (extrapolated from the 1994 Cuban *balseros*). They are remembered with grief and defensiveness; a returned rafter is a loaded arrival.
- **Disabled people were the interregnum's first and least-counted dead — and disproportionately the builders of the mutual-aid infrastructure that survived it.** The Hard-Choices Protocol's ban on worth-scoring and the disabled-persons' council veto are scars answering specific Break-era abandonments.
- **The world's grammar for the turning is elegiac, never triumphal.** "We got through it," "it cost what it cost." To speak of the turning as heroism marks a character as young, naive, or selling something.
