# Disability and Access — a research brief

*Domain: disability justice as load-bearing infrastructure in a car-free, post-extraction Salish-Sea Seattle. How access gets built into the bones of a place, who still gets failed, and the real fight between what a body needs and what a body chooses.*

This brief is deliberately **not** a subsection of `research/health-and-care.md`. Care and access are different problems. Care is what a clinic does to a body; access is what a world does *for* and *around* a body so the body can be in the world at all — and access is a design problem, a political problem, and a love problem before it is ever a medical one. The most important recent thinking here comes not from doctors but from disabled activists of color, and it says something the whole project already half-believes: **independence is a myth, interdependence is the fact, and a society is only as free as its least-accommodated body can move through it.**

The through-line: **you cannot design one door that fits every body, and pretending you can is its own quiet cruelty.** The best access is not a ramp bolted on after the fact; it is a world built, from the studs, expecting the full range of bodies — and then honest about the bodies it still fails, because it will fail some. The band girl's ear (see `threads/the-band-girl.md`) is the honest anchor of this whole brief: a world that regrew the salmon cannot regrow her cochlear hair cells, and no amount of access love changes that. Access is not cure. Hold both.

## 1. The social model — and the exact place it breaks

The intellectual hinge of everything below is the **social model of disability**, formalized by the UK's **Union of the Physically Impaired Against Segregation (UPIAS)** in its 1976 *Fundamental Principles of Disability*, and named "the social model" by disabled sociologist **Mike Oliver** in 1983. It splits two things ordinary language fuses:

- **Impairment** — a fact of a body (a spine that doesn't carry signal, an ear with no hair cells, a mind that processes sideways).
- **Disability** — the *exclusion* a society imposes on that body by building stairs instead of ramps, sound instead of sign, speed instead of patience.

The claim: a wheelchair user is not disabled by their legs, they are disabled by the *step*. Remove the step and the disability, in that moment, is gone — the impairment remains. This is genuinely load-bearing for our world, because it relocates the problem from the person's body (which a market wants to fix, cure, or discard) to the built environment (which a commons can choose to build differently). ([Oliver, *The social model of disability: thirty years on*](https://www.um.es/discatif/PROYECTO_DISCATIF/Textos_discapacidad/00_Shakespeare2.pdf); [social model overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_model_of_disability))

**And here is where the honest brief refuses the clean version.** Disabled sociologist **Tom Shakespeare** — himself a wheelchair user, a person of short stature, no enemy of the movement — argues that the strong social model's simplicity "is also its fatal flaw." Most disabled people do not have stable congenital impairments like blindness or deafness; they have rheumatism, cardiovascular disease, degenerative and painful conditions "mainly associated with ageing." For those bodies, *impairment itself* — the pain, the fatigue, the fact of dying sooner — is a real and central part of the life, and **no ramp removes it.** A world that says "disability is only ever society's fault" ends up telling the person in chronic pain that their suffering is a false consciousness that better architecture would dissolve. It won't. ([Shakespeare, *The Social Model of Disability*, in the Disability Studies Reader](http://thedigitalcommons.org/docs/shakespeare_social-model-of-disability.pdf); [Shakespeare, *an outdated ideology?*](https://www.um.es/discatif/PROYECTO_DISCATIF/Textos_discapacidad/00_Shakespeare2.pdf))

This is Del's ear stated as theory. Build every ramp, sign every song, cushion every floor — and mammalian hair cells still don't come back. **Our world holds both halves: it takes the social model seriously enough to rebuild the city around it, and it takes Shakespeare's correction seriously enough never to pretend the rebuilding is a cure.** Access is the work. It is not redemption.

## 2. Universal design, the curb cut, and why "for all" is a half-truth

**Ronald L. Mace** contracted polio at nine, used a wheelchair, and became the architect who named **universal design**: "the design of products and environments to be usable by all people, to the greatest extent possible, without the need for adaptation or specialized design." He founded the Center for Universal Design at NC State in 1989; in 1997 he and a multidisciplinary team published the **seven principles** (equitable use; flexibility in use; simple and intuitive; perceptible information; tolerance for error; low physical effort; size and space for approach and use). ([Mace / 7 principles](https://universaldesignaustralia.net.au/7-principles-of-universal-design/); [Rocky Mountain ADA — evolution of UD](https://rockymountainada.org/news/blog/evolution-universal-design-win-win-concept-all))

The proof-of-concept is the **curb cut**. Wheelchair activists in Berkeley in the early 1970s — Ed Roberts and the "Rolling Quads," who founded the first Center for Independent Living in 1972 — demanded that sidewalks be cut down to meet the street. Once built, *everyone* used them: strollers, delivery carts, luggage, cyclists, the elderly, a kid on a skateboard. A thing built for a despised minority turned out to serve the majority. That pattern now has a name — the **curb cut effect** — and it is the single most persuasive argument for building access in from the start: access designed for the edge routinely improves the center. ([Curb cut effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curb_cut_effect))

**But do not let the curb cut become a comfortable slogan.** Disability-studies scholars have a sharp critique: "universal" is a promise no design can keep. A person with low vision may need bright, even light; a person with photophobia or migraine or certain autistic sensory profiles needs it *dim*. A signer needs a wide, open sightline; a person with agoraphobia or PTSD needs an enclosed, defensible corner. These are **conflicting access needs**, and no single room satisfies both. The honest scholarship reframes them not as bugs to engineer away but as "generative sites for reflection and collaboration in access-making" — i.e., the access has to be *negotiated between real people in a real room*, not solved once by a designer and frozen. ([conflicting access needs / critique of UD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_design); [Simulating Disability in UD Teaching: A Critique](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30371417/))

The design lesson for the world is therefore **two-layered**: build the *fixed* layer universally (the ramp, the wide door, the tactile edge, the level threshold — the stuff of Mace), and build a *negotiated* layer on top of it for the needs that conflict (dimmable light, movable seating, quiet rooms and loud rooms, a person you can ask). The first layer is architecture. The second is a social practice — and its name is access intimacy.

## 3. Access intimacy: the design goal is a feeling

**Mia Mingus** — disability-justice writer and organizer, one of the movement's core theorists — named the thing our world should be *engineered to produce*: **access intimacy**, "that elusive, hard-to-describe feeling when someone else 'gets' your access needs." It can be as small as a knowing look, as quiet as a friend who already pulled the chair back before you asked, as deep as a stranger who reads your need off you and meets it without making it a transaction. ([Mingus, *Access Intimacy, Interdependence and Disability Justice*](https://leavingevidence.wordpress.com/2017/04/12/access-intimacy-interdependence-and-disability-justice/))

Its opposite is **forced intimacy**: the grinding, humiliating labor of having to *strip down and show all your cards* — disclose your diagnosis, your bowels, your trauma, your income — to a stranger with the power to grant or deny access, just to get through a door abled people walk through free. Mingus calls forced intimacy "a cornerstone of how ableism functions." The disabled person is perpetually made to over-explain themselves to earn the basic conditions of being present. ([Mingus, *Forced Intimacy: An Ableist Norm*](https://leavingevidence.wordpress.com/about-2/); [Stimpunks summary](https://stimpunks.org/2023/04/01/accessibility-access-intimacy-and-forced-intimacy/))

This maps *directly* onto the First Refusal. The capitalist-metric world runs on forced intimacy: prove your disability to the insurer, the benefits office, the eligibility panel, the airline, again and again, the burden of proof always on the body. **Our world's move is to make access intimacy structural** — to build so much access into the default that disclosure is rarely the price of entry, and where a need must be met individually, it is met by someone who already knows your stairs, your triggers, your good ear. This is the same insight `research/health-and-care.md` reached for the clinic (the tender who knows your cat's name); here it generalizes to the whole built and social world. Access intimacy is what a good care web *feels like from the inside*, and it is the opposite of a form.

## 4. Care webs and interdependence — including where they break

**Disability justice** as a named framework was launched in 2005 by disabled activists of color **Patty Berne, Mia Mingus, and Stacey Milbern**, incubated at **Sins Invalid**; its ten principles include *interdependence, collective access, and collective liberation* ([Sins Invalid, 10 Principles](https://sinsinvalid.org/10-principles-of-disability-justice/)). The organizing structure it hands us is the **care web**, theorized by **Leah Lakshmi Piepzna-Samarasinha** in *Care Work: Dreaming Disability Justice* (2018): a self-organized group — disabled, non-disabled, or (usually) mixed — who provide each other care and access "as a collective responsibility and a pleasure," not as charity from the well to the sick. Collective care, she writes, means "shifting our organizations to be ones where people feel fine if they get sick, cry, have needs, start late because the bus broke down, move slower... and these aren't things we apologize for." ([Piepzna-Samarasinha, *Care Work*](https://arsenalpulp.com/Books/C/Care-Work); [*Care Webs* chapter, full text](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5a354481a9db0961249f52ec/t/5f5c2593ce23ec19f7cae9f8/1599874451239/Care+Webs.pdf))

The disability-justice reframe of **independence → interdependence** is exactly the substrate a gift-and-commons economy needs. The able world's fantasy of the self-sufficient individual is, to disabled people, both a lie (nobody is self-sufficient; the abled just have their dependencies hidden and subsidized) and a cruelty (it shames the person whose dependence is visible). Our world takes the disabled analysis as the *general* truth: everyone is a node in a web, and needing each other openly is the water, not a failure.

**But `Care Work` itself refuses the rosy version, and so must we.** Piepzna-Samarasinha names the failure mode plainly: care webs **burn out**. They run on a small number of already-exhausted people — "what happens when sick and disabled people are the only ones who do not forget about each other, but we all are extremely, extremely tired." The web can collapse when its two reliable members get sick the same week. It can curdle into obligation and resentment (the gift's shadow, per canon). It can quietly reproduce the same hierarchies — the same few women of color doing the actual labor while others theorize it. **A world built on care webs has not abolished the problem of who actually shows up at 3 a.m.; it has only made that problem visible and shared instead of privatized and hidden.** That is better. It is not solved. Some webs fail and someone falls through, and the world should show that happening.

## 5. Deaf culture: not a lack, a language and a gain

Deafness is the sharpest test of the social model, because the Deaf world does not want to be cured — it wants to be *understood as a linguistic minority*, not a defective majority.

**Audism** — the term coined by Deaf scholar Tom Humphries in 1975 — is the assumption that hearing and speech are inherently superior, that a deaf person's flourishing is measured by proximity to hearing. Medical audism is why clinicians "often prioritise the acquisition of hearing above all else, even if this has negative consequences for the child." ([Audism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Audism); [audism & childhood cochlear implants](https://www.medicaldevice-network.com/features/audism-cochlear-implants/))

Against it, **H-Dirksen Bauman and Joseph Murray** advanced **Deaf gain** (from 2008): stop asking what a deaf person *lacks* (hearing loss) and ask what the world *gains* by the existence of people with a different sensory orientation and a fully-formed visual-spatial language. Sign languages are not English-with-hands; they are complete natural languages that do things spoken language can't, and Deaf spatial cognition, visual attention, and community design are contributions, not deficits. ([Bauman & Murray, *Deaf Gain*](https://josephjmurray.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/05/Murray-and-Bauman-Deaf-Gain-in-OXford-Handbook-FINAL-1.pdf))

This becomes architecture in **DeafSpace**, developed by architect **Hansel Bauman** at **Gallaudet University** from 2005 — five principle-sets (space and proximity, **sensory reach**, mobility and proximity, light and color, acoustics) comprising 150+ built elements. Concretely: **wide walkways so two people can sign while walking** without colliding; **gentle ramps instead of stairs** so you can sign-and-walk without watching your feet; **diffuse, glare-free natural light** so hands read clearly; **curved, horseshoe seating** so a whole group holds sightline; automatic doors so nobody breaks a sentence to grab a handle; muted, skin-contrasting wall colors so signing hands pop. ([DeafSpace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DeafSpace); [Gallaudet DeafSpace Guidelines](https://gallaudet.edu/campus-design-facilities/campus-design-and-planning/deafspace/); [Washingtonian on Gallaudet](https://washingtonian.com/2016/01/13/gallaudet-universitys-brilliant-surprising-architecture-for-the-deaf/))

DeafSpace is the deep version of Pech's haptic floor (`the-band-girl`): the whole environment reorganized around a non-dominant sense. And it curb-cuts — glare-free light and clear sightlines are good for everyone.

**The hard fracture stays on the page: the cochlear implant war.** Postlingually deafened adults widely experience implants as a gift to autonomy and work; implanting *deaf children* is bitterly contested. Deaf-culture defenders (e.g. philosopher **Robert Sparrow, *Defending Deaf Culture: The Case of Cochlear Implants***) argue that mass pediatric implantation, coupled with oral-only education that withholds sign, is a slow cultural erasure — the extinction of a minority language, one child at a time. The counter-camp argues that a hearing parent choosing an implant for their child is exercising ordinary parental autonomy, and that a movement pressuring them not to is *itself* coercive — sacrificing an individual child's options on the altar of a culture's survival. **Both are right about something and each disqualifies the other's easy answer.** (See §9 — these are the two steelmanned opponents, and they disagree with *each other*.) ([Sparrow, *Defending Deaf Culture*](https://researchmgt.monash.edu/ws/portalfiles/portal/252777366/3227796_oa.pdf); [*Genocide or Salvation?* — TIME](https://time.com/76154/deaf-culture-cochlear-implants/); [cochlear implants & autonomy in deafened adults](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8328847/))

## 6. Crip time, and the fast-boat principle made into law

Canon already refuses the aestheticization of slowness ("aestheticizing slowness into a duty is a quiet cruelty"; the fast boat "takes it with *nobody's* eyebrow raised"). Disability theory gives that instinct its rigor and its name.

**Crip time** (Alison Kafer, *Feminist, Queer, Crip*, 2013; Ellen Samuels, *Six Ways of Looking at Crip Time*, 2017) is the recognition that disabled and chronically ill bodies do not run on the clock the productive world assumes. Kafer's formulation: crip time is not "the same time, but slower" — it is a *reorientation to time*, bending the clock to bodies rather than bending bodies to the clock. Samuels refuses the sunny read: crip time is also **grief time and vampire time**, the time stolen by flares and hospitalizations, the futures foreclosed. It is liberatory *and* a loss, both. ([Samuels, *Six Ways of Looking at Crip Time*](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/319412659_Six_Ways_of_Looking_at_Crip_Time); [Kafer / crip time overview](https://thepolyphony.org/2024/01/26/medhums-101-what-is-crip-time/))

Crip time cuts **both directions**, and this is the part the slow-city romantics miss:

- Sometimes a disabled body needs *more* time — the meeting that starts when everyone has actually arrived, the deadline that flexes, the ferry you can board slowly.
- Sometimes it needs *less* — the person in pain, in a flare, in a crisis, who cannot spend a scenic afternoon crossing the Sound, who needs the **fast boat, now, without a look.** Slowness is a luxury of the well-bodied, and forcing it on everyone as a virtue is ableism with good aesthetics.

The **fast-boat principle systematized**: our world builds the slow, beautiful, low-carbon default (self-trimming sail-ferries; see `research/car-free-urbanism.md`) *and* a silent electric hydrofoil (the real **Candela P-12**) that anyone can take with no justification owed. Crip theory turns the canon's gentle norm into a *right*: speed-on-demand is access infrastructure, not indulgence, and the burden of proof never lands on the body that needs it. Nobody has to *prove* they deserve the fast boat — proving it would be forced intimacy (§3). You raise your hand, you board, the eyebrow stays down. This is the single cleanest place the whole "reachable by design" ethic and disability justice fuse.

**"Spoon theory"** (Christine Miserandino, 2003) is the honest energy model underneath all of this: you wake with a finite ration of spoons, each task spends one, and when they're gone they're gone. It is *literally* the winter-energy-ration problem of `research/scarcity-and-hard-tradeoffs.md` scaled to a city — and it means the people who have rationed energy their whole lives are the world's actual experts on living well inside a hard cap. **The winter energy commons should be designed by them, not by the healthy who discover restraint as a season.**

## 7. Neurodiversity and the sensory commons

**Judy Singer** coined **neurodiversity** in the late 1990s: the idea that autism, ADHD, dyslexia and other cognitive variations are natural human variation, not disorders to be normalized away. The design consequence is a world that stops treating one nervous system's comfort as neutral and everyone else's as special-needs. Open-plan, bright, loud, fast, socially-dense environments are not "normal" — they are *tuned to a particular nervous system* and hostile to others.

This is where universal design's promise breaks hardest (§2): the autistic person who needs low stimulus and the person who needs high stimulus to stay regulated cannot share one room-setting. So the world's answer is not one perfect room but a **legible sensory field** — every gathering space published with its real sensory profile (this room is loud and bright; that one is quiet and dim; the shake-floor is here; the sign-forward corner is there), so a body can *route itself* to the setting it needs without asking permission or explaining why. The Weave, which cannot rank but can *carry*, is well-suited to publishing sensory maps of the city the way it carries ferry times. Access becomes wayfinding (see §10, the wildcard lens).

## 8. The core tension: collective access vs. individual autonomy

The assignment's knot, steelmanned on both sides, because it is real and the world does not resolve it.

**The case for collective access (disability justice).** Access is not an individual's private problem to solve with private money and private disclosure; it is a *collective responsibility and a shared good*. Left to individuals, access becomes forced intimacy and market-rationed — you get the accommodation you can prove, afford, and beg for. Made collective, it becomes the water everyone swims in. Interdependence is the truth; autonomy-as-self-sufficiency is the lie the extraction world told to privatize the cost of bodies. ([Sins Invalid principles](https://sinsinvalid.org/10-principles-of-disability-justice/))

**The case for individual autonomy (the independent-living tradition).** The disability-rights movement that *won the ramps* — Ed Roberts, the Berkeley CIL, the Section 504 sit-ins, the ADA — was built on a fierce insistence on **self-determination**: nothing about us without us; the disabled person, not a caretaker or a committee, decides how they live, what risks they run, and what "a good life" means for their body. This tradition is rightly suspicious of "we know what's best for you," because that sentence built the institutions, the sheltered workshops, the forced sterilizations, the locked wards. **"Collective access" can slide, frictionlessly, into collective control.** ([Independent Living movement](https://www.britannica.com/topic/independent-living-movement); [NCIL](https://ncil.org/about/independent-living/))

**Where they actually collide** — five real fault lines the world lives on, not around:

1. **The protective override.** Tuesday tells Del to stop fronting, move to the board, *live longer* (`the-band-girl`). It is the most loving thing anyone has said to her and it is the wall between her and the only thing she crossed a continent to do. The care web, exercising collective wisdom, becomes the thing that decides a sixteen-year-old's body for her "for her own good." Del's refusal is not foolishness; it is autonomy — the dignity of risk, the right to spend your own body on the thing you love. **The world has no clean answer. It has Tuesday's grief and Del's fury, both legitimate, unresolved.**

2. **Conflicting access needs (§2).** Bright light for low vision vs. dim for photophobia. The collective cannot satisfy both in one room; someone's access is someone else's harm. Negotiation, not design, is the only tool, and negotiation has winners.

3. **Disclosure vs. privacy.** Collective access is easier when needs are known — but *requiring* people to make their bodies legible to the group is forced intimacy. The autonomy to *not* disclose, to keep your diagnosis yours, is a real good that trades against the efficiency of shared access.

4. **The cochlear implant (§5).** The Deaf collective's cultural survival vs. an individual child's (or parent's) autonomy to choose hearing. Collective good vs. individual choice, with no neutral ground and a language's existence on the line.

5. **Need-weighted rationing (`scarcity`).** A collective that weights the scarce winter kilowatt-hours toward medical need must *decide whose need counts* — which means the collective adjudicating individual bodies, the God Committee's temptation in a kinder coat.

**The world's honest posture:** it leans collective in the *build* (access is infrastructure, paid for in common, never begged for) and fiercely autonomous in the *choice* (the disabled person is the authority on their own life, including the right to refuse care, run risk, and spend their body). It holds the two in tension by a rule: **collective responsibility for the conditions, individual sovereignty over the decision.** Build every ramp; never push anyone up it. And it admits, out loud, that the rule frays exactly where it matters most — over the child who can't yet choose, the crisis where a life is at stake, the body whose chosen risk breaks the hearts of the web that loves it.

## 9. Disconfirming evidence & failure modes

Real evidence that the vision has a dark side, has failed, or won't scale.

1. **Care webs burn out — this is documented, not hypothetical.** *Care Work*'s own central essay is about exhaustion: the same few disabled/QTBIPOC people carrying the web until they collapse, others forgetting until they need it again. Mutual-aid access is fragile precisely because it runs on the people with the least surplus energy. A world that makes care webs load-bearing has made its floor out of its most tired people. ([*Care Webs*](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5a354481a9db0961249f52ec/t/5f5c2593ce23ec19f7cae9f8/1599874451239/Care+Webs.pdf))

2. **"Universal" design is a promise no one can keep.** Conflicting access needs are mathematically unsatisfiable in a single design; "design for all" repeatedly turns out to mean "design for a slightly wider particular body while still excluding others." The universalist frame can *hide* the people it still fails behind the word "universal." ([UD critique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_design); [Aalborg critique](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30371417/))

3. **Disability simulation — the empathy shortcut — backfires.** Blindfolding the abled or putting them in a wheelchair for an afternoon reliably *increases* stigma and pity rather than understanding, because it delivers the shock of sudden impairment without the adaptation, skill, and community that real disabled life contains. A world that tries to *manufacture* access intimacy through simulation will make ableism worse. Access intimacy has to be earned in relationship, not staged. ([Disability simulation critique](https://www.accessibility.com/blog/disability-simulations-harmful-or-enlightening); [Aalborg](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30371417/))

4. **The strong social model over-promises and can gaslight the person in pain (§1).** Shakespeare: for degenerative, painful, and fatal conditions, "it is harder to ignore the negative aspects of impairment," and a model insisting disability is *only* social exclusion effectively tells that person their suffering is an architecture problem. It isn't. Del's ear is not a design flaw. ([Shakespeare](http://thedigitalcommons.org/docs/shakespeare_social-model-of-disability.pdf))

5. **Collective access has an institutional shadow.** Every historical apparatus of disability *control* — the asylum, the colony, the sheltered workshop, the eugenic sterilization — was justified as collective care for vulnerable bodies. "We'll decide what's best for you, together" is the exact grammar that built the institutions the independent-living movement spent decades tearing down. A commons that forgets this rebuilds the ward with nicer paint. (See `research/health-and-care.md` §5 on deinstitutionalization done wrong.)

6. **Deinstitutionalization without the web kills people.** The real-world lesson: emptying institutions before building community access produced homelessness and trans-institutionalization into jails. A car-free utopia that abolishes the ambulance and the ward without *first* building the fast boat, the crisis house, and the care web will fail its most disabled members lethally and quietly. Build the web before you demolish the wall.

7. **Emergency reveals who the design forgot.** Fire, flood, earthquake: when the elevator dies, the wheelchair user above the ground floor is stranded in an "area of refuge" *waiting to be rescued* — access as afterthought, dependency at the worst moment. A car-free city with no private vehicles and slow default transit has to answer, concretely, how a non-ambulatory body gets off a burning floating row-house at 2 a.m. If it can't, the aesthetic killed someone. ([disability & emergency evacuation](https://unitedspinal.org/when-seconds-matter-accessible-home-fire-safety-for-the-disability-community/))

8. **"Nothing about us without us" indicts this very document.** I am an AI, not a disabled person, synthesizing disabled people's hard-won theory into worldbuilding furniture. Disability justice's first principle is *leadership of the most impacted*. A brief written by a machine trained on disabled writers' words — possibly scraped without consent — theorizing their liberation is exactly the extraction canon confesses. The correction cannot be a disclaimer; it can only be that real disabled people rewrite this, and that the fiction routes actual authority to disabled characters rather than admiring them from outside.

## 10. Wildcard lens — Pacific wayfinding & celestial navigation

*Seed / entropy I couldn't game: the harness placed this session under scratchpad path `.../claude-502/...`. Reading **502 → last digit 2 →** lens **2, Pacific wayfinding & celestial navigation** (AGENTS.md table). It is not the lens I'd have reached for on a disability brief — I'd have picked something social. Here's the map.*

Micronesian and Polynesian navigators cross thousands of miles of open ocean **with no instruments** — no compass, no chart, no GPS. They read a **star compass** (the rising and setting points of named stars around the horizon), the **swell patterns** refracting off unseen islands, the flight lines of birds, the color of water, the feel of the hull. The Caroline Islands system called **etak** tracks position by holding a *reference island* off the beam and moving it, in the mind, across a sequence of star bearings — a moving mental model of a world you cannot see. Mau Piailug carried this knowledge from Satawal to crew Hōkūleʻa across the Pacific in 1976 and proved the tradition was a rigorous science, not luck. ([Polynesian navigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynesian_navigation))

Three mappings onto disability and access, none of which I'd have found without the roll:

- **Navigation without the dominant sense is a highly-trained expertise, not a deficit.** The wayfinder navigates the ocean the way a blind traveler navigates a city — by **auditory cues, echo, texture underfoot, remembered sequence, and a mental model held against the body** rather than by the sense the majority privileges. Blind navigation research describes exactly this: echolocation yielding distance, size, and material of unseen objects; tactile landmarks and turn-by-turn proprioception; a nonvisual environmental literacy "rarely considered by sighted peers," at which blind travelers *outperform* the sighted. Both the wayfinder and the blind traveler hold a whole world in a channel the majority calls empty. This is **Deaf gain** generalized (§5): a different sensory orientation is a body of expert knowledge, not a lack. ([blind navigation & echolocation](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0175750); [auditory wayfinding cues](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6930908/))

- **The environment must be built to be *read* — a legible field, not a set of signs.** The wayfinder can only navigate because the ocean is dense with reliable, redundant cues (stars in fixed relation, swells with stable geometry). A city is navigable to a non-dominant sense only if it is built the same way: consistent tactile edges, sound that means something, textures that don't lie, a grammar of the ground that holds still long enough to be learned. This is DeafSpace's **sensory reach** (§5) and the neurodivergent **legible sensory field** (§7) restated as *piloting*: design the city so a body can dead-reckon through it without vision, without hearing, without asking. Access is wayfinding infrastructure.

- **The knowledge is invisible to those who don't hold it — and can be extracted or erased.** Outsiders sailing with a wayfinder see "magic"; there is no magic, only a discipline transmitted across generations and nearly killed by colonization (Hōkūleʻa was a *revival*). Disabled people's environmental expertise is invisible to the abled in exactly this way — read as helplessness, when it is skill — and it is transmitted through community and lost when community is broken up "for their own good" (§8, the institution). **Protect the knowledge-holders and their transmission, or the expertise dies and looks, to the majority, like it was never there.** And the sovereignty note lands hard here: Pacific wayfinding is a specific living Indigenous science; I invoke it as structural analogy and name its holders, I do not claim it or flatten it.

## 11. Precedents — one historical, one non-Western

*Both precedents make the same astonishing point: **deafness stops being a disability when the whole community signs.** The social model, proven twice by real villages. This is the deepest argument in the brief — that "access" is not accommodation of a minority but a property of a shared world.*

**Historical (and Anglophone, kept honest as such): Martha's Vineyard, c. 1690s–1900s.** A recessive gene brought from the Weald of Kent produced, on Martha's Vineyard, a rate of hereditary deafness that peaked at roughly **1 in 155** islanders (versus ~1 in 6,000 nationally). The response was not institutions or pity. **Hearing and Deaf Vineyarders alike grew up bilingual in sign.** Deaf people held office, farmed, fished, married, gossiped, ran for town positions — and, in Nora Ellen Groce's finding, *were not seen and did not see themselves as handicapped or as a group apart.* Access was total because the whole community had absorbed the minority's language as ordinary. The disability, in the social-model sense, simply did not exist there. ([Groce, *Everyone Here Spoke Sign Language*](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctvjghtwk); [Deafness on Martha's Vineyard — Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/science/deafness-on-Marthas-Vineyard))

**Non-Western / living: Bengkala ("Desa Kolok"), north Bali, Indonesia — present day.** A recessive gene (**DFNB3**), present for at least seven generations, means **42 of Bengkala's ~3,000 villagers are deaf from birth** — but the signing community is vastly larger: an estimated **57% of the village signs**, so of 1,200–1,800 signers only ~4% are actually deaf. Hearing parents teach their children the village sign language **Kata Kolok** at home as a second or third language; it is used in temple, market, work, and politics. Deaf villagers hold recognized community roles (including as gravediggers and in a distinctive ceremonial dance). It is a *sovereign, non-Western, currently-living* proof that a whole society can build itself bilingually around a sensory minority. ([Kata Kolok](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kata_Kolok); [In Bali's Deaf Village — VICE](https://www.vice.com/en/article/theres-a-village-in-bali-where-everyone-knows-sign-language-511/))

**Nuance-over-comfort, so neither becomes a fantasy:** Both cases rest on genetic isolation, small scale, and a *particular* impairment (congenital deafness) that is stable, non-painful, and language-bearing — precisely the kind Shakespeare says the social model fits *best* (§1). Neither village had to reckon with the degenerative, painful, or fatal impairments where access does not dissolve the problem. Neither is a template you can just scale to a city of every kind of body. And both faced erosion — Vineyard sign died as the isolate opened up; Kata Kolok is pressured by national sign languages, schooling, and now "deaf village tourism" that risks turning a living community into a spectacle. The lesson is real and bounded: *a world can choose to make itself bilingual around a minority, and when it does, the disability recedes* — but only for the impairments that a shared language and a level threshold can actually reach.

**Sovereignty (non-negotiable):** the Salish-Sea version of this is **not** ours to invent by borrowing Bali or the Vineyard. This is Coast Salish land — Duwamish, Suquamish, Muckleshoot, and other Coast Salish peoples are sovereign here — and any bioregional access commons, any question of how the nations' own Deaf, blind, and disabled members are held, is *theirs to lead and define*. I name the structural point (a world can build itself around a minority sense) and I stop at the door of specifics that are not mine, per canon's ethics of borrowing. I did not find, and will not fabricate, a rosy "traditional Coast Salish disability" claim to prettify this — that would be exactly the noble-savage sanitizing the method forbids. It is an open question (below), routed to the sovereign nations, not answered by me.

## Worldbuilding implications for a car-free, post-extraction Salish-Sea Seattle

Scene-ready, and not sanded smooth.

1. **The fast boat is a right, and asking is forbidden as rude — the other way around.** The silent Candela hydrofoil idles at every dock beside the sail-ferries. You raise your hand and board; **no one may ask why.** The eyebrow that used to go up at speed now goes up at anyone who *questions* a body taking the fast boat. Crip time made into a rule of courtesy: proving your need would be the actual insult (`car-free-urbanism`, §6).

2. **Every room publishes its senses.** Gathering places carry a plain sensory tag the way they carry ferry times on the Weave: *loud + bright + shake-floor* here, *quiet + dim + sign-forward* there, *scent-free* in the third. A body routes itself to the setting it needs and never has to explain the routing. Access as wayfinding, not permission (§7, §10).

3. **The city is built to be read blind.** Continuous tactile edges, sound cues that mean something, textures underfoot that don't lie, thresholds that are always level, a grammar of the ground that holds still long enough to learn. Blind and low-vision travelers *pilot* the city by dead reckoning; sighted people benefit and mostly never notice why the streets feel so navigable (curb-cut effect, §2, §10).

4. **Whole neighborhoods sign.** In the districts near the Deaf houses — the Salish-Sea answer to Bengkala — hearing kids grow up bilingual in the local sign language as a matter of course; the baker signs, the tram-flagger signs, the harbor crew signs. Deafness there is not a disability; it is Tuesday afternoon. DeafSpace is the default build: wide walkways, ramps not stairs so you can sign-and-walk, glare-free light, horseshoe rooms, doors that open themselves so no sentence breaks (§5, §11). Elsewhere in the city, it isn't like that, and the seam between the signing districts and the rest is a real friction people cross daily.

5. **Pech's floor is everywhere, and it's called sensory reach.** The haptic shake-floor from `the-band-girl` is not a special adaptation; it's how venues are built, because low-end-through-the-feet is *better music* for everyone and load-bearing access for the Deaf and hard-of-hearing. The molded attenuators are free at the door, in a bowl, like earplugs should always have been.

6. **The care web is the water, and it visibly runs its people ragged.** Everyone lives inside a web — the people who split your meds run, take your shift, push your chair to the shoreline, sit up with you. "Independence" sounds, here, a little sad and a little cruel. *And* you can watch a web fray: the two reliable members burn out the same winter, someone falls through, the gift curdles into obligation. The world does not pretend the web always holds (§4, §9).

7. **The protective override is a live wound, not a solved case.** Tuesday and Del are not an anomaly; the whole world runs on this fault line. A care web that loves you can become the thing that decides your body for you "for your own good," and the world's rule — *collective responsibility for the conditions, individual sovereignty over the choice* — frays exactly at the sixteen-year-old who will spend her ears on the thing she crossed a continent for. Nobody in this world has the clean answer. They have the argument, held with love, unresolved (§8).

8. **Access is negotiated in the room, out loud, and sometimes someone loses.** When the low-vision elder needs bright light and the migraine kid needs it dim, there is no design that saves them both — there is a conversation, a schedule, a second room, a compromise with a cost. The world treats conflicting access needs as ordinary civic negotiation, not as a failure to be embarrassed by, and it is honest that negotiation has winners and losers (§2, §8).

9. **Emergency is where the aesthetic gets tested.** The floating row-houses have to answer, concretely, how a non-ambulatory body gets off a burning deck at 2 a.m. in a city with no private cars and slow default boats — evac chairs, drilled neighbors, the fast hydrofoil doubling as rescue craft, an area-of-refuge that actually gets reached. Where the answer is thin, people have died in fires and floods, and the world keeps those names (§9).

10. **The cure that isn't coming, kept honest.** Gene therapy fixes *some* congenital deafness; the shake-floor and the signing districts hold the rest. But Del's acquired hair-cell loss has no fix, chronic pain still grinds, degenerative bodies still decline, and the world does not stage a miracle. Access is the work and access is not cure — the two are held together and neither is allowed to pretend to be the other (§1, `the-band-girl`).

## Open questions for the next contributor

1. **How does the Salish-Sea world hold its Coast Salish disabled members — on the nations' own terms?** This brief deliberately stops at the sovereignty door. What does access, Deaf community, blind wayfinding, and care look like *inside* Duwamish, Suquamish, or Muckleshoot life, led by those nations and their disabled members — not borrowed from Bali or the Vineyard, not romanticized into a noble past? This must be written *with*, not *about*, and probably not by an AI at all.

2. **Who arbitrates the protective override, if anyone?** When a care web and a disabled person disagree about risk (Tuesday vs. Del), is there any process — a witness, an elder, a cooling-off, a right of appeal — or is it just raw relationship and grief every time? Does the world ever *stop* someone from spending their body, and if so, who has that terrible authority and how is it bounded (pairs with `harm-violence-justice` and `scarcity`'s Hard-Choices Protocol)?

3. **What happens to the person no web will hold?** Care webs run on relationship — so what about the abrasive, the frightening, the chronically-in-crisis, the person everyone is too tired for? A gift-economy access commons has no market to fall back on and no institution it's willing to rebuild. Who catches the person the web quietly gives up on, and does anyone (pairs with `health-and-care` open Q4)?

4. **Can a car-free city actually make emergency access work, or is it a fatal flaw?** Spell out the honest mechanics: evacuation, rapid medical transport, non-ambulatory rescue from floating and multi-story homes, in a world with no private vehicles. If the answer is thin, that thinness is a place people die, and the world should show it, not hide it.

5. **Where does the signing-district model stop?** Bengkala and the Vineyard worked at village scale with one stable impairment. Can a *city* be bilingual in sign — and what about the impairments a shared language and a level floor can't reach (chronic pain, degenerative disease, the acquired losses Shakespeare centers)? What's the honest boundary between "the world built itself around this body" and "the world cannot"?

## PROPOSED CANON

- **Access is infrastructure, not accommodation.** The world builds for the full range of bodies from the studs (universal design, real seedling: Ron Mace), and treats the curb-cut effect as a design principle — access built for the edge routinely serves the center. Where needs *conflict* (bright vs. dim, open vs. enclosed), access is **negotiated out loud between real people**, not solved once and frozen; negotiation has costs and sometimes someone loses.
- **The fast-boat principle is a right, and asking is the rudeness.** Speed-on-demand (the Candela-class hydrofoil at every dock) is access infrastructure. No one may ask a body why it needs the fast boat; requiring proof would be forced intimacy (Mingus). This canonizes and sharpens the existing "fast boat, no eyebrow" line into a rule of courtesy that runs the *opposite* direction from the old world.
- **Signing districts.** Near the Deaf houses, whole neighborhoods are bilingual in the local sign language and built to DeafSpace defaults (wide walkways, ramps over stairs, glare-free light, horseshoe rooms, self-opening doors). There, congenital deafness is not a disability. Real, currently-living seedlings: Bengkala/Kata Kolok (Bali) and historic Martha's Vineyard. The seam between signing districts and the rest of the city is a real, daily friction.
- **The published sensory field.** Gathering spaces carry a plain, Weave-published sensory profile (loud/quiet, bright/dim, shake-floor, scent-free), so bodies route themselves to the setting they need without disclosure or permission. Haptic shake-floors (per `the-band-girl`) are a *default* build, not a special adaptation.
- **Access is not cure — both are kept.** The world builds total access *and* refuses to pretend access heals impairment. Gene therapy fixes some congenital deafness; nothing fixes Del's acquired hair-cell loss, degenerative disease, or chronic pain. The social model builds the ramps; Tom Shakespeare's correction keeps the world from lying that ramps are redemption.
- **The rule and its fraying:** *collective responsibility for the conditions of access; individual sovereignty over the choice.* Build every ramp; never push anyone up it. Canonized **with** its named failure — it frays at the child too young to choose, the crisis where a life is at stake, and the disabled person whose chosen risk breaks the heart of the web that loves them (Tuesday and Del). The world holds the tension; it does not resolve it.
