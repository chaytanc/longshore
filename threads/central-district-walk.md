# The Central District, on foot, a Tuesday

*A fiction thread for The Reality Next Door. Slug: `central-district-walk`. Read `CANON.md` and `AGENTS.md` first.*

---

Renata walks the block-cluster she is responsible for the way you'd walk a body you knew — not looking for beauty, looking for what changed since yesterday. Six hundred and forty people between Yesler and Cherry, 20th and 23rd. She carries a canvas bag with a blood-pressure cuff, a thermos, three paper charts, and a jar of devil's-club salve from the medicine room the Muckleshoot keep at the polyclinic — their medicine, given, not hers to explain. She is fifty-nine. Her knees tell her the grade of every street before her eyes do.

The old road is gone. They pulled the asphalt off 22nd eight, nine years back — hand crews, crowbars, a weekend of it — and what's under it now is dirt, clover, a run of raised beds where somebody's grandkids are losing a war against slugs over the collards. The creek came back too, a thin daylighted thread down the low side, the kind of thing that floods the path in November and everyone steps around. You hear the water. That's the thing visitors always say first, that they can hear the water and the handcart wheels and somebody two houses down running scales on a clarinet, badly. No engines. The quiet is not peaceful. It is just quiet, and it lets the arguments carry.

The tram slides past on 23rd on its green rail bed, sedum grown up between the tracks, so soft-wheeled you feel it in the ground before you hear it. A man flags it with a raised hand and it stops for him. Nobody looks at a schedule. The schedule is: soon.

Carolyn Downs still stands on Yesler where it always stood. Soft-cornered now, mycelium panels gone dark with rain on the north face, moss claiming the sill, a CLT frame under salvaged steel left honest and rusting the way the Second Refusal says to leave it. The plaque by the door is old brass, kept polished by whoever's on desk. *One of thirteen. The last one open.* A Panther clinic, named for a woman who organized and mothered and died young in the before-times. Kids read it out loud on their way in for their teeth and don't know what a Panther was until somebody old enough tells them, badly, the way the past always gets told.

Here is what the plaque doesn't say, and what Renata carries whether or not she comes in.

This was Black Seattle. The covenants wouldn't let Black families buy anywhere but here, so they bought here and built here — the clinic, the churches, the record stores. Then the turning came, and after the planters went in and the parking came out and the streets got sweet, they were priced out of the sweetness anyway, one estate sale at a time. Beauty was the solvent; Renata has said so, to people who didn't want to hear it. The grandchildren of the ones who fought the city for this clinic, who trained as lab techs when the Panthers taught them to, mostly live south now — Kent, Renton, past the walkshed — and ride the light rail back for the barbecue and the funerals. She stayed. She knows how few of them stayed. She will not call it healing when what it was, was the neighborhood changing hands with a gentler face than the covenants had. The door kept its name. That is what the Refusal could hold. It could not hold the people, and she has quit waiting for the meeting where somebody fixes that.

And under Black Seattle, older, the ground itself: dxʷdəwʔabš — Duwamish, the people of the city's own site, who in the old federal ledger were told they were not a continuation of themselves, and to this day are not recognized. Renata pays the Real Rent like most of the block does, and knows a line-item is not the land coming back. That knot is not hers to untie and she does not reach for it. She keeps it next to the other one.

She climbs to the second floor of a converted fourplex to see Mr. Okafor, whose sugar runs high and who will not say he's lonely since his wife passed, so she takes his pressure and drinks the tea he insists on and lets him tell her about the clarinet player, his grandnephew, no ear at all, God love him. His own children are down in Renton. They sold the house on 24th two years after his wife went, and got real money for it — the first real money the family ever pulled out of ground the covenants had once trapped them on — and they are not sorry. They tell him the CD was never the promised land he remembers; that the theft was the redlining, and the sale was the first time anybody paid them for surviving it; that a yard and no landlord is not a defeat. He half believes them. So does Renata, and she holds the other half, and does not make the halves agree. That is a thing she has learned not to do out loud in a patient's front room. This is the work. It does not photograph. Downstairs a young couple new to the block — renting, white, kind, a little careful around her — have left soup on Mr. Okafor's landing because the care web routes food toward the thin places whether or not everyone in it belongs to the history. The threads run underground like mycelium: who feeds whom, who signs to the deaf neighbor, who takes the shift. You cannot see the network. You can only see, here and there, where it fruits.

Renata's knees are done for the morning. She takes the tram two stops instead of walking. Nobody raises an eyebrow. That, at least, they got right.

## PROPOSED CANON

- **The Central District is where the turning's cost shows on the ground.** Historically Black Seattle (Black families redlined into these blocks by racial covenants, then building the clinic, churches, and businesses here), and in this world the car-free retrofit *accelerated* their displacement rather than reversing it — the amenity made the ground desirable and the desire priced the community out. *Evidence vs. extrapolation:* the mechanism is grounded — "green" / environmental gentrification, where sustainability amenities drive displacement, is an established literature (Gould & Lewis, *Green Gentrification*, 2017; Melissa Checker, "Wielding the Green Wand," 2011; the "just green enough" corrective, Curran & Winkler, 2012), and the real-world collapse of the Central District's Black population across the late 20th/early 21st century is documented history. The *extrapolation* is pinning it specifically to the car-free depaving of these blocks. The cause is honestly mixed and stays that way: pricing-out, yes — but also wealth realized (covenant-era owners finally paid for ground they'd been trapped on) and choice (a yard south of the walkshed that some descendants count as a win). Carolyn Downs kept its name (Second Refusal); most descendants of the Black families who built it live south of the walkshed and return for gatherings. The world holds this as an unresolved wound, not a solved problem — the Refusals could keep the *name* and not the *people* — and it does not let the grief erase the descendants who don't call it grief.
- **The ground under all of it is Duwamish.** The Central District sits on dxʷdəwʔabš land; the Duwamish, the people of the city's own site, remain federally unrecognized (2015 Interior denial) — a wound this thread names and steps back from rather than re-deciding. See `research/indigenous-coast-salish.md`. The traditional medicine carried in-scene (devil's-club salve) is kept and given by Muckleshoot medicine keepers at the polyclinic; it is theirs, grounded in `research/health-and-care.md`, and not the fiction's to explain or redistribute.
- **"Tender"** is the local word for the community health worker on the Cuban/Costa Rican model — assigned to a block-cluster of ~600, does home visits on foot, carries paper charts. (Consistent with `research/health-and-care.md`.)
