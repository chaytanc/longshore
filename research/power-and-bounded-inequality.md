# Power & Bounded Inequality — how the Salish keeps concentration below the runaway point

*A research brief for* The Reality Next Door. *Domain: the world's third Refusal — "bound the concentration." Not "no one has more" (a lie that shows), but no runaway: no point where wealth and power implode under their own weight and drag the periphery down. This brief is the mechanics of the cap — why a bound on inequality sometimes holds, why it usually doesn't, what actively enforces it, and how a small powerful center is kept from taking everyone with it when it fails. It sits downstream of `political-economy.md` (Ostrom, Mondragón, Bookchin) and `red-team.md` (Freeman's* Tyranny of Structurelessness*, the kibbutz brain-drain) and tries not to repeat them — its job is the narrow, load-bearing question those briefs circle: **what makes a bound on inequality real instead of decorative?***

**Wildcard lens (rolled, not chosen):** last decimal digit of `red-team.md`'s byte count (29139) → **9 → monastic / liturgical timekeeping & attention discipline.** It turns out to bear directly on the design problem this world sets itself: the Rule of St. Benedict is a ~1,500-year-durable institution built on an authority that is elected, term-bindable, graduated in its discipline, forbidden to enrich itself, and answerable to a written rule it did not author and cannot rewrite. It owns §5 and threads the whole document.

## 1. The thing being bounded: what "runaway" actually is

The canon's wager is that inequality has a *shape*, not just a size — that below some threshold it's a tolerable gradient and above it a positive-feedback loop that eats the society hosting it. Two real bodies of evidence give that threshold teeth.

**Piketty's `r > g`.** When the return on capital (`r`) durably exceeds economic growth (`g`), inherited and accumulated wealth grows faster than the wage economy, so wealth concentrates *mechanically*, without anyone deciding it should — "past wealth recapitalizes itself faster than output increases." Piketty's claim is that absent deliberate counter-pressure (his answer: a progressive wealth tax), capitalism has no internal governor and drifts toward oligarchy. It's contested — a majority of surveyed economists reject the strong form, and the mechanism needs capital and labor to substitute easily — but even the critics concede the *direction*: without a governor, concentration self-amplifies. ([Bruegel, "The Piketty theory controversy"](https://www.bruegel.org/blog-post/piketty-theory-controversy); [Forbes on the r−g debate](https://www.forbes.com/sites/jonhartley/2014/10/17/why-economists-disagree-with-pikettys-r-g-hypothesis-on-wealth-inequality/))

**The empirical ceiling.** The U.S. CEO-to-typical-worker ratio ran ~**281:1 in 2024** on realized pay (290:1 in 2023), up from ~20:1 in 1965 — CEO pay is up 1,085% since 1978 against ~24% for typical workers. That is what an ungoverned ratio does over two generations: it doesn't wobble around a mean, it climbs a ramp. ([EPI, "CEO pay increased in 2024"](https://www.epi.org/blog/ceo-pay-increased-in-2024-and-is-now-281-times-that-of-the-typical-worker-new-epi-landing-page-has-all-the-details/); [EPI CEO Pay report](https://www.epi.org/publication/ceo-pay/))

**Why bound it at all — the harm claim.** Wilkinson and Pickett's *The Spirit Level* (2009) argues that *within* rich countries, higher income inequality tracks with worse outcomes across eleven health and social measures — violence, mental illness, addiction, imprisonment, distrust, low mobility — and that the mechanism is psychosocial (status anxiety), so inequality harms the whole distribution, not only the poor. **Steelmanned opposition, immediately:** this is heavily contested. Critics show the correlations may not be causal; that *poverty*, not inequality, does much of the work (the effect is strong where poverty is high and weak where it's low); and that the relationship weakens or vanishes at the individual level once you control for confounders. The honest reading for the world: inequality-as-such-is-toxic is *plausible and unproven*, so the canon should not lean on it as settled science — the firmer ground is the runaway/implosion argument (§6), not the "equality is simply healthier" one. ([Equality Trust summary](https://equalitytrust.org.uk/the-spirit-level/); [Recalibrating the Spirit Level, *Soc. Sci. & Med.* 2015](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0277953615001094))

## 2. The two steelmen, and they disagree with each other

Rule Zero demands the strongest opponent. Here there are *two*, from opposite flanks, and — usefully — they refute each other, so neither becomes its own echo.

**Steelman A — the egalitarian: any bounded hierarchy is a hierarchy, and hierarchy always re-concentrates.** The canonical statement is Robert **Michels's "iron law of oligarchy"** (*Political Parties*, 1911): *every* organization, however democratic in intent, inevitably develops a ruling minority, because someone must specialize in coordination, and specialists accrete knowledge, network, and control of the apparatus that ordinary members can't match — even the German Social Democratic Party, the most internally democratic mass party of its day, ossified into rule by functionaries. On this view a "6:1 cap" is theater: the ratio you can see is not where power lives; power lives in the informal control of information, agenda, and succession, which no pay ratio touches. Pair this with Jo Freeman (`red-team.md` §1): abolish the visible hierarchy and you don't get equality, you get an *invisible* one you can't vote out. ([Britannica, iron law of oligarchy](https://www.britannica.com/topic/iron-law-of-oligarchy); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_law_of_oligarchy)) *Jacobin* even argues Michels overstated it — the SPD stayed contestable longer than he claimed — which is itself useful: the iron law is a tendency to fight, not a fate to accept. ([Jacobin, "Robert Michels Was a Flawed Theorist"](https://jacobin.com/2024/04/robert-michels-oligarchy-political-parties-spd))

**Steelman B — the realist: the cap can't hold because it drives out the people who could make it work.** This is the empirical killer, and it comes with a body. **Ben & Jerry's** held a 5:1 top-to-bottom pay cap for *sixteen years* — and then abandoned it in 1994 because they could not recruit a CEO to replace Ben Cohen at the capped salary. The ratio slid to 7:1, then to **17:1 by 2000**, at which point Unilever bought the company and stopped disclosing. ([ABC News, "A Sweet Solution…"](https://abcnews.go.com/Business/companies-follow-ben-jerrys-lead-wages/story?id=19920634); [CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/occupy-wall-street-why-ben-jerrys-endorsement-rings-hollow/)) This is the **kibbutz adverse-selection problem** (`red-team.md` §2) in miniature: a hard cap is a standing incentive for your most skilled or mobile members to leave for the uncapped world next door, and the cap erodes from the top under exactly that pressure. Even **Mondragón**, the strongest surviving case, does not hold a single clean number: the famous ~6:1 is a *range*, roughly 3:1 to 9:1 across its ~80 cooperatives, agreed co-op by co-op, and the pressure to loosen it (especially for internationally-mobile managers and non-cooperativized foreign subsidiaries) is continuous and documented. ([Australian Cardijn Institute, "Mondragon limits maximum salaries to 6x"](https://australiancardijninstitute.org/mondragon-limits-maximum-salaries-to-6x/); [Mondragón, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mondragon_Corporation))

**Where they collide — and where the world stands.** Note that A and B can't both win. The egalitarian says the cap is *cosmetic* (real power routes around it); the realist says the cap is *too binding* (it costs you your best people). If the realist is right, the cap is doing real work — enough to hurt — which refutes the egalitarian's "it's theater." If the egalitarian is right, there's nothing binding enough to drive anyone out, which refutes the realist. The world's actual position lives in the narrow seam between them: **a bound is real when it costs something to hold and is actively re-held anyway.** A cap nobody would ever want to breach isn't binding; a cap that holds itself is a fantasy. The interesting society is the one paying, visibly and repeatedly, to keep the ratio down — and arguing about the price. That argument, not the number, is the institution.

## 3. Why "cap the number" is the weakest version — and what the durable cases actually cap

The Ben & Jerry's failure teaches the real lesson: **a pay ratio is a lagging indicator of a governance fact.** Where a bound endures, what's actually bound is not the salary but the *power to set the salary* — and, upstream of that, the power to accrete unaccountable authority at all. The durable mechanisms cap the runaway at its source:

- **Revocability and term limits.** Power that reverts on a schedule can't compound. Canon already has this ("earned, revocable, term-limited"); the evidence is that this, not the wage rule, is the load-bearing beam.
- **Sortition and rotation.** Ancient Athens filled most offices — the Council of 500, the juries, most magistracies — *by lot*, not election, precisely to prevent "the emergence of an identifiable governing class." The Council seat was one year, non-repeatable more than once in a lifetime; every magistrate faced *euthyna*, a mandatory end-of-term audit (especially of money handled) and could be removed by popular vote mid-term. Sortition + short terms + rotation + audit is a machine for denying oligarchy time to form. ([Britannica, sortition](https://www.britannica.com/topic/sortition); [Brewminate, "Sortition… in Ancient Athens"](https://brewminate.com/sortition-selection-by-lot-and-democracy-in-ancient-athens/); [Equality by Lot, "The Principle of Rotation"](https://equalitybylot.com/2019/04/24/the-principle-of-rotation/))
- **Ostracism — the ceiling on individuals.** Once a year the Athenian assembly could vote (6,000 ostraka minimum) to exile, for ten years, *whoever had grown too powerful* — no crime alleged, no property confiscated, citizenship retained on return. It was a pure anti-runaway device: a socially-legitimate way to "decapitate" a concentration of power *before* it became tyranny. It's also a warning — ostracism was gamed by factions and could exile the merely excellent. A bound enforced by collective exile is potent and abusable in the same stroke. ([World History Encyclopedia, "Ostracism"](https://www.worldhistory.org/Ostracism/); [The Conversation, "the art of exiling your enemies"](https://theconversation.com/lessons-from-ancient-athens-the-art-of-exiling-your-enemies-68983))

The through-line: **you don't bound inequality by legislating a number and walking away. You bound it by never letting authority sit still long enough to harden** — and by keeping a legitimate, dangerous, collective power to pull anyone back down.

## 4. The active enforcement problem: bounds don't hold themselves — who does the pulling-down, and by what right?

The realist is right that caps erode by default. So the real question is: *who does the pulling-down, and by what right?* The deepest evidence says egalitarian bounds are never passive — they are the continuous output of an active, sometimes brutal, leveling labor.

**Boehm's reverse-dominance hierarchy.** Anthropologist Christopher Boehm (*Hierarchy in the Forest*, 1999) found that egalitarian forager bands are not un-hierarchical; they are hierarchies *inverted* — the rank-and-file actively, collectively, and vigilantly suppress any would-be "upstart," using a graduated ladder of **public opinion, ridicule, disobedience, ostracism**, and, at the limit, expulsion or killing. Equality is not the absence of a power structure; it is a power structure aimed *upward*, maintained by everyone, all the time. The famous ethnographic instance is the Ju/'hoansi custom of "insulting the meat" — a successful hunter's kill is mocked as skinny and worthless, precisely so that his skill can't become status. ([P2P Foundation, Reverse Dominance Hierarchy](https://wiki.p2pfoundation.net/Reverse_Dominance_Hierarchy); [Boehm, "Egalitarian Behavior and Reverse Dominance Hierarchy," 1993, PDF](https://gwern.net/doc/sociology/intrasexual-aggression/1993-boehm.pdf))

**On this land, the first place to look: standing that is publicly granted can be publicly lost.** This world sits on Coast Salish land, and the leveling logic it should reach for *first* is the one already documented for these nations — not a famous example from the far side of the continent. The dedicated brief `indigenous-coast-salish.md` (§2, and Implication 5) is the source; this section only points to it. Coast Salish society was ranked, not flat — Wayne Suttles's "inverted pear," a large high-class majority over a minority of low-class people and slaves — and rank was not private assertion but *public legitimation*: standing was tied to inherited names, ancestral knowledge, and access to resource sites, and it had to be witnessed and validated in the open to be real. The potlatch was where that happened — redistribution *and* the public validation of status, names, and territorial claims, governance and economy and law in one event — and its corollary is the load-bearing one here: **standing raised by public witness can be lowered by it; it is watched, and it can be lost.** ([Suttles, *Private Knowledge, Morality, and Social Classes among the Coast Salish*](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/229501011_Private_Knowledge_Morality_and_Social_Classes_among_the_Coast_Salish1); [Leland Donald, *Aboriginal Slavery on the Northwest Coast*, UC Press 1997](https://www.jstor.org/stable/jj.5973043), on the rank order these ceremonies sat inside; [Canadian Encyclopedia, "Potlatch"](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/potlatch-ban).) Two disciplines follow and are non-negotiable. First, **this is not a mechanism the brief may lift and install.** The potlatch is living Coast Salish law and ceremony; in this world the Coast Salish nations are sovereign and their own law governs their own people, and reproducing or renaming their ceremony as the fiction's ritual would be exactly the extraction the project opposes. What the settler-descended commons can honestly take is the *design principle*, named as borrowed with the source pointed to — authority is legitimate only while publicly witnessed, and the public that grants it can withdraw it — while the ceremony itself stays with its nations. Second, **the gift's shadow travels with the principle:** among the neighboring Kwakwaka'wakw, rivalry potlatches could destroy coppers and canoes, status by conspicuous burning, and the whole order rested on hereditary slavery (Donald). Leveling-by-standing is a real power with real coercion inside it, and the world keeps that on the page.

**A comparative precedent, cited and left where it belongs — the Haudenosaunee "dehorning."** Far to the east, a *different* confederacy codified revocable rank centuries ago. Among the Haudenosaunee (the Six Nations — Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, Seneca, later Tuscarora), the **Great Law of Peace** vests in the **Clan Mothers** the power to nominate, install, *and depose* the male sachems who sit in council: a leader who behaves improperly gets three warnings, and if he does not correct, the Clan Mother — with her clan's and the other Clan Mothers' consensus — "pulls his horns," removing the antlers that are the emblem of his office and, with them, his authority. The title never belonged to him. This is real evidence that *revocable, publicly-accountable* rank is buildable and has lasted. But it is **evidence, not set-dressing to relocate.** The Haudenosaunee are an Eastern Woodlands people; this is their living constitutional-spiritual law; and grafting it onto a Coast Salish-Sea Seattle as the fiction's leveling ritual would treat "Indigenous" as fungible — the exact pan-Indian flattening `AGENTS.md`'s ethics-of-borrowing and `indigenous-coast-salish.md` §4 (the Paula Gunn Allen critique) forbid. So the brief cites the dehorning and steps back: the world's own recall grows from the Coast Salish standing-can-be-lost logic *above*, on the land it actually stands on, and from the Athenian and Benedictine recall (§3, §5) — not from another nation's ceremony, and it does not take the name. (Honest caveat the sources carry: the popular "the Iroquois inspired the U.S. Constitution" claim is contested and often overstated — cite the mechanism, not the flattering myth.) ([Onondaga Nation, "Clan Mothers"](https://www.onondaganation.org/government/clan-mothers/); [Haudenosaunee Clan Mother, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haudenosaunee_Clan_Mother))

**The uncomfortable synthesis.** All of these say the same hard thing: *a bound on inequality is a coercion.* Someone mocks the hunter; someone lowers the standing they once raised; someone pulls the horns; someone drops the ostrakon. The leveling power that keeps the top down is itself a real power — and therefore itself a candidate for capture, cruelty, and runaway. This is the knot the world must keep on the page: **you cannot have bounded inequality without a levelling authority, and the levelling authority is the next thing that needs bounding.** Turtles, but you can at least make each turtle visible, rotated, and recallable.

## 5. [WILDCARD: monastic timekeeping & attention discipline] The Rule that binds the abbot

Roll 9 lands on a strikingly durable working model of accountable, term-bindable, non-enriching authority: the Benedictine monastery, ~1,500 years old and still running.

**Authority bound by a rule it didn't write.** The abbot is *elected by the monks* and wields real, even "supreme," authority — but the Rule of St. Benedict (c. 530) subordinates him to itself at every turn: he "must do everything in the fear of the Lord and out of reverence for the Rule," "knowing that he will have to give an account" for every ruling; he must summon the whole community to counsel on important matters (RB 3) *and listen to the youngest, "because the Lord often reveals what is better to the younger"*; he may not treat the monastery's goods as his own. The authority is genuine and the constraint is genuine, at once — which is exactly canon's "hierarchy is not tyranny; discipline is sometimes necessary" if and only if it's accountable and revocable. And there is a backstop against a captured election: RB 64 instructs the neighboring abbots and the bishop to intervene and remove an abbot if a community, "in conspiracy with vice," installs a bad one. Bounded authority with an external recall. ([Britannica, Benedictine Rule](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Benedictine-Rule); [Erie Benedictines, "The Election of a Prioress or Abbot"](https://eriebenedictines.org/spirituality/rule-of-benedict.html/event/2023/12/20/1703048400/the-election-of-a-prioress-or-abbot))

**Graduated discipline — Ostrom's fifth principle, thirteen centuries early.** The Rule's correction ladder is explicitly graduated: private admonition first, then public rebuke, then exclusion from the common table, then from communal prayer, then corporal measures, then — last — expulsion, and even then the door is left open to return *three times*. This is Ostrom's "graduated sanctions" as lived monastic practice: discipline that escalates only with persistence and prefers restoration to exile. ([Rule of Benedict overview, Erie Benedictines](https://eriebenedictines.org/spirituality/rule-of-benedict.html))

**Measurement that serves care, not extraction (the First Refusal, illustrated).** The monastery is saturated with measurement — the day is cut into the *horarium* by the bells of the Divine Office, seven-plus times from Vigils to Compline. But notice *what the counting is for*: the hours don't rank monks, farm their attention, or extract output; they **gather** people, pace shared rest and labor, and protect the interior life from being colonized by work. It is the cleanest available model of the canon's distinction: *count the tide and the harvest and the hours, never the soul.* The bell that calls you to table is measurement in the service of care; the feed that counts your reach is measurement in the service of extraction. Same verb, opposite masters. And the anti-ambition norm is structural — you do not campaign to be abbot; humility (RB 7) makes self-promotion the *disqualifying* trait, which is the monastic echo of "no attention-metrics, enforced at the level of ambition itself."

**The dark half, kept honest.** Monasteries also concentrated land and wealth spectacularly (medieval abbeys became the great landlords the Reformation looted); "stability of place" could trap the miserable; obedience curdled into abuse in real houses. A vow-bound hierarchy is not a safe hierarchy — it is a *legible, rule-bound* one, which is better and not the same. The world should steal the mechanism (elected, rule-subordinated, graduated, non-enriching, anti-ambition) and remember the abbey that grew rich.

## 6. How the center is kept from imploding and dragging the periphery down

This is the third Refusal's actual engineering problem, and it has a real literature.

**Tainter: complexity has diminishing returns, and collapse is the correction.** Joseph Tainter (*The Collapse of Complex Societies*, 1988) argues societies solve problems by adding complexity (bureaucracy, specialization, infrastructure), each layer of which has an energy cost; past a point, added complexity yields *declining* marginal returns, until the society is paying more to maintain its structure than that structure returns — and collapse is the system shedding unaffordable complexity. His index case: the debasement of the Roman denarius as the imperial center's upkeep outran its yield. The lesson for a bounded world: **a center that keeps centralizing is not getting safer, it's climbing a diminishing-returns curve toward a cliff.** Bounded inequality is, read through Tainter, a *deliberate cap on complexity's concentration* — refusing to let the core grow so elaborate and so load-bearing that its failure is fatal to everyone. ([Tainter via Schumacher Center, excerpts](https://centerforneweconomics.org/publications/excerpts-from-the-collapse-of-complex-societies/); [Polycrisis resource page](https://polycrisis.org/resource/the-collapse-of-complex-societies/))

**Core-periphery networks: the specific shape of the danger.** Financial-network research on real interbank systems finds a recurring **core-periphery topology** — a small, densely interconnected core of systemically-important nodes and a large, sparsely-connected periphery. Its characteristic failure: the network is fairly robust to random shocks *but acutely vulnerable to a common shock hitting the core*, which then propagates to the periphery through interlinkage, fire sales, and liquidity hoarding. "Too big to fail" is precisely a core node whose implosion drags the periphery down — the exact dynamic canon names. The mitigation the literature points to is *reducing the chance of correlated shocks to core nodes* and not over-connecting the periphery to the core. ([Kley, Klüppelberg & Reinert, "Systemic risk through contagion in a core-periphery… network," 2014, arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1406.6575); [Eboli, "Systemic Risk in Core-Periphery Interbank Networks," SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5008133))

**Holling's panarchy: modularity is what stops a cascade.** Ecological-resilience theory (C.S. Holling, Lance Gunderson) gives the antidote in one word. As a system moves into its "conservation" phase, *connectedness rises and resilience falls* — the tightly-bound, efficient, maximally-connected system is exactly the fragile one, primed for a shock to cascade through it. What prevents local failures from becoming total collapse is **modularity and nested hierarchy** ("panarchy"): loosely-coupled subsystems on different scales, where larger-slower levels hold the memory that lets smaller-faster levels reorganize after a release, and where a failure in one module doesn't instantly propagate to all. This is Ostrom's nested enterprises and Bookchin's confederation, restated as a *survival property*: the periphery survives the core's implosion only if it is not too tightly bound to the core to begin with. ([Resilience Alliance, Adaptive Cycle](https://www.resalliance.org/adaptive-cycle); [Sundstrom & Allen, "The adaptive cycle: more than a metaphor," 2019](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1476945X1830165X))

**The positive case: Mondragón absorbing Fagor.** When the founding co-op, Fagor Electrodomésticos, went bankrupt in 2013 (~€1.1bn debt, ~1,800 members), the federation did *not* let the core's failure cascade — it relocated the majority of displaced members into other cooperatives and protected members' capital ahead of external creditors (`political-economy.md` §3). That is modularity and mutual-insurance working as designed: a big module failed and the periphery did not go down with it. The dark footnote is that ~1,800 people still lost their jobs, and the shock was survived, not painless. Bounded systems don't abolish failure; they *localize* it. ([CSMonitor, Mondragón](https://www.csmonitor.com/Business/2024/0513/income-inequality-capitalism-mondragon-corporation))

**Design rules that fall out of all three:** keep the core *small* (little to seize, little whose failure is fatal); keep the periphery *able to stand alone* (subsidiarity isn't only democratic hygiene, it's a firebreak); *don't over-connect* efficiency-maximizing links that turn local failures global; and hold *slack* — the redundancy an extraction economy would call waste is the resilience a post-extraction one calls insurance.

## 7. The paradox of the ratio: to bound it you must measure it

The First Refusal forbids the metric turned against a person, not measurement — and bounded inequality makes that distinction concrete and slightly paradoxical: **to cap the ratio, you must count the ratio.** You cannot bound what you refuse to measure. So this world counts the top-to-bottom spread in every co-op, and counts the caregiver's load too (`red-team.md` §5: refuse to count the care and it silently crushes the same women and disabled people it always did). The monastic bell (§5) already models the distinction that keeps this legitimate — count to gather and protect, never to rank. The live danger, worth dramatizing, is the one measurement in §5 doesn't reach: the same instrument built to hold the top down can be quietly re-pointed at the person. The census that keeps the top from running away becomes the file that pins the bottom in place. This is the First Refusal's permanent maintenance problem, not a solved thing.

## 8. Disconfirming evidence & failure modes

Every load-bearing claim above has a crack. Named, not hidden.

- **Caps erode by default; the durable ones are ranges under constant pressure.** Ben & Jerry's (5:1 → 17:1 → sold), the kibbutz privatizations, Mondragón's 3:1–9:1 spread with continuous loosening pressure on mobile managers. The honest prior is that an inequality bound *decays unless actively re-held*, and the re-holding is costly and contested forever. There is no set-and-forget ratio.
- **Adverse selection is real and unresolved.** A hard cap is a standing exit incentive for the most skilled/mobile — you keep the surgeon who *wants* to stay and lose the one who'd rather be rich elsewhere. The world's answer can only be partial: de-commodified abundance lowers the *value* of the extra money, and belonging raises the *cost* of leaving — but some will still go, and a world that pretends otherwise is lying (`red-team.md` §2).
- **The iron law is a tendency, not a fate — but it's a *strong* tendency.** Michels/Freeman: specialization + information asymmetry + control of succession re-grow an informal elite under any formal flatness. Rotation and sortition fight this but cost expertise and continuity; the "dull big commons" (water main, cold chain, seawall) is where specialists *must* persist, and therefore exactly where the iron law bites hardest (`political-economy.md` §10).
- **The leveling power is itself a concentration.** Ostracism was gamed by factions and exiled the merely excellent; reverse-dominance leveling can become a tyranny of the mediocre punishing anyone who excels; "pulling the horns" assumes the levellers are themselves accountable. Bounded inequality requires a coercive equalizing authority, and that authority is the next runaway risk. Unbounded regress; the best you get is *visible, rotated, recallable* levellers.
- **The Spirit Level's causal claim is contested.** Don't rest the world on "inequality is simply unhealthy" (poverty may do the work; individual-level effects are weak). Rest it on the sturdier runaway/implosion argument instead.
- **Modularity has a price: it's less efficient.** Slack, redundancy, loose coupling, small cores — all of it costs output an extraction economy would harvest. A neighboring growth-world will always look richer and faster in the short run, and some inside the Salish will resent paying resilience's premium in good years (you only see the firebreak's value in the fire).
- **Bounded ≠ humane.** The Benedictine abbey grew rich and sometimes cruel *within* its rule; a legible, rule-bound hierarchy is better than an unaccountable one and still not safe. Bounding a power does not sanctify it.

## 9. Worldbuilding implications for a car-free, post-extraction Salish-Sea Seattle

Concrete, scene-ready texture, all extrapolated from the cases above.

1. **The ratio is public, argued about, and slightly sore.** Every co-op posts its top-to-bottom spread the way a kitchen posts a cleaning rota — the shipwright's hall runs 4:1, the medicine fabricators 6:1 because they had to keep a chemist who could've left. The live, recurring argument at the seasonal accounts-meeting is *whether this year's spread crept*, and who let it.

2. **"We lost her to the growth coast" is a known, un-melodramatic grief.** Someone brilliant took the uncapped money and left across the water. She isn't a traitor and isn't pitied; her name comes up the way an emigration does — a fact, a small ache, a thing the cap cost. The world says the cost out loud instead of pretending everyone stays.

3. **Roles are drawn by lot as often as chosen, and it's a chore.** The water-table seat, the harm-circle facilitator rota, the accounts-auditor — filled by sortition from those eligible, one term, non-repeating, with a mandatory hand-over audit at the end (Athenian *euthyna*, folk-named something like *"the reckoning"* or *"showing your hands"*). Being drawn is met with a groan, not a thrill. There's no rising star because there's no ladder to rise.

4. **Standing granted in the open can be set down in the open — and everyone dreads the day it is.** The settler-descended commons has a last-resort recall for someone who accreted too much informal power and won't let go: because a role's authority was legitimate only while publicly witnessed, the same assembly can withdraw it — three warnings, then a public *setting-down* of office before witnesses. The design principle is borrowed *as borrowed* from the Coast Salish logic that publicly-granted standing can be publicly lost (§4; `indigenous-coast-salish.md`) — the world takes the principle, points to the source, and does not reproduce or rename any nation's ceremony as its own. Where the recall touches a Coast Salish person on Coast Salish matters, their nation's own law governs and the commons steps back; sovereignty is not the commons's to administer. It works and it frightens people, because a power that can pull anyone down can be turned on the merely inconvenient. Write the case where it's *arguably* misused.

5. **The bells still ring, and they count nothing about you.** Some rhythm structures the shared day — old church bells, a foghorn, a tide-clock on the pier — gathering people to table, to the tide-work, to rest. It is measurement everywhere and it ranks no one. A newcomer from the metric world keeps flinching, waiting for the count to be turned against her, and slowly stops.

6. **The census that guards the top could pin the bottom, and someone watches for that.** There's a body — heavily double-linked, most-rotated, most-watched — that keeps the ratio-numbers and the care-load numbers. Its recurring civic nightmare, spoken aloud: *are we becoming the file?* (§7's paradox, worn as a job nobody wants for long.)

7. **The core is deliberately kept small and boring.** The genuinely centralized things — the vaccine cold-chain, the fifty-year sea defense, the one fusion tie-in — are run lean and un-glamorous, with as little irreplaceable concentration as the physics allows. The folk version of the core-periphery rule: *"don't build anything the whole coast dies without."*

8. **Slack is a virtue with a name, and misers of efficiency are mistrusted.** The redundant grain store, the spare pump, the second crew that trains alongside the first — kept on purpose, defended in lean years against the person who says it's waste. There's a word for the resilience premium (call it *"the firebreak"*), and paying it is a moral act, resented in fat years and blessed in the fire.

9. **A big give-away caps wealth without a rule against wealth.** The potlatch logic (`political-economy.md` §8, sovereignty central) does what no salary rule can: status flows to the one who *moves* wealth through, not the one who holds it, so accumulation self-corrects socially before it needs policing. The hoarder isn't punished; he's just quietly no one. But the give-away has its own shadow — competitive generosity can become its own arms race and its own rank (Mauss), and the world should show a give-away that curdled into one-upmanship.

10. **When the cap is breached, it's a scandal, not a shrug.** The co-op that quietly let its spread run to 12:1 to keep a star gets *called on it* at the reckoning — not jailed, not fined, but named, in front of the witnesses, and made to account. Graduated, Benedictine: first the private word, then the public one, then the harder measures, exit only at the end. The sanction that matters is legibility, not force.

## 10. Open questions for the next contributor

1. **Who bounds the levellers?** §4 and implication #4 leave the world's deepest unresolved knot: bounded inequality needs a coercive equalizing power (ostracism, the setting-down of office, the reckoning), and that power is itself a concentration. Walk *one actual case* where the leveling authority is misused — the excellent person set down by mediocrities, the reckoning weaponized by a faction — all the way through, without letting the world off the hook.

2. **What exactly makes staying worth more than the uncapped money?** The adverse-selection answer can't just be "belonging." What is the *concrete* bundle — de-commodified housing/health/food, a specific kind of standing, work that means something — that outweighs the growth-coast salary for the person who could leave? Cost it out; make the arithmetic felt, the way Piercy did her homework.

3. **How is the "reckoning" (end-of-term audit) run without becoming a metric that ranks people?** Athenian *euthyna* audited the office, not the soul — but the line is thin. Design the ritual so it examines *the role's conduct* and can't slide into a performance score. This is the First Refusal's hardest applied case.

4. **What is the give-away's failure mode here, specifically?** The potlatch's own shadow is competitive, wealth-destroying one-upmanship and the soft rank of the "big man." Write the Salish give-away that went wrong — where generosity became domination — so the mechanism isn't romanticized.

5. **How does the small core actually resist growing?** Tainter says complexity accretes because each layer solves a real problem. What stops the lean cold-chain / sea-defense core from *legitimately* growing more complex and more load-bearing year over year until it's the fat center the world swore off? Name the mechanism that keeps the core small *against* the constant good-faith pressure to make it do more.

- **The bound is a range that is actively re-held, not a fixed number.** Pay/authority spreads in the Salish run role-by-role (roughly 3:1 to ~9:1, Mondragón-style), are *public*, and are re-argued each accounting season. Canon should say the cap is a maintained tension, not a constant — "a cap that holds itself is a fantasy; ours is re-held every year, and people complain about the cost."
- **A last-resort recall exists — a public "setting-down" of office** — applied to anyone, capped-role or informal-power, who accretes unaccountable authority and won't relinquish it: three warnings, then removal before the assembly that granted the standing. Its design principle is borrowed *as borrowed* from the Coast Salish logic that publicly-witnessed standing can be publicly lost (`indigenous-coast-salish.md` §2; the world points to the source and neither reproduces nor renames any nation's ceremony), and reinforced by Athenian and Benedictine recall. Where it touches a Coast Salish person on Coast Salish matters, their nation's own law governs. It is legitimate, dreaded, and abusable, and the world keeps that unease unresolved. *(The Haudenosaunee "dehorning" is cited in §4 as admired comparative evidence only — an Eastern Woodlands nation's living law, not this world's ritual and not its to relocate.)*
- **Sortition + one-term rotation + an end-of-term "reckoning" (audit of the role, not the person)** is the ordinary way authority is prevented from hardening, alongside the co-chairing/recall already in `political-economy.md`.

Efficiency-maximizing that removes slack is mistrusted.
- **Measurement is pointed at roles, ratios, tides, harvests, and care-loads — never at a person's worth**, and there is a watched, rotated body that keeps these numbers and openly fears becoming "the file."
