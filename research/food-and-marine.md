# Research brief: Food systems & marine/coastal ecology for a car-free Salish-Sea Seattle

*A grounded look at how a bioregional, post-extraction Seattle could actually feed itself — from the tideflats up. Every claim below is anchored to a real project, study, or number. Where I've extrapolated for the fiction, I flag it.*

---

## 1. The honest constraint: you cannot feed a city on romance

Start with arithmetic, because utopias die when the calories don't add up.

**Land per person.** Diet is the dominant variable. A plant-forward diet aligned with US dietary guidelines needs roughly **0.13–0.14 hectares (about one-third of an acre) per person per year**; a meat- and dairy-heavy "standard American" diet needs about **1.08 ha (~2.67 acres)** — an eight-fold difference (Finca Tierra synthesis of land-footprint studies). Intensive biointensive methods can grow a nutritionally complete plant-based diet on **~4,000 sq ft (under a tenth of an acre)** per person at intermediate yields (John Jeavons / GROW BIOINTENSIVE tradition).

**Cities can grow a real fraction, not all, of their food.** Cuba's *organopónicos* — born of the 1990s "Special Period" fuel collapse — are the best real-world proof of concept: **over 300,000 urban farms and gardens produce roughly 50% of the island's fresh produce** (note: *fresh produce*, i.e. vegetables — not grain or protein). A modeled Oakland study found that even at *half* of Cuban yields, ~1,200 acres could supply 100 kg of vegetables/year to over 90% of residents (Civil Eats / McClintock).

**Takeaway for the world:** a car-free Seattle almost certainly grows most of its *vegetables, fruit, herbs, eggs, and some protein* inside and just outside the city, while *grain, pulses, and bulk staples* still arrive from the wider bioregion (the Skagit and Snoqualmie valleys, the Palouse) by rail and water. This matches canon's "slow travel, wind and rail and water" — the foodshed is regional, not planetary, and the boring bulk calories move by barge and boxcar, not container ship.

Sources: [Finca Tierra land-per-person](https://fincatierra.com/journal/how-much-land-to-feed-a-person), [Civil Eats urban ag / Oakland & Cuba](https://civileats.com/2019/02/14/how-urban-agriculture-can-improve-food-security/).

## 2. Urban & peri-urban agriculture: Seattle already has the bones

Seattle is not starting from zero — the institutions a post-extraction city would lean on already exist in seed form:

- **P-Patch community gardens.** Since **1973**, Seattle's municipally run P-Patch program has grown to **91 gardens**, food on **14.9 acres** (plus ~18.8 acres of stewarded public land, ~33.7 acres total), serving **3,600+ gardeners**, all under an organic-practice rule. In 2022 alone, gardeners donated **44,403 lbs of organic produce** to food banks and meal programs. It's "the largest municipally managed community gardening program west of New York." This is a working template for food-as-commons: land held publicly, worked collectively, surplus gifted. (Seattle Dept. of Neighborhoods; Seattle Times 50th-anniversary coverage.)

The waitlist detail is worth keeping for realism: demand vastly exceeds plots. In a car-free city where former arterials, parking structures, and rights-of-way convert to growing space, that constraint eases but never fully vanishes — good gardening ground and *sunlight* stay contested.

Sources: [Seattle P-Patch program](https://www.seattle.gov/neighborhoods/p-patch-gardening/about-the-program), [Seattle Times: 50 years of P-Patch](https://www.seattletimes.com/seattle-news/seattles-p-patch-program-celebrates-50-years-of-community-gardening/).

## 3. Restorative ocean farming: the Salish Sea's second field

The single most usable real-world model is **GreenWave's "3D / regenerative ocean farming,"** developed by former trawler fisherman **Bren Smith** off Connecticut and now open-sourced.

- **The structure:** a simple lattice of floating ropes anchored to the seafloor. Kelp and seaweed grow down from surface longlines; mussels grow on socked droppers; scallops and oysters hang in lantern nets and cages; clams and oysters sit in cages on the bottom. Every depth of the water column is a field. **Zero inputs** — no freshwater, no fertilizer, no feed. The crops *eat* excess nitrogen and carbon out of the water.
- **The economics (as fiction-grade numbers):** ~20 acres and a boat, **$20,000–$50,000** startup, yielding roughly **60,000 kg of kelp and 250,000 shellfish per year**. GreenWave has trained **120+ farmers/hatchery techs** across New England, California, Alaska, and **the Pacific Northwest**.
- **The point:** it's food *and* habitat *and* water-cleaning at once. Kelp forests and shellfish reefs are structure that fish shelter in; filter-feeders clarify water; seaweed draws down carbon and can locally buffer acidification.

This is the perfect mechanism for canon's "mussel-rope and floating garden where the parking used to be." A retired ferry pier becomes a farm co-op's anchor; the ropes are hauled by hand or by small electric winch from open rowed dories, because the fast boats are considered rude.

Sources: [Ellen MacArthur Foundation on GreenWave](https://www.ellenmacarthurfoundation.org/circular-examples/regenerative-ocean-farming), [GreenWave — Our Model](https://www.greenwave.org/our-model), [Bioneers on Bren Smith](https://bioneers.org/bren-smiths-open-sourced-3d-ocean-farm-model-can-feed-hungry-planet-ztvz1709/).

## 4. Shellfish: the region's real aquaculture backbone

Washington already **produces more farmed shellfish than any other US state.** The dominant crops are **Pacific oysters, Manila clams, and geoduck**, concentrated in **South Puget Sound and Hood Canal** (nutrient-rich, protected waters).

- **Manila clams** are the volume crop (~47% of regional production by weight, ~40% of value). **Mussels** ~12% of production. **Geoduck** is <1% of production but ~6% of value — a luxury/export good even here.
- **Native Olympia oyster** (*Ostrea lurida*) — the *only* oyster native to Puget Sound — was nearly wiped out; **less than ~5% of historical beds remain.** But restoration works: the **Puget Sound Restoration Fund**, with WDFW and Tribes, has restored **100+ acres since 1999**, and biologists now describe mudflats with "tens of thousands of Olympia oysters spawning naturally" where there were once scattered few (Jamestown S'Klallam Tribe). Olympias are small, coppery, intensely flavored — a slow-food delicacy, not a bulk protein.

Sources: [NOAA West Coast shellfish aquaculture](https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/west-coast/aquaculture/commercial-shellfish-aquaculture-west-coast), [Puget Sound Restoration Fund — Olympia oysters](https://restorationfund.org/programs/olympiaoysters/), [EoPS: Olympia oysters comeback](https://www.eopugetsound.org/magazine/is/olympia-oysters-comeback).

## 5. Kelp: food, forest, and the thing that's disappearing

Kelp is both a crop and a wound.

- **Decline:** Bull kelp (*Nereocystis luetkeana*) — the iconic bullwhip canopy kelp — has declined **80–90% in Puget Sound over the last century.** In South Puget Sound, kelp cover is down **63%** overall, with some sub-basins down **96%**. The Samish Indian Nation documented a **36% loss (305 acres) in the San Juans in a single decade (2006–2016).** Drivers: warming water, excess nutrients and sediment from runoff, shoreline armoring, and food-web shifts increasing grazing.
- **Food use:** Native **sugar kelp** (*Saccharina*) can be eaten fresh, dried into snacks, used as a sweetener/umami base, or dried for storage. Kelp is fast, needs no inputs, and stores well dried — a near-ideal post-cold-chain staple.
- **Restoration is genuinely hard and not yet solved:** the **Puget Sound Kelp Conservation and Recovery Plan (2020)** and experimental "kelp gardening" (seeding rope or gravel from hatchery-reared spores) are underway but honest sources say it "hasn't worked yet" at scale. There's evidence of **local genetic adaptation** in bull kelp populations, meaning you must restore with *locally sourced* stock — a nice detail: the seaweed, like the people, is bioregional.

Sources: [Puget Sound Restoration Fund — Bull Kelp](https://restorationfund.org/programs/bullkelp/), [PSP Making Waves: losing kelp](https://makingwaves.psp.wa.gov/2020/11/09/puget-sound-is-losing-its-kelp-but-theres-a-plan-to-save-it/), [NOAA Kelp Conservation](https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/west-coast/ecosystems/kelp-conservation).

## 6. Salmon, eelgrass & estuaries: the keystone the whole story hangs on

You cannot write a credible restored Salish Sea without reckoning with salmon, because everything is downstream of them — including the orcas.

- **The deficit:** Only **22 of at least 37 historic Chinook populations remain** in Puget Sound; wild Chinook are at roughly **10% of historic abundance** and ESA-listed as threatened. **Southern Resident orcas**, who eat Chinook almost exclusively, hit a 30-year low of **~73–74 whales (July 2024 census)**, down from 98 in 1995. Recovery goals: 86 orcas by 2030, 110 by 2050 — *contingent on more Chinook.* Salmon abundance is the load-bearing variable for the whole ecosystem.
- **What actually works — process-based restoration:**
  - **Elwha River dam removal (2011–2014)** — the largest dam removal in US history. A decade on, Chinook and steelhead are recolonizing reaches blocked for a century; adult Chinook densities in some newly opened middle reaches now *exceed* pre-removal lower-river densities; summer steelhead (once nearly gone) number in the hundreds upstream. The **Lower Elwha Klallam Tribe** reopened a ceremonial/subsistence fishery for the first time since removal. Recovery is real but measured in *decades*, not seasons — a good corrective to instant-utopia thinking.
  - **Estuary restoration** (Nisqually Delta, Skokomish) — reconnecting diked farmland to tidal flow rebuilds the nursery habitat juvenile salmon need to transition to salt water.
  - **Eelgrass** (*Zostera marina*) — WA DNR's Nearshore Habitat Program has transplanted eelgrass since 2012, targeting river-delta mouths. Eelgrass meadows are salmon nurseries, sediment stabilizers, and carbon sinks; young Chinook shelter and feed there on their way to sea.

**Fiction implication:** a restored Sound is *recovering, not recovered.* The grief in canon's "before-times" stories is literally true for salmon — old people remember rivers black with fish. A ceremonial first-catch reopening is exactly the kind of hard-won, communal, non-market event this world would ritualize.

Sources: [NOAA Elwha restoration](https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/feature-story/elwha-river-restoration-case-study-adaptive-management-salmon-recovery), [Columbia Climate School on Elwha & the Klallam](https://news.climate.columbia.edu/2024/03/22/dam-removal-spurs-the-return-of-salmon-and-a-local-tribes-hopes-of-sustainable-fishing/), [WA DNR seagrass restoration](https://dnr.wa.gov/aquatics/aquatic-science/nearshore-habitat-program/seagrass-restoration), [Puget Sound Vital Signs: orcas](https://vitalsigns.pugetsoundinfo.wa.gov/VitalSignIndicator/Detail/32).

## 7. Indigenous food systems: the 3,500-year-old technology that already worked here

The most important thing a fiction writer can internalize: **the Salish Sea was a highly productive, actively cultivated foodscape long before it was a "wild" one to be "restored."** These are not metaphors; they are engineering.

- **Clam gardens ("sea gardens").** Coast Salish and other Northwest peoples built **rock-walled terraces at the low-tide line**, beginning **~3,500 years ago**, from Alaska through BC into Washington. The wall traps sediment and flattens the beach slope, expanding ideal clam habitat. Tending included clearing, adding gravel and crushed **shell hash** (which buffers larval clams against acidity and gives settlement cues), predator exclusion, and selective harvest. **Measured result:** transplanted littleneck clams grew **nearly 2× faster** in clam gardens than on unwalled beaches, and small clams survived better. This is a real, replicable aquaculture technology — and its disruption by 19th–20th-century commercial fisheries is documented as an *ecological legacy of dispossession* (Royal Society, 2025).
- **Reef-net fishing** (*sxwo'le*). Invented by Coast Salish peoples **at least 1,800 years ago**: paired canoes/floats suspend a net between them with funnel-like leads, sited at specific inherited locations near river mouths (San Juans, Saanich) to intercept summer sockeye runs. It's **selective** (you can release non-target fish) and scaled to produce winter surplus *and* trade goods. Lummi Island reef-netters still practice it; the salmon is prized precisely because the method is gentle.
- **Camas** (*Camassia*). A calorie-rich bulb, **pit-cooked** (slow-roasted underground for a day+, converting inulin to digestible sugars) into something like a sweet, garlicky chestnut. Dried, it **stored through winter** alongside dried/smoked salmon, clams, and berries, and was a major **trade commodity — "as valuable as salmon"** to Swinomish ancestors. Camas prairies were cultivated: burned, weeded, and harvested to favor the bulbs.
- **First Foods framing.** Many Columbia Plateau and Coast Salish traditions order foods by ceremonial priority — water, salmon, game, roots (camas, wapato), berries — and this ordering *is* a management ethic. Restoration in the fiction should be led by, and credited to, Tribes and First Nations, who are the actual present-day leaders of clam-garden revival, kelp monitoring, and salmon recovery (Swinomish, Lummi, Samish, Jamestown S'Klallam, Lower Elwha Klallam).

Sources: [EoPS: Ancient clam gardens](https://www.eopugetsound.org/articles/ancient-clam-gardens-northwest-coast-north-america), [Ecology & Society: sea gardens & littleneck clams](https://ecologyandsociety.org/vol28/iss2/art8/), [Royal Society: ecological legacy of dispossession](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/rstb/article/380/1930/20240277/234959/Ecological-legacy-of-Indigenous-dispossession), [HistoryLink: Coast Salish reef-net fishery](https://www.historylink.org/file/21237), [NW Treaty Tribes: camas](https://nwtreatytribes.org/this-coast-salish-food-staple-is-blooming-again/).

## 8. The climate wound that doesn't heal on ideology alone: ocean acidification

Crucial for keeping the world *credible rather than utopian*: a post-extraction economy stops *adding* CO₂, but the Salish Sea is already committed to decades of acidification, and its cold, CO₂-rich upwelled water makes it a global hotspot.

- The **2007–2008 Pacific NW oyster hatchery crash** is the canonical case: **Whiskey Creek Hatchery (Netarts Bay, OR)** lost **70–80% of larval oysters** as corrosive, low-aragonite water hit the tanks during the critical first-24-hour shell-building window. Regional industry losses were estimated near **$110 million.**
- **Adaptation that actually happened:** hatcheries now **buffer intake water** (sodium bicarbonate — literally dosing the sea with antacid) and monitor carbonate chemistry in real time. This spawned better ocean-monitoring networks.

**Fiction implication:** even in the restored world, hatcheries and clam gardens still *tend* chemistry — crushed-shell hash on the beaches (the old Indigenous trick, now understood as local buffering), kelp beds grown deliberately upstream of shellfish plots to raise pH locally. The sea is not "healed"; it is *nursed*, daily, by people who read water like weather.

Sources: [NRDC: The Great Oyster Crash](https://www.nrdc.org/stories/great-oyster-crash), [OSU Newsroom: acidification & larval oysters](https://news.oregonstate.edu/news/hatchery-osu-scientists-link-ocean-acidification-larval-oyster-failure).

## 9. Distribution: food as commons, not commodity

Canon's Third Refusal — "abundance is arranged, not sold" — has a real intellectual lineage.

- **Jose Luis Vivero-Pol** (UCLouvain; lead editor, *Routledge Handbook of Food as a Commons*, 2019) argues food should be governed as a **commons, not a commodity**, via **"tricentric" governance** — a hybrid of **markets + public provision + collective/community action** operating locally. Note the nuance the fiction should honor: he does *not* abolish markets; he demotes them to one instrument among three. That maps exactly onto canon's "money exists but is thin and local and boring," with the "thick layer" running on gift and mutual aid.
- Practical non-market and hybrid forms that already exist and would dominate here: **community gardens with gleaned surplus** (P-Patch's 44,000 lbs to food banks), **gleaning networks**, **community-supported fisheries / agriculture (CSF/CSA)** where you subscribe to a share of the catch/harvest rather than buy by the pound, **cooperative buying clubs**, and **free fridges / mutual-aid larders.**

**Design guardrail (Three Refusals check):** any distribution system must have **no ranking, no scoring, no metric of who "deserves" food.** So: no points, no reputation dashboards for gift-givers. The right texture is the ferry scene — "handed tea you didn't order by a person who had extra." Surplus flows by proximity and abundance, not audit.

Sources: [Shareable: Why food should be a commons](https://www.shareable.net/why-food-should-be-a-commons-not-a-commodity/), [Resilience: Vivero-Pol interview](https://www.resilience.org/stories/2022-01-05/jose-luis-vivero-pol-treat-food-as-a-commons-not-a-commodity/).

## 10. Seeds: the commons that has to be defended actively

If food is a commons, seed is its taproot — and seed is exactly where enclosure (patents) tried hardest to reassert itself.

- **The Open Source Seed Initiative (OSSI, founded 2012)** answered seed patenting with a viral-license move borrowed from open-source software. The **OSSI Pledge**: *"You have the freedom to use these OSSI-Pledged seeds in any way you choose. In return, you pledge not to restrict others' use of these seeds or their derivatives by patents or other means, and to include this Pledge with any transfer."* Four freedoms: **save/grow, share/trade/sell, study/publish, and adapt/breed.**
- **Seed libraries** (borrow seed, grow it out, return more) and regional seed-saver networks keep locally adapted varieties alive and in circulation — the plant analogue of the Weave: knowledge and living material passed hand to hand, un-owned.

**Fiction implication:** a Salish-Sea seed commons would be *bioregionally adapted* — overwintering kales, quick brassicas for the short cool summer, dry-farming tomatoes bred for cloud, potato landraces. Someone in the city is the keeper of the tomato line that ripens in a Seattle August. The bull-kelp "local adaptation" finding (§5) means the *marine* seed stock (kelp spores, oyster larvae) is bioregional too — you can't import your restoration.

Sources: [Open Source Seed Initiative — the Pledge](https://osseeds.org/), [OSSI on seed sovereignty](https://osseeds.org/control-of-seed-and-seed-sovereignty/).

## 11. Seasonality & preservation without an industrial cold chain

A car-free, low-energy city can still have refrigeration (canon has advanced tech), but bulk preservation would lean on methods that need little or no continuous power — and this shapes the *calendar* of eating.

- **Salt & smoke:** salt-cured and **cold-smoked salmon** (smoke held below ~30°C for hours/days) is shelf-stable for months — the Coast Salish winter staple, industrialized only recently.
- **Lacto-fermentation:** salt + vegetables + a cool cellar. Kimchi, sauerkraut, brined beans, pickled kelp. Improves with age; needs only a root cellar, basement, or cold pantry.
- **Drying:** salmon, kelp, berries (as cakes/pemmican-style), camas, apples, beans, peas.
- **Root cellaring:** the classic underground high-humidity/low-temp room holds roots, apples, squash, cabbages, and canned goods through winter without power.
- **Canning / bottling:** shelf-stable, needs only a burst of heat at harvest.

**Fiction implication:** the eating year has a *shape*. Late summer is the frantic gorgeous glut — fish-camp smoke, jars stacking up, kelp drying on racks. Deep winter is fermented, smoked, dried, cellared: intense, salty, umami-rich food, with the first fresh overwintered greens in late winter treated as a small holiday. This is the opposite of the flat, seasonless, everything-always grocery — and the seasonality is a *pleasure*, not a deprivation, the same way slow travel is.

Sources: [Modern Homesteading: preservation without power](https://modernhomesteading.ca/food-preservation/), [Cultures for Health: lacto-fermentation storage](https://culturesforhealth.com/blogs/learn/blog-blog-post-lacto-fermenting-garden-long-term-storage).

## Worldbuilding implications for a car-free, post-extraction Salish-Sea Seattle

Concrete, droppable-into-a-scene details:

1. **The parking-lot farms are real and stacked.** Where canon says "floating garden where the parking used to be," picture a GreenWave-style rope lattice off an old ferry slip: sugar kelp and bull kelp down from surface lines, mussels socked on droppers, scallops in stacked lantern nets, oyster and clam cages on the bottom — one 20-acre patch feeding a co-op, hauled by hand from rowed dories because the foiling boats are considered rude.

2. **The eating year has a shape and a smell.** Late-summer fish camps thick with alder smoke and racks of drying kelp and salmon; deep-winter meals of smoked fish, brined kelp pickle, sauerkraut and cellared squash; the first overwintered kale in February treated as a minor festival. Nobody eats a fresh tomato in January and nobody wants to.

3. **Crushed shell on the beach is technology, not litter.** Clam-garden tenders spread shell hash at the low-tide line — it buffers the water against acidity and cues larval clams to settle. It's the 3,500-year-old Coast Salish trick, now also understood as local pH management against a still-acidifying sea. The sea is *nursed daily*, not "healed."

4. **Restoration is credited, led, and ongoing — not finished.** Tribes and First Nations (Lummi reef-netters, Swinomish and Samish clam-garden and camas revival, Lower Elwha Klallam salmon) are the acknowledged leaders. A river's *first reopened ceremonial salmon catch in a generation* is exactly the communal, non-market, grief-and-joy event this world ritualizes.

5. **Salmon are back but not abundant, and everyone knows the number.** Old people remember rivers "black with fish." The orca count (once 98, once down to the 70s) is common knowledge, tracked like weather — because the whales eat the Chinook and the Chinook are the whole Sound's report card.

6. **Camas cakes and kelp are pantry staples, geoduck is a delicacy.** Pit-roasted camas (sweet, faintly garlicky), dried kelp for umami and thickening, smoked salmon — everyday. Geoduck and coppery little Olympia oysters are slow-food treats, the "boring thin money" splurge, not bulk protein.

7. **The seed keeper is a named neighborhood role.** Someone holds the tomato line bred to ripen in a cloudy Seattle August; the OSSI-style pledge travels with every packet ("use freely; never lock it up; pass the pledge on"). Marine "seed" — locally adapted kelp spores and oyster larvae from a community hatchery — is bioregional too; you literally cannot import your restoration.

8. **Surplus moves by proximity, not audit.** Free larders and gifted shares, CSA/CSF subscriptions to "a share of the catch," gleaning crews — and pointedly *no* leaderboard of who gave most. The ferry gift of unordered tea is the whole distribution ethic in miniature.

9. **Bulk staples still arrive from the bioregion, slowly.** Grain, dry beans, and flour come from the Skagit, Snoqualmie, and the Palouse by rail and sailing barge — the city grows its produce and much of its protein but is honestly interdependent with its valleys. The foodshed is regional, visible, and named.

10. **P-Patch is the ancestor institution.** The waitlist tension survives in softened form: sunlight and good ground stay contested even when former arterials and rooftops are all growing space. Community-garden governance (plots, shared tools, organic rules, gifted surplus) is the civic muscle memory the whole food commons is built on.

## Open questions for the next contributor

1. **Protein honestly:** vegetables and shellfish are covered, but where do *fats and concentrated protein* come from at scale — farmed mussels/kelp, wild but strictly limited salmon, pastured poultry/pigs on urban-edge food-waste, legumes and nuts from the valleys? What does a normal winter *dinner* actually contain?
2. **Who decides the salmon harvest?** Wild salmon are recovering but scarce. What's the governance of a limited common catch — Tribal treaty rights (which are legally paramount and must be honored, not erased), plus what commons mechanism for everyone else — without inventing a central authority the canon forbids?
3. **Hatcheries and the "no counterfeit" ethic:** the world relies on shellfish/kelp/salmon hatcheries. How does high biotech aquaculture (assisted gene flow for heat-tolerant kelp? selective breeding?) sit with the world's suspicion of replacing the natural — where's the line between *tending* and *counterfeiting*?
4. **The working waterfront as a place:** someone should walk one tideflat co-op or one fish camp at dawn in full sensory detail — the sounds, the winches, the barter, the kid learning to sock mussels. (Pairs naturally with the band girl's city.)
5. **Failure and scarcity:** what happens in a bad year — a marine heatwave "blob," a red tide / PSP toxin closure shutting the shellfish beds, a failed kelp restoration? The world is "restoration, ongoing and imperfect"; show the imperfect.
