# The tender at the door

Mara knocks the way a Tender knocks—two soft, then waits, then one more so you know it's not the wind. The door to Suki's float-row apartment is warped where the tide gets under it and the wood has learned the shape of the wet. Third time this winter Mara's stood here. The tide chart is in her head the way the whole neighborhood is in her head—who needs stairs, who needs the fast boat, who hasn't answered in three days.

Suki opens. Sixty-something, angular, wrapped in the same wool throw Mara saw last week, same mug of something that has stopped steaming. The apartment is cold. Not freezing—this city does not let people freeze—but cold the way a body gets cold when heating a room costs kilowatt-hours from the winter commons ration and the ration is already thin and you are alone and no one will know if you pull it tighter around yourself instead of the stove.

"I'm fine," Suki says, before Mara says anything.

Mara nods. She has heard *I'm fine* in every variation a person can say it. This one means: *I know you came because the algedonic pinged you and I do not want to be the person the channel had to route toward.*

"May I come in?"

Suki steps back. The throw stays around her shoulders.

Mara sets the bag down—tea that wants drinking, a jar of the ferment cellar's kraut (lacto-fermentation, vitamin C through the hungry gap, no cold chain), a wool second layer someone passed to the polyclinic because their mother had died and the mother wouldn't want it in a drawer. This is the Tender's work. Not diagnosis. Not the prescription pad. The **proactive, on-foot care that comes before the crisis**—what Cuba's *consultorios* and Costa Rica's EBAIS teams proved can resolve 80% of what happens to a body if you catch it while it's still small `[DEPLOYED · health-and-care §1]`. Mara is responsible for roughly 600 people in this cluster of float-rows and the hillside above them, whether they come to the neighborhood clinic or not. She knows Suki's stairs (there aren't any—this is a dock-access float-row, which is why Suki could afford it and also why she is alone here when her knee is bad and the February wet makes it worse). She knows Suki's cat died last spring and there is still a bowl on the sill. She knows Suki's daughter is on the other side of the Sound and visits when the ferry runs and did not visit last week because the wind was up.

"Your knee?" Mara asks.

"It's fine."

It is not fine. Suki is moving the way a person moves when standing costs and sitting costs and the meds that would take the edge off are the ones you save for when it gets *really* bad, because you don't want to be the one who asks for more.

This is where the rest-ledger lives, in practice. Not an accounting trick. A **recognition that care labor is work, that the work has a cost in a person's body, and that the community—not the individual patient—carries the debt for it** `[CANON · rest-ledger]`. Every home visit Mara makes, every night the crisis-line pulls her out of bed, every hour spent with someone who is not in crisis but is *approaching* it—those hours go on the ledger, and the neighborhood assembly, every season, looks at the number and **owes Mara rest-days in return**. It is a permitted measurement under the First Refusal because it counts **the debt the commons carries**, never Mara's productivity or a patient's worth. It is a form of honor. And it runs, honestly, in arrears—Mara works, the community notices late, and rest comes after, if it comes.

But here is the thing the ledger cannot solve and the world does not pretend it can: **Mara still has to decide, in this cold room, whether Suki is someone who needs the algedonic channel to ping louder—needs the commons to route care her direction ahead of the slow assembly—or whether she is someone Mara can leave for another three days because there are five other people in worse shape and Mara's body is also finite**. The decision is not a metric. It is not a score of Suki's life's worth. It is a **judgment about a person that Mara is making in real time with incomplete information and her own exhaustion as one of the weights on the scale**.

This is the knot Rule Zero will not let anyone tie off. The First Refusal says no metric shall be turned against a person. The rest-ledger obeys that—it measures Mara's labor, not Suki's value. But in the room, *now*, Mara is still the one deciding whether to pull the commons's limited attention toward Suki or not, and the decision necessarily involves the question: **How bad off is Suki, really? And compared to whom?** That is an adjudication. It is exactly the kind of judgment a world built to refuse ranking is most afraid of. And someone has to make it anyway, or people die alone in cold rooms while the commons is off deliberating.

Mara makes it. She sits. She pours tea from the thermos she brought. She asks about the knee, about the daughter, about whether Suki has been to the second-hand shelf at the polyclinic lately because there's a good winter coat someone passed along. She is **reading Suki's access needs off her without making Suki strip down and prove them**—the thing Mia Mingus called **access intimacy**, "the feeling when someone else *gets* your access needs" without you having to beg for them `[DEPLOYED · health-and-care §4, disability-and-access §3]`. Mara already knows Suki will not ask. So Mara is building the ask into the visit, into the tea, into the coat, into the fact that she is here at all.

"The commons can turn your heat up," Mara says. "Your ration isn't fixed. If you're cold, we pull from the reserve."

"Someone else needs it more."

"That's not yours to decide."

"Then whose is it?"

**Mara's.** That is the unspoken answer. That is the weight the world places on the Tender—the weight the rest-ledger tries to honor and cannot actually remove. In a utopia that refused rank, Mara would not have to triage. In the world that actually exists—a world with finite kilowatt-hours in February, finite hours in Mara's body, finite everything except need—someone has to be the one who routes the commons's limited care, and that someone is Mara, and the routing is judgment, and judgment about bodies is exactly what the First Refusal exists to prevent. The world holds both. It does not reconcile them.

Mara marks Suki in her head as *next visit tomorrow, and flag the heat co-op tonight*. Not because Suki is the worst case. Because she is the case Mara can still catch before it becomes the worst case. That is triage. It is the mercy and the violence both.

"You're not fine," Mara says, gently. "And that's fine."

Suki looks at her. The throw is still around her shoulders. The tea is cooling in her hands. She does not argue.

When Mara leaves, she stops at the heat co-op's message board—an actual corkboard, under a dry overhang, because the Weave is slow and heat is not—and pins a note with Suki's float-row address and the word *cold*. The co-op will route a temp-bump from the commons reserve that night. No form. No proof of need. Just: a Tender said so, and Tenders are trusted, and trust is the thing this whole system runs on, and the trust is **weight** Mara carries every day and will carry until the rest-days the ledger promises actually arrive, which they have not yet this season, because the commons is short and someone has to hold the gap.

The rest-ledger counts the debt. It does not pay it early. And it cannot make the judgment Mara just made anything other than what it was: a decision, by one person with a name and a tired back, about whether another person with a name and cold hands was worth pulling the scarce warmth toward. The world built itself to refuse that math. The world runs on it anyway. Mara walks home in the wet.
