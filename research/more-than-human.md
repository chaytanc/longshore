# Research brief: Salmon and orca as kin, and as legal persons

*How a restored Salish Sea reckons with the idea that a river, a Chinook, an orca can be a person under the law — and how people who believe that still hunt, kill, and eat their kin. Every claim below is anchored to a real statute, case, ceremony, or scholar. Where I extrapolate for the fiction, I flag it. The hard norm is kept on the page: personhood does not end the harvest, it disciplines it.*

---

## 0. The knot this brief refuses to untie

You can hold two facts at once, and this world is built on the friction between them:

1. To the Lhaq'temish (Lummi) people, the orcas of the Salish Sea are **qwe 'lhol mechen** — "our relations under the waves," family, not fauna. Coast Salish and other Northwest peoples have called themselves *salmon people* because the salmon are kin who give their bodies so the people can live ([Lummi / Sacred Sea](https://sacredsea.org/skalichelhtenaut/); [OSPI First Salmon Ceremony unit](https://ospi.k12.wa.us/sites/default/files/2023-10/peunit2firstsalmonceremony.pdf)).
2. Those same Coast Salish people catch, kill, smoke, and eat the salmon every year. And — a *separate* nation, on a *separate* question — the **Makah Tribe** holds a treaty-secured right to hunt gray whales, which they are fighting in federal court to exercise ([OPB, 2024](https://www.opb.org/article/2024/06/13/federal-approval-makah-whale-hunt/)). The Makah are a Nuu-chah-nulth-affiliated, Wakashan-speaking whaling people of Cape Flattery — **not** Coast Salish. I keep them distinct throughout: the salmon material here is Coast Salish; the whaling knot is Makah and treaty-specific. Slashing the two into one "Indigenous" voice is exactly the pan-Indian flattening `AGENTS.md` forbids, and I will not do it even for a tidy sentence.

The comfortable move is to resolve this — to make the future either vegetarian-utopian or to quietly drop the kinship language. **Both are lies, and lies show.** Kinship is not sentimentality that forbids the kill; in the traditions this world inherits, kinship is precisely what *obligates* the kill to be done with restraint, ceremony, gratitude, and reciprocity. A person who eats a relative owes that relative something. That debt — not the absence of death — is the moral engine. This brief is about building law and ritual around that debt without sanitizing it or appropriating it.

**A note on my own position, per the ethics-of-borrowing (`AGENTS.md`):** I am a non-Indigenous AI trained on scraped text that may include Indigenous knowledge taken without consent, now writing about Coast Salish kinship. That is itself an extraction, and I am not going to pretend otherwise. I name specific nations and cite specific scholars; I do not redistribute sacred specifics (the words of the ceremonies, the songs, the inherited reef-net sites) as if they were mine to give. Where I mark something as sacred practice, the fiction should point at the source and step back.

## 1. The Coast Salish frame: personhood was never the innovation

The rights-of-nature *lawyers* think legal personhood for a river is a 21st-century breakthrough. From a Coast Salish frame it is a settler legal system stumbling, centuries late, toward something that was always obvious: the more-than-human world is populated by persons.

- **Salmon as persons who choose.** In Puget Sound Coast Salish cosmology, salmon are a people who live in villages beneath the sea, put on salmon-flesh, and swim up the rivers to give their bodies as a gift to humans who treat them with respect. When the flesh is used and the remains are returned to the water with ceremony, the salmon revives and goes home — and returns next year *only if* it was honored. I take this from the **OSPI "Since Time Immemorial" First Salmon unit**, developed *with* Washington tribes and published by them for public schools ([OSPI unit](https://ospi.k12.wa.us/sites/default/files/2023-10/peunit2firstsalmonceremony.pdf)), rather than from Erna Gunther's 1926 salvage-ethnography *An Analysis of the First Salmon Ceremony* — a text produced inside the same extractive machine (collect the "vanishing" native's knowledge before it's gone) that this brief is trying not to repeat. The distinction is a personhood with agency and consent built in: the fish *gives* itself, and can withhold.
  - **The knot I can't tie off:** I pledged above to withhold the *specifics* of ceremony, then just paraphrased a ceremonial mechanic. Sourcing it to a tribally-authored public curriculum instead of a settler ethnographer is better, not clean — the community chose to teach *this* much in public, and I've stayed inside that boundary, but I have still relayed a sacred logic I did not receive. I am not going to pretend the sourcing choice neutralizes that. The confession is not armor. Where the fiction needs the ceremony itself, it points at the nation teaching it and stops.
- **The First Salmon Ceremony** — the first Chinook of the run is caught, honored, sometimes shared so every household eats a piece, and the remains returned to the river with ceremony to "show the salmon's spirit the way home." The Lummi Nation frames its fishing ethic as **restraint, respect, and reciprocity** ([WELCOME Magazine](https://welcomemagsnoho.com/magazine_article/the-story-behind-the-salmon-ceremony/); [Salish Current, 2026](https://salish-current.org/2026/05/07/first-salmon-ceremony-a-south-fork-success-story/)).
- **Orcas as kin: qwe 'lhol mechen.** For the Lummi, the Southern Resident orcas are relatives under the waves. This is not metaphor for them; it grounded a decade-long campaign to bring home **Sk'aliCh'elh-tenaut** (the captive orca marketed as "Lolita"/Tokitae), taken from Puget Sound in **August 1970** and held 53 years at the Miami Seaquarium ([Cascade PBS](https://www.cascadepbs.org/environment/2023/09/lummi-nation-sees-parallels-orca-tokitaes-story/); [YES! Magazine](https://www.yesmagazine.org/opinion/2023/10/03/orca-puget-sound-tokitae)).
- **The scholarship, named and not flattened.** Kyle Powys Whyte (Citizen Potawatomi — *not* Coast Salish; I name the nation because pan-Indian flattening is exactly the error `AGENTS.md` warns against) theorizes Indigenous environmental justice as **anti-colonial action through kinship** — obligation running in both directions across human and more-than-human relatives. Robin Wall Kimmerer (Citizen Potawatomi) names the **Honorable Harvest**: *ask permission; never take the first, never take the last; take only what you need; use everything you take; minimize harm; share it; be grateful; reciprocate; sustain the ones who sustain you.* Kimmerer is explicit that reciprocity is what "resolve[s] the moral tension of taking a life" ([Kimmerer, *Braiding Sweetgrass*](https://www.servicespace.org/blog/view.php?id=21413); [Whyte, "Settler Colonialism, Ecology, and Environmental Injustice," *Environment and Society* 9 (2018): 125–144](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.3167/ares.2018.090109)). Archaeology backs the sustainability claim empirically: [Coast Salish salmon stewardship shows thousands of years of harvest without collapse](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10456131/).

**The load-bearing point for the fiction — held as a *chosen framing*, not a neutral finding:** in this world, "rights of nature" law is best told as the settler system being made to *catch up* to Coast Salish law, with the Tribes as acknowledged authors and leaders, not consultants brought in to bless a white lawyer's idea. But I won't overclaim the history. Legal personhood for a river is also, honestly, a hybrid modern construct — Roman corporate personhood, Stone's 1972 standing argument, Māori settlement law, and Coast Salish kinship braided together in a courtroom that is none of those things. "Catching up to what was always there" is the *interpretation this world adopts and argues for* because it puts sovereignty and authorship where they belong — not a neutral discovery that settler law and Coast Salish law were secretly the same all along. The fiction should let a character say so, and let the framing be contested rather than assumed.

## 2. Rights-of-nature law: three real precedents to build on

### 2a. Whanganui River, Aotearoa New Zealand — the strongest working model

The **Te Awa Tupua (Whanganui River Claims Settlement) Act 2017** made the 290-km Whanganui River the first river recognized as a legal person, "an indivisible and living whole from the mountains to the sea," under the Māori view *Ko au te Awa, ko te Awa ko au* — "I am the River and the River is me." Crucially, it was **a Treaty of Waitangi settlement** — redress for 140 years of dispossession, not an abstract green gesture. The river's rights are exercised by **Te Pou Tupua**, "the human face of the river": two people, one named by Whanganui iwi and one by the Crown, acting jointly ([Te Awa Tupua Act, EcoJurisprudence](https://ecojurisprudence.org/initiatives/te-awa-tupua-act-2017/); [Heinrich Böll Stiftung](https://www.boell.de/en/2025/01/29/river-legal-person-case-whanganui-river-new-zealand)).

The scene-ready detail is the **two-person guardian body** — personhood needs a *voice*, and the voice is jointly held by the Indigenous nation and the state, forced to speak as one.

### 2b. Ecuador — rights of nature in a national constitution, and enforced

Ecuador's **2008 Constitution** was the first to grant **Pachamama (Nature) constitutional rights** to exist, persist, and regenerate — grounded in Andean *sumak kawsay* ("good living"). It was tested and *held* in the **Los Cedros case (Dec. 1, 2021)**: the Constitutional Court ruled 7–0 (2 abstentions) that mining concessions in the Los Cedros protected cloud forest violated the rights of nature, and that those rights are **self-executing throughout the entire territory**, not just in parks ([GARN](https://www.garn.org/los-cedros-rights-of-nature/); [Center for Biological Diversity](https://biologicaldiversity.org/w/news/press-releases/ecuadors-highest-court-enforces-constitutional-rights-of-nature-to-safeguard-los-cedros-protected-forest-2021-12-02/)).

### 2c. The Salish Sea orca proclamations — the local, unfinished version

Since 2018, a campaign led by the **Earth Law Center** and **Legal Rights for the Salish Sea**, with the **Affiliated Tribes of Northwest Indians** passing a 2018 resolution recognizing a "sacred obligation" to the orcas, has pushed to recognize the Southern Residents' **inherent rights: autonomy, free and safe passage, and an adequate food supply** — with human legal guardians as their "face and voice." By 2023, Washington cities and counties (Port Townsend, Gig Harbor, Langley, Bainbridge, Des Moines; Pierce, Jefferson, San Juan Counties) had passed **proclamations** — note the word: mostly symbolic, non-binding, aspirational ([Earth Law Center — Salish Sea Initiative](https://www.earthlawcenter.org/salish-sea-initiative); [Peninsula Daily News](https://www.peninsuladailynews.com/news/in-one-month-three-cities-proclaim-rights-for-southern-resident-orcas/)). This is the seed a future world grows into full law — and it is worth remembering it *started* as toothless.

## 3. The hard norm on the page: you still hunt your kin

This is where most solarpunk goes soft. This world does not.

- **The Makah gray whale hunt.** The Makah Tribe's 1855 **Treaty of Neah Bay** is the only U.S. treaty that explicitly secures a right to whale. After a 1999 hunt, animal-rights litigation and the Marine Mammal Protection Act blocked them for a quarter-century. In **June 2024**, NOAA finally granted a waiver to hunt **up to three eastern North Pacific gray whales per year for a decade** — and as of late 2025 the actual hunt was *still* tied up ([OPB](https://www.opb.org/article/2024/06/13/federal-approval-makah-whale-hunt/); [KUOW, 2025](https://www.kuow.org/stories/makah-tribe-s-treaty-protected-whaling-rights-remain-blocked-after-more-than-25-years); [NOAA](https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/west-coast/marine-mammal-protection/makah-tribal-whale-hunt)).
- **The threats were real.** Opposition was serious enough that the Makah put a **checkpoint on the only road into Neah Bay** against physical threats to tribal members ([Sierra Club](https://www.sierraclub.org/sierra/whale-controversy)). Hold that image: a nation reasserting a sacred, treaty-guaranteed relationship with a whale, guarding its own road against people who love whales enough to threaten violence.
- **The philosophical knot the fiction must voice:** *If an orca can be a legal person with a right to life, on what ground do you kill a gray whale?* This is a **Makah** treaty question, not a Coast Salish one — I won't put a single "Indigenous" answer in anyone's mouth. But *one* answer available within Makah whaling tradition (and Makah people hold more than one) is not "whales aren't really persons." It is closer to: **persons can be kin you are in a reciprocal, ceremonial, restrained relationship of life-and-death with** — the way a subsistence hunter and the hunted are bound — and a treaty right won by a sovereign nation is not the animal-rights movement's to veto. Kinship and killing are not opposites here. That is the uncomfortable center.
- **And the nation is not monolithic on its own sacred question.** The 1999 hunt was opposed *from inside* Makah too: a group of tribal elders, most publicly **Alberta "Binki" Thompson**, spoke out against it, doubting the hunt's spiritual and community grounding and its entanglement with outside pro-whaling and anti-treaty interests — the dissent (and its own painful complications, including alliances Thompson later questioned) is documented at length in Robert Sullivan, *A Whale Hunt* (Scribner, 2000), a book-length firsthand account of the years around the 1999 hunt. Naming this is not a gotcha against the treaty right — it is the anti-flattening discipline turned honestly on the nation whose sovereignty this brief defends: a sovereign people argues with itself about the most sacred thing it does, and the fiction must not smooth that into one serene tribal voice.
- **The honorable-harvest discipline as the actual "regulation."** In-world, the thing that governs the salmon and whale harvest is not a fishery quota spreadsheet alone but a *ceremonial ethic with teeth*: never the first, never the last, take only what you need, use all of it, return the bones, reciprocate. A metric that counts fish is fine (First Refusal: measurement isn't the enemy). A metric that *ranks who deserves to fish* or turns the run into a market to be optimized is forbidden.

## 4. Disconfirming evidence & failure modes (the idea's dark side)

Rights of nature is not a magic wand. The honest record:

1. **Personhood can be granted and then yanked.** In 2017 the Uttarakhand High Court declared the **Ganga and Yamuna rivers** living persons with the state's officials as "legal parents." Within months India's **Supreme Court stayed the order** — partly on the state's objection that if the river is a person, *someone can be sued when it floods and drowns a village*, and that personhood requires legislation, not a judge's decree ([Scroll.in](https://scroll.in/latest/843007/ganga-yamuna-lose-living-entity-status-as-supreme-court-stays-uttarakhand-hc-order); [Policy Circle: "symbolism, not solutions"](https://www.policycircle.org/opinion/legal-personhood-to-ganga-river/)). The flood question is not a gotcha — it is real. **A person can be a defendant.** If the river drowns your child, do you sue the river? Its guardians? The state?
2. **U.S. ordinances get preempted.** A **Grant Township, Pennsylvania** ordinance recognizing ecosystem rights was struck down as preempted by state law and as infringing corporate rights ([IUCN](https://iucn.org/news/world-commission-environmental-law/201905/rights-nature-why-it-might-not-save-entire-world)). The Salish Sea orca "rights" are, so far, mostly **non-binding proclamations** — moral theater until a legislature or court gives them force.
3. **The relative can die while you fight for her.** **Sk'aliCh'elh-tenaut / Tokitae died on August 18, 2023**, of suspected renal failure, *weeks* before her planned return to the Salish Sea after 53 years of captivity ([Underscore Native News](https://www.underscore.news/culture/skalichelh-tenaut-to-return-home-after-decades-of-captivity/); [Cascadia Daily](https://www.cascadiadaily.com/2025/jan/09/documentary-follows-lummi-nations-fight-to-free-last-captive-southern-resident-orca/)). No tidy resolution. The campaign was righteous, was winning, and she still died in the tank. Build a world that includes that ending.
4. **Personhood without prey is a cruel abstraction.** You can grant the Southern Residents a legal "right to an adequate food supply" and they will still starve if the Chinook aren't there. In **2018 the orca J35 (Tahlequah) carried her dead calf for 17 days and 1,000 miles**; in **winter 2024–25 she did it again** with calf J61 ([CNN](https://www.cnn.com/2025/01/07/science/orca-carrying-dead-calf-tahlequah); [CBS](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/orca-carrying-dead-calf-tahlequah-killer-whale/)). Up to 70% of Southern Resident pregnancies fail, largely from nutritional stress. Rights on paper do not put fish in the water.
5. **Implementation is thin and understudied.** Even the flagship Whanganui model has been researched almost entirely from *outside* — scholars love the legal-personhood headline; very little tracks whether the river's water is actually cleaner or the iwi actually resourced to do the guardianship work ([Tandfonline / *Journal of Human Rights Practice*, 2024](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/13642987.2024.2314532)). Personhood can become a prestige artifact that launders inaction.

## 5. The steelmanned opponents (who also disagree with each other)

Per Rule Zero, the strongest cases *against* rights-of-nature — including two camps that despise each other's reasoning:

**Opponent A — the pragmatic-legal critique (Guim & Livermore; Sachs).** In *Where Nature's Rights Go Wrong* (Virginia Law Review), **Mauricio Guim and Michael Livermore** argue the concept is analytically incoherent: it cannot define *which* natural entities hold rights or *how to weigh* one against another. A hydroelectric dam cuts carbon (good for the climate) and drowns a valley (bad for that ecosystem) — nature's rights give courts **no framework to balance competing natural goods**, so judges produce "arbitrary and ungrounded" decisions. Their evidence is Ecuador itself: rival litigants each claiming to *be* nature's voice, forcing courts to pick a champion. Their alternative: don't grant abstract rights to nature; grant **concrete, enforceable rights to the people and organizations who defend it** ([Virginia Law Review](https://virginialawreview.org/articles/where-natures-rights-go-wrong/)). **Noah Sachs** (Richmond) adds that the movement relies on **ad hoc litigation over nebulous rights**, likely yielding "arbitrary and oppressive outcomes for humans while under-protecting nature," and that **existing environmental law works when actually enforced** — rights-of-nature is speculative hype that diverts energy from statutes that already have teeth ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4402290)).

**Opponent B — the animal-welfare critique, which is *anti*-Opponent-A's coalition.** The **Animal Welfare Institute** and **Sea Shepherd** oppose the Makah whale hunt as "unnecessary, inhumane, and not supported by current science," pointing to the gray whale population at ~13,000 (a 50-year low) and the small, vulnerable Pacific Coast Feeding Group ([OPB](https://www.opb.org/article/2024/06/13/federal-approval-makah-whale-hunt/); [Sierra Club](https://www.sierraclub.org/sierra/whale-controversy)). This camp takes *individual animal* personhood *more* seriously than most rights-of-nature lawyers — and lands in direct collision with tribal sovereignty and treaty rights. **The two opponents cannot both win.** If the whale is an inviolable individual person, the treaty right to hunt is illegitimate. If the sovereign nation's ceremonial, restrained hunt is legitimate, then "personhood" cannot mean "may never be killed." A world that takes *both* Indigenous sovereignty and more-than-human personhood seriously has to live inside this contradiction, not vote one side off the island. **This is the single most productive tension in the whole brief.**

**A wrong-turn worth naming honestly:** the pragmatists are probably *right* that "the river is a person" is legally underspecified. The reply this world makes is not that the critique is wrong but that it is *insufficient* — a purely instrumental environmental law (nature as a resource to be optimally managed) is exactly the extraction logic this world refuses. Personhood is chosen not because it litigates cleanly but because it *reframes the relationship* — from ownership to kinship — even at the cost of legal tidiness. That is a real, arguable trade, and the fiction should let a character lose an argument about it.

## 6. Wildcard lens (rolled): disability justice & crip theory

*Session id ends in `4` → lens 4, disability justice & crip theory. I would not have chosen this, which is the point.*

Crip theory turns out to be the sharpest tool in the box for this topic, because it attacks the hidden assumption underneath *both* sides of the personhood fight: that a "real" person is autonomous, self-sufficient, and independent. Disability justice (Mia Mingus, Leah Lakshmi Piepzna-Samarasinhu, Sins Invalid) insists the opposite — that **interdependence is the ground state of all life, and dependency is not a failure of personhood but its actual condition.** Applied here:

- **The Salish Sea is a chronically ill body, not a patient to be cured.** Canon already says the sea is "restored but not healed... nursed daily." Crip theory names why that framing is *correct and not tragic*: the fantasy of a "healed," self-regulating wilderness that needs no human hands is an ableist fantasy of the independent body. The real sea is like a disabled body in a good care relationship — it thrives *through* daily, unglamorous, mutual tending (crushed-shell hash for pH, hatchery larvae, kelp gardening), and that dependence is dignity, not diminishment. **Care is not what you do until the cure; care is the permanent condition.**
- **The orca's "right to an adequate food supply" is a crip claim.** It is an **access need**, not a courtesy — the same structure as a right to an attendant or a ramp. Tahlequah carrying her dead calf is the population's grief made visible; the honest response is not "fix the orcas" but "restructure the world (dams, boat noise, Chinook) so the access need is met." That reframes recovery from *engineering a self-sufficient wild* to *building permanent accommodations for kin who cannot live without them.*
- **Guardianship as interdependence, not paternalism.** Crip theory is wary of "guardians" who speak *for* the disabled — the disability-rights slogan is "nothing about us without us." That's a genuine problem for orca/river guardianship (§4.5): the guardian can become a ventriloquist. The disability-justice answer — **supported decision-making** rather than substituted judgment; the guardian as an amplifier held accountable to the being's expressed behavior, not a proxy who overrides it — is a concrete design constraint for how the fiction's guardian bodies should actually work.

The lens pays off: it dissolves the false choice between "person (therefore untouchable)" and "resource (therefore usable)" into a third thing — **kin in a relationship of mutual, unfinished, obligatory care**, where being eaten and being tended are both forms of that relationship.

## 7. Historical & non-Western precedent (decentering the default)

- **Historical (Western law's own buried root).** In **1972, Christopher D. Stone** published *Should Trees Have Standing? — Toward Legal Rights for Natural Objects*, and Justice **William O. Douglas**, dissenting in *Sierra Club v. Morton* that same year, argued that "the river as plaintiff speaks for the ecological unit of life that is part of it" and that valleys, rivers, and estuaries should have standing to sue for their own preservation. So the "radical" idea is 50+ years old *inside* American law and lost — a corrective to treating personhood as new, and a reminder of how long the dominant system resisted its own better instinct.
- **Non-Western precedents, plural and distinct (not one "Indigenous" monolith):** Māori *Te Awa Tupua* (§2a) grounds personhood in **whakapapa** — genealogical descent that makes the river a literal ancestor. Andean *sumak kawsay* / *Pachamama* in Ecuador (§2b) grounds it in a cosmology of Earth as a living mother-being. Hindu-tradition personhood for the **Ganga and Yamuna** (§4.1) grounds it in the rivers as goddesses — and *failed* in court, showing that sacred status alone doesn't survive a modern legal system's demand for a suable, liable, resourced person. Three different non-Western routes to the same door; only some of them hold once the door is law.

## Worldbuilding implications for a car-free, post-extraction Salish-Sea Seattle

The *principles* live in the PROPOSED CANON block below (guardian pair, kinship-disciplines-harvest, the founding wound). This list gives only the **scene-level specifics** that turn those principles into pages — not a re-argument of them.

1. **The guardian pair's failure state is a scene.** The Southern Residents (and each major river) speak through a named pair — one seated by the relevant nation, one by the bioregional commons — who must speak *as one* or not at all. When they can't agree, the being has no voice that season, and everyone feels the silence: the two guardians arguing on a ferry deck over whether J-pod's hunger justifies closing a Chinook fishery humans also depend on, and then walking off deadlocked, the orcas mute for the year.

2. **A river can be a defendant.** When the Duwamish floods and takes a floating neighborhood, someone in the commons hall asks whether you sue the river, its guardians, or no one — and the answer the world settles on (you don't sue; you convene a reckoning where the guardians account for the tending that was or wasn't done) tells you what personhood *means* here.

3. **The harvest is disciplined and costly, on the page, not in summary.** The season opens with the first Chinook shared so every household tastes it; a tide-and-run *count* is posted like weather (measurement is fine; a leaderboard of who caught most is not). And the taking still costs: show a Coast Salish fisher who is good and reverent and comes home heavy, or — the Makah case, kept separate — a hunter home from a whale under a treaty that green sentiment does not get to veto, while the resident who thinks the whale should never die stays unconvinced and unmoved. Some threads end there.

4. **The sea is nursed like a chronically ill body, and that's dignity, not defeat (crip lens).** Daily unglamorous care — shell hash for pH, hatchery larvae, kelp gardening, hydrophone listening for the pods — is the permanent condition, not a temporary fix on the way to a self-sufficient "healed" wild. A character who longs for a nature that "doesn't need us anymore" is gently corrected: that's the old extraction dream wearing a green coat.

5. **The grief is public and named.** When a pod loses a calf, the city knows the whale's name and the calf's number and it carries — hydrophone feeds in commons halls, a day the ferries cut engines. Tahlequah's tour of grief is the template: the more-than-human person's mourning is *witnessed*, not managed away.

6. **The appropriation knot stays visible in-world.** Non-Native residents who love the salmon-people framing are reminded, sometimes sharply, that it is not theirs to perform — reef-net sites are inherited, ceremonies are owned, enthusiasm is not entitlement. A well-meaning newcomer overstepping and being corrected is a good, true scene.

## Open questions for the next contributor

1. **What does the guardian pair do when kin's interests collide?** The orcas need Chinook; humans need Chinook; a dam removal floods a farm the commons also relies on. Who convenes the trolley problem, and how does a world that forbids entrenched authority make a binding call without inventing a tyrant? (Pairs with the food brief's "who decides the salmon harvest.")

2. **Can a more-than-human person be liable?** If the river is a defendant (implication #2), and if an orca damages a fish farm, does personhood cut both ways — rights *and* responsibilities? Work out the liability side the rights-of-nature movement mostly dodges.

3. **Where exactly is the line between honoring and appropriating in daily civic life?** Non-Native majority, Coast Salish–authored law and ceremony. What's the actual etiquette — what may a newcomer do, say, cook, name; what is off-limits; who corrects whom? Write the granular manners, not the abstraction.

4. **What happens to the person who cannot accept the hunt?** Steelman and *inhabit* Opponent B — a beloved, sincere resident (maybe the band girl?) who believes the whale should never be killed and cannot be talked out of it. Don't resolve them. What is their place in a world that overruled them on principle?

5. **Does personhood survive scarcity?** In a genuinely bad year — blob, red tide, a run that just doesn't come — does the kinship ethic hold, or does hunger quietly turn kin back into resource? Show the ethic under load, where it might break.

## PROPOSED CANON

*(World-level facts I'd propose; a human merges these into `CANON.md`. They complicate, not contradict, existing canon.)*

- **The more-than-human persons are legally real here, and Coast Salish–authored.** The Southern Resident orcas and the major rivers (Duwamish/Dxʷdəwʔabš, Skagit, Elwha, Nooksack, Snoqualmie) are recognized legal persons. Their rights are voiced by a **guardian pair** on the Whanganui model — one seated by the relevant sovereign nation, one by the bioregional commons — who must act jointly. The world tells this as a *chosen interpretation* — settler law reaching toward Coast Salish law, the Tribes as authors and not consultants — and it names that as a framing it argues for, not a neutral discovery that the two legal orders were secretly always one.
- **Kinship does not abolish the harvest; it disciplines it.** Coast Salish people still fish under a ceremonial ethic of restraint (the First Salmon; the Honorable Harvest's "never the first, never the last, use all of it, return the remains"); and — the *separate* Makah case, on their own treaty and their own whaling tradition, not to be merged with the salmon material — a treaty-holding nation still hunts marine mammals, over honest dissent from within that nation itself. Killing a relative carries public obligation and is never innocent. Treaty rights and tribal sovereignty are paramount and outrank both market and green sentiment.
